How to Remember the Periodic Table

By Vyasa Academy | 30 Aug 2026 | 2 min read

On this page

  • Draw the blank skeleton first
  • Fill it from memory in layers
  • Link each element to what it does
  • Let the trends do the heavy lifting
  • Review by recall on a schedule
  • Why this method wins in inorganic chapters
  • The close

There is a difference between recognising the periodic table and remembering it. Recognition happens when the table sits open in front of you; remembering happens when you can rebuild it on a blank page. The second skill, not the first, is what makes inorganic chemistry feel easy, so this article is about building it.

Draw the blank skeleton first

Get the layout into muscle memory before filling in elements. Draw the seven periods as rows and label the group columns 1 to 18. Note where the blocks live: the s-block on the left, the p-block on the right, the d-block in the middle. A skeleton you can draw without thinking is the frame every element will later hang on.

Fill it from memory in layers

  1. Layer one: the first twenty elements and their positions.
  2. Layer two: the well-known groups beside them, alkali metals, alkaline earth metals, halogens and noble gases.
  3. Layer three: the rows that appear most often in your syllabus, like the elements studied in practical chapters.
  4. Layer four: the transition row you actually need for class work, filled by repeated recall not by staring.

Each layer is a writing session from memory, then a check against the real table. Do not read the table into your eyes and hope; write it from your head and compare.

Link each element to what it does

Elements stick when they are tied to behaviour, not to a number. For each element you list, add one fact you can picture: what it looks like, what it reacts with, or where it is used. The fact is the hook and the position is the shelf; the hook pulls the shelf into your memory first.

Let the trends do the heavy lifting

Rather than memorising thirty facts about thirty elements, remember the four trends: atomic radius, electronegativity, ionisation energy and metallic character. These predict behaviour across a period and down a group. A question on an element you have never revised becomes answerable from the trend, which is the real power of remembering the map.

Review by recall on a schedule

  • Day one: write the skeleton plus the first layer from memory, twice.
  • Day three: add layers two and three, again from memory.
  • Day seven: the whole table cold, then tick off what you nailed.
  • Each following week: one full cold write in five minutes.

Five minutes a week keeps the table permanently yours. This is the retention half of the story; the familiar memorisation tricks are covered in our companion article.

Why this method wins in inorganic chapters

Every inorganic chapter hands you elements you already placed on your map. Location, group behaviour and trends combine to answer most questions without separate memorisation. Our inorganic mastery guide builds directly on this skill.

The close

Remembering the periodic table means rebuilding it from memory: draw the skeleton, fill it in layers, link elements to behaviour, and review by recall. Do that and the table stops being a chart on the wall and becomes the working map of your chemistry.
