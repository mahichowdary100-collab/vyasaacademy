Understanding Concepts vs Memorising Mathematics

By Venu Chennupati | 25 Aug 2026 | 2 min read

Students often ask whether maths should be memorised or understood, as if they were rivals. They are not rivals. Memory is the tape that holds a method in place; understanding is the map that tells you when to use it. The trouble starts only when the tape has no map.

What memorisation is actually good at

  • Multiplication tables, squares, cubes and standard angle values: learned by heart, used instantly.
  • Standard formulas whose derivation is time-consuming: memorised with a hook, verified once.
  • Exam formats like steps for constructing a geometry figure: rehearsed exactly.
  • Common definitions in exact words: learned deliberately.

Where memorisation alone fails

The failing moment is the unfamiliar question. A memorised student has methods but no way to choose between them, so a small variation in a word problem creates a blank page. The examiner who wrote the paper assumes the student understands, and understanding is the only thing a fresh question can test.

Three ways to build real understanding

Explain the why once

When you meet a new formula, spend two minutes on why it works. Area of a triangle is half the rectangle because two triangles tile the rectangle. Negative times negative is positive can be reasoned from opposites on a number line. One paragraph of why removes pages of later confusion.

Test with change, not repetition

If you can solve a problem, change something: turn a number into a variable, swap the known and unknown, reverse the question. A student who can ride those changes has understanding; a student who cannot has only the template.

Teach it back

Explain the method aloud to a study partner, a sibling, or a wall. Understanding appears at the moment you can teach it, and gaps appear at the exact sentence where you stall. The stall is the learning point.

A healthy mix that works

  1. Memorise the small building blocks: tables, squares, standard values.
  2. Understand the relationships: why formulas exist and when they apply.
  3. Memorise the exam routine around the understanding: the layout, the two-step checking, the time budget.
  4. Refresh recall on a schedule so the map never fades.

How to tell which one you have

Open the book, close it, and attempt a problem that was not assigned as homework. If you breeze through because you know what to do next, you understand. If you stall until you peek at the answer, you were memorising. That honest test will tell you exactly which chapter needs the why-step next.

The concluding advice

Use memory as the tape and understanding as the map, and the two work together. Commit the tables and routines to memory, invest two minutes in every why, and test yourself with changed problems. That balance is what turns mathematics from a list into a skill.
