How to Prepare for Case-Based Maths Questions in Class 9

By Venu Chennupati | 8 Oct 2026 | 5 min read

On this page

  • The four-step case method
  • Worked case: the community park
  • Practising case passages at home
  • Building your own cases

A case-based mathematics question is a short passage about one real situation, followed by several sub-questions all created from the same data. The skill it tests is not a new topic – it is the ability to read a description, pull out the numbers, and connect each sub-question to the right Class 9 concept without being told which one to use.

What makes a case question different

Ordinary problems state "a rectangle is 100 m by 60 m; find the area." A case wraps the same shape inside a story about a park, seeds the passage with extra numbers that may or may not be needed, and then asks three or four questions in sequence. Because the questions share one passage, the work done for the first question often feeds the next.

The four-step case method

  1. Scan, do not read yet. Half a minute to notice what the passage is about and whether a table or figure is present.
  2. Extract a data table. Convert the passage into a short list of quantities: what is given, what is asked, which numbers are noise. Write it before solving anything.
  3. Map each sub-question to a concept. Name the chapter and tool for each of (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv). This is the same concept-naming step used for competency-based questions.
  4. Solve and verify. Solve each in order, then check the units and sanity of every answer.

Worked case: the community park

The case. A neighbourhood park is rectangular, 80 m long and 60 m wide. In its centre is a circular fountain with a radius of 7 m. The rest of the park is grass. The park committee wants to know: (i) the area of the whole park, (ii) the area of the fountain, (iii) the grass area available for walking, (iv) the cost of laying new grass at ₹15 per square metre.

Step 2 – data table.

QuantityValue
Park length80 m
Park width60 m
Fountain radius7 m
Grass cost₹15 per m²

Step 3 – concept map.

  • (i) Area of a rectangle – Chapter 6 (measuring space). Formula: length × width.
  • (ii) Area of a circle – the same chapter. Formula: πr².
  • (iii) Difference of the two areas.
  • (iv) Rate × area – multiplication.

Step 4 – solve.

(i) Park area = 80 × 60 = 4800 m².

(ii) Fountain area = π × 7² = (22/7) × 49 = 154 m².

(iii) Grass area = 4800 − 154 = 4646 m².

(iv) Grass cost = 15 × 4646 = ₹69,690.

Check. Units: all areas in square metres, cost in rupees. A fountain of 154 m² inside a 4800 m² park leaves almost all the space as grass – sensible. These formulas are laid out in the Chapter 6 notes, which also covers the circle (Chapter 5 material) needed for the fountain.

Practising case passages at home

Case practice improves fastest with a repeatable routine:

  1. Rewrite ordinary problems as cases. Take a chapter's standard problem and wrap it in two sentences of story with one decoy number. Then answer the rewritten version a day later.
  2. Practise the data table. For every case attempted, write only the table in the first two minutes. Table-writing turns a confusing passage into a clean problem.
  3. Name the concept before calculating. Keep a running label column – "rectangle area", "circle area", "subtraction", "rate". This trains the exact skill the format tests.
  4. Schedule them in the routine. One case per week in Friday's mixed set works with the weekly plan, and cases belong inside the timed sets of the pre-test arc.

The Class 9 Mathematics study material groups related problems by chapter, which gives plenty of raw material to convert into self-made cases.

Building your own cases

Writing a case is the deepest form of preparation. Choose one situation from daily life – a plot boundary, a festival stall, a school garden – then write three questions that use different concepts over the same data. A student who has built cases rarely fears them. To make one well:

  • Use one setting and two or three shapes or rules.
  • Include one extra number the questions do not need.
  • Order the questions so each answer feeds the next, as the park case does.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a case question take in Class 9?

Ten to twelve minutes across all sub-questions, including the reading. The data table should be complete within the first two minutes.

Should students answer the sub-questions in order?

Usually yes – case passages are built so early answers feed later ones. Only skip ahead when a sub-question clearly needs a different concept.

What if the passage is long?

Extract faster, not read harder. Long passages usually mean more data is given and the concept per sub-question is kept easier. The table exposes that quickly.

Are case questions related to competency questions?

Yes. Case passages are the extended form of the same idea: concepts tested inside real situations. The competency guide covers the short single-situation form; this guide covers the passage form.

Conclusion

Case-based Class 9 Maths questions stop being intimidating once the passage is turned into a data table and each sub-question is named with a concept. The community park case shows the whole routine on one worked example. Practise one case a week, build your own passages, and keep the habit alive through the pre-test arc.
