The colour-changing world of chemistry — become a chemistry detective!

A hidden message appears on a science fair sheet. Which substance was used to write it, and which indicator revealed it? Test the evidence as you read through NCERT Curiosity Chapter 2.

Before you start

A solution is a mixture in which a substance is dissolved. You will use a control sample, record colours before and after testing, classify solutions, and explain how neutralisation works. Allow about 50–65 minutes including part of the virtual lab.

Work safely

Use labelled, teacher-approved samples. Wear splash goggles for practical work. Never taste, touch or directly smell a test sample. Keep droppers separate, never mix cleaning products, and follow your teacher’s disposal instructions.

2.1 Nature — our science laboratory

2.1.1 Litmus as an indicator

An acid–base indicator shows a recognisable change across acidic and basic conditions. Litmus is a natural indicator obtained from lichens. Red and blue litmus papers let us classify the same solution using two tests on fresh strips. Synthetic indicators are prepared in laboratories.

Lemon or amla juice, tamarind water, vinegarRedRedAcidic
Soap, baking soda, lime water, washing powder solutionBlueBlueBasic
Sugar or pure table salt solution, pure waterRedBlueNeutral in these tests
Tap waterTest to find outTest to find outOften near neutral. Source and treatment can affect it.

The colours are expected outcomes for suitable samples. A very dilute sample, coloured sample or old strip can make a change hard to see. Compare with a known acid, known base and a neutral control when results are unclear. Clear liquids can belong to any group. Some dissolved salts make acidic or basic solutions.

2.1.2 Red rose as an indicator

NCERT uses red garden rose petals. Their coloured compounds can change appearance in different acid–base conditions. With the textbook setup an acidic sample produces a red shade, a basic sample a green shade, and a neutral control leaves the starting shade largely unchanged. The starting shade in the book’s paper illustration is pink.

Make a batch for the whole class with teacher-prepared warm water. Reserve an untreated sample of extract and compare the same volumes under the same light. Flower variety, extract concentration, lighting and sample colour can change the exact shades. The chapter suggests trying beetroot, purple cabbage and jamun too. Test their behaviour with known controls before drawing conclusions.

2.1.3 Turmeric and olfactory indicators

Turmeric paper starts yellow. A suitable basic sample makes it red or reddish-brown. Acidic and neutral samples usually leave it yellow. Curcumin is a coloured component of turmeric that helps produce the change. Soap can change the colour of a turmeric stain when the soap solution is basic.

The turmeric greeting card uses this behaviour. Basic ink such as an approved baking soda solution makes writing visible on dry turmeric-coated paper. At the science fair, soap solution as ink and turmeric as the revealing liquid is one possible combination.

Olfactory indicators

Some indicators change the strength of their odour in different conditions. In Activity 2.6, strips exposed to chopped onion are compared after adding tamarind water or baking soda solution. The onion scent is expected to remain more noticeable with tamarind and to weaken with the basic sample. The virtual activity represents these observations with text. A teacher supervises any demonstration with known materials and provides an alternative for students sensitive to odours.

How to write an exam answer

  1. State the observation using the indicator’s name and colour change.
  2. Give the rule connecting that change to acid, base or neutral behaviour.
  3. State the conclusion and any limit of the test.

2.2 What happens when acids mix with bases?

In a typical acid–base neutralisation the acid and base react to form salt and water, and heat is released. Here, salt means a class of substances. Common table salt is one example.

Word equation

Acid + base → salt + water. Heat is released.

Activity 2.7 starts with diluted lemon juice and blue litmus solution. The mixture is red while acidic. A teacher adds lime water gradually and swirls. Eventually a blue colour shows that the mixture has gone into the basic range. A suitable additional amount of lemon juice can move it back towards acidic conditions. An indicator marks a colour-transition range. Blue is evidence of excess base here. The demonstration does not measure the exact neutral point.

The virtual equal-capacity model uses steps of equal neutralising ability. Real drops need not have equal volume or concentration. Some acid–base reactions involving carbonates or hydrogencarbonates also produce carbon dioxide. Lemon juice and lime water do not need visible bubbles to demonstrate neutralisation.

Activity 2.1 · Test with both litmus papers

Aim and question. Which samples behave as acids, bases or neutral solutions?

Materials. Approved labelled solutions, clean droppers, separate red and blue paper pieces.

Prediction. An acidic sample turns blue paper red.

  1. Label two sets of fresh strips.
  2. Apply one sample to each matched pair using its own dropper.
  3. Log both changes and sort A (acidic), B (basic) and C (neither changes).
Sample or stepExpected observation
Lemon juiceRed, red
Baking soda solutionBlue, blue
Sugar solutionRed, blue

Explanation and conclusion. Two tests resolve cases where one strip stays unchanged.

Precautions. Keep drops small and use eye protection. Use virtual samples for cleaners.

Follow-up. Why would a mixed dropper give misleading results?

Activity 2.7 · Follow the neutralisation demonstration

Aim and question. How does adding a base change the indication from diluted lemon juice?

Materials. Teacher demonstration or virtual model. Diluted lemon juice, blue litmus solution, labelled lime water and separate droppers.

Prediction. The indicator is red at first and can become blue after enough base is added.

  1. Observe the indicator in diluted lemon juice.
  2. Watch the teacher add lime water gradually with mixing.
  3. Record colour and connect it to the relative acid or base remaining.
Sample or stepExpected observation
Acid still in excessRed
Base in excessBlue

Explanation and conclusion. The colour shows that the mixture shifted through the indicator’s transition. A blue result means basic behaviour.

Precautions. Teacher handles lime water and waste. Students may use the virtual alternative.

Follow-up. Why can extra lemon reverse the observed shift?

Projects and deeper enquiry

Create paper rangoli with teacher-approved turmeric, dilute baking soda and an indicator control. For water comparisons use prepared or safely collected samples under supervision. Indicator readings cannot establish drinking-water safety. For soil compare filtered teacher-prepared water extracts and record the source. Vinegar reacting with eggshell or marble can release carbon dioxide from the carbonate. Soap does not supply the acidic conditions for that observation.

Explore further · optional enrichment

The topics below extend the chapter. The core tests are litmus, red rose, turmeric, onion and the NCERT neutralisation demonstration.

The pH scale

In water, pH below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral near room temperature, and above 7 is basic. The simulator uses example pH values to demonstrate the scale. Household samples vary.

Other indicators

Hibiscus (China rose) is a different plant from the textbook’s red rose. Some preparations give a deeper pink in acidic conditions and greener shades in basic conditions. Red cabbage often shifts from pink-red through purple to blue-green, but growing conditions and preparation affect these outcomes. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic and neutral solutions and becomes pink in its usual basic transition range. Methyl orange changes from red at low pH to yellow above its transition range, including near neutrality. Use teacher-supervised demonstrations or virtual charts.

Stomach acid helps digestion. Some antacids supply a base such as magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia) that reacts with some of this acid. This animation illustrates chemistry. It gives no dose or treatment instruction.

30 worked questions and reasoning examples

NCERT questions 1–12

NCERT Q1 · Reverse the blue colour of an originally red strip

Worked answer. The first test shows a base. Excess vinegar supplies enough acid to overcome the base and produce a red response. Option iii.

NCERT Q2 · Compare A, B and C using three indicators

Worked answer. A turns red litmus blue, B turns turmeric red, and C turns red rose green. Each is evidence of basic behaviour. Option iv.

NCERT Q3 · Interpret Figs. 2.13–2.15

Worked answer. Compare the wetted end with the original pink strip. In order: green means basic, unchanged pink indicates neutral in this setup, and red means acidic.

NCERT Q4 · Classify the sample in the indicator table

Worked answer. It is acidic. Blue litmus turned red. Red litmus and turmeric can stay unchanged with an acid, so both observations fit.

NCERT Q5 · Choose a test that does not rely on colour

Worked answer. The onion example is an olfactory indicator. Its odour tends to weaken in the basic sample. Use labelled teacher-controlled samples or a simulated comparison. Never ask a blindfolded student to handle or smell unknown liquids.

NCERT Q6 · Plan combinations for a hidden message

Worked answer. Use baking soda solution as the ink and turmeric as the developer to obtain reddish-brown strokes. Alternatively use lemon juice and blue litmus solution for red strokes, or an approved weak basic ink and red rose extract for green strokes. Try each combination on scrap paper with controls.

NCERT Q7 · Predict the grape juice mixture after adding baking soda

Worked answer. The red shade indicates acidic conditions. Adding a little base may reduce acidity but leave the mixture acidic. Enough base to make it basic can shift the red rose indicator toward green. Grape pigments may obscure the change.

NCERT Q8 · Reveal writing made with orange juice

Worked answer. Orange juice supplies acidic material on the dried sheet. Apply blue litmus solution to a small test area; acidic marks turn red against a bluer background. Turmeric is less suitable because acid and the background can both leave it yellow.

NCERT Q9 · Explain how to make a natural indicator

Worked answer. Teacher-prepared warm water extracts colour from red rose petals. Filter the extract, preserve an untreated portion, and check it against a known acid and base.

NCERT Q10 · Use turmeric on three colourless samples

Worked answer. Baking soda solution produces a reddish-brown result. Vinegar and sugar solution both leave it yellow, so another test is needed to distinguish them.

NCERT Q11 · Interpret green red rose extract and add excess amla juice

Worked answer. The original solution is basic. Amla juice supplies acid. If enough is added to make the mixture acidic, the indicator can give a red shade.

NCERT Q12 · Complete the soil flowchart

Worked answer. Use both colours of litmus on a filtered soil-water extract. For acidic soil the textbook suggests suitable lime. For basic soil it suggests organic matter such as compost. In real decisions consider crops, nutrients and soil advice.

Additional worked examples

Example 1 · Classify a clear sample that turns blue litmus red

Worked answer. The observation is blue→red. That is the acidic response. Classify it as acidic under the test conditions.

Example 2 · Interpret two unchanged litmus strips

Worked answer. Both colours retain their starting appearance. With working strips and a suitable concentration, the sample behaves as neutral.

Example 3 · Interpret a single unchanged blue strip

Worked answer. Blue remains blue in both basic and neutral samples. Test a fresh portion with red litmus to decide.

Example 4 · Justify using separate droppers

Worked answer. A shared dropper can transfer acid or base into the next sample. Separate droppers protect the evidence.

Example 5 · Identify one reliable basic result

Worked answer. Red litmus became blue. Basic solutions produce this change, so it is evidence of a base.

Example 6 · Investigate unexpectedly pale rose extract

Worked answer. Compare the untouched extract with known acid and base. If those also show weak changes, prepare a fresh batch before retesting unknowns.

Example 7 · Explain the water control in a turmeric test

Worked answer. Water checks the normal response of the wetted paper. A different colour with baking soda can then be attributed to the sample conditions.

Example 8 · Explain a turmeric stain exposed to soap

Worked answer. Basic soap solution changes the turmeric pigment, creating a reddish-brown shade. This explains a stain-based indicator example.

Example 9 · Distinguish a food example from a lab test

Worked answer. Vinegar contains acetic acid. Use blue litmus for scientific evidence. Tasting the solution cannot substitute for a test.

Example 10 · Check the green rose result

Worked answer. Green is the basic response in the textbook preparation. Confirm on a fresh sample with red litmus turning blue.

Example 11 · Decide when to repeat a test

Worked answer. Turmeric becomes reddish-brown but red litmus remains red. Check the strips with controls, clean the equipment, and repeat before classifying.

Example 12 · Name the reaction products and energy change

Worked answer. A typical acid plus base reaction produces salt and water. The process can release heat to the surroundings.

Example 13 · Explain a basic ending after lemon and lime water

Worked answer. Addition of lime water lowers the acid excess. When litmus is blue at the end, the solution is on the basic side of its transition.

Example 14 · Predict the effect of adding too little lime water

Worked answer. Some acid may remain. If the mixture is still sufficiently acidic, the litmus remains red.

Example 15 · Explain why counting drops is insufficient

Worked answer. Different solutions have different concentrations and drops may differ in volume. Observe the indicator and control the setup.

Example 16 · Compare neutralisation with removing contaminants

Worked answer. Adjusting acidity addresses one property of wastewater. Other pollutants require other tests and treatment steps.

Example 17 · Explain poor growth in verified neutral soil

Worked answer. Neutrality answers one chemistry question. Nutrient deficiency, drainage problems or pests can still affect plant health.

Example 18 · Contrast vinegar and soap on marble

Worked answer. Vinegar supplies acid that reacts with the carbonate and releases carbon dioxide. Soap solution does not produce that acidic reaction.
