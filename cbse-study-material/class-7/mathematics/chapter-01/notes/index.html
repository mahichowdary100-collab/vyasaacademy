1. Why large numbers matter

Population and services

Compare town populations, plan bus capacity, and make sense of city census tables.

Money and budgets

Read project costs in lakhs or crores and check whether a national or state budget allocation can cover a project.

Distance and astronomy

Estimate travel time from a distance and a daily rate. Keep units with every quantity.

Learning objectives

  • Read, write, form and compare large whole numbers using place value.
  • Translate between Indian and international comma groups and number names.
  • Round at a stated place, choose sensible estimates and explain their limits.
  • Use regrouping to simplify products and solve multi-step, real-world problems.

2. Place value and the Indian system

The face value of a digit is the digit itself. Its place value is its face value multiplied by the value of its column. Each move left multiplies the column value by 10. A zero holds a position even when it contributes nothing to the expanded form.

To write Indian commas, keep three digits at the far right, then take digits in pairs. For 42860315, the groups are 4 | 28 | 60 | 315, so write 4,28,60,315. Read them as four crore, twenty-eight lakh, sixty thousand, three hundred fifteen. An all-zero group has no spoken name, but its places still appear in the numeral.

Standard and expanded forms

Standard: 6,04,20,709. Expanded: 6,00,00,000 + 4,00,000 + 20,000 + 700 + 9. To go back, align the place values and fill unused places with zero.

The successor of a whole number \(n\) is \(n+1\). For any positive whole number the predecessor is \(n-1\). At a boundary, carry or borrow through the digits: 99,999 → 1,00,000 → 1,00,001. Zero has no predecessor within the whole numbers.

Convert in two steps

  1. From Indian units to international units: 24 crore = 24 × 10,000,000 = 240,000,000 = 240 million.
  2. From international units to Indian units: 680 million = 680,000,000. Divide by 10,000,000 to find 68 crore.

Arabic-derived arab is the larger Indian unit given in the chapter: one arab equals 100 crore or one billion. Trillion is included here as a scale extension; one trillion equals 1 lakh crore.

4. Compare, order and form numbers

  1. Remove grouping separators and ignore any leading zeros. The number with more digits is greater.
  2. If the lengths match, compare from the left. Stop at the first place where the digits differ.
  3. Ascending runs smallest to greatest. Descending runs greatest to smallest.

To form the greatest number from a set of digits, arrange them from high to low. To form the least number with the required length, put the smallest nonzero digit first and place the other digits in increasing order. For 0, 6, 3, 3 and 8, the two extremes are 86,330 and 30,368.

For an extra condition, first reserve the units digit. An even number ends in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8; a multiple of 5 ends in 0 or 5. Test any allowable endings that might change the leading digits. When deleting digits from a string, keep the order of the digits you retain.

5. Exact values, nearest neighbours and estimates

An exact value states the measured or counted number as given. An approximate value gives a useful nearby number. Use exact values for identifiers such as phone numbers, account references and ticket codes. Use estimates when a rough scale is enough.

Rounding rule for positive whole numbers

Identify the two multiples of the requested place that bracket the number. Choose the closer one; at an exact midpoint choose the higher one. The quick method checks the next digit on the right: 0–4 keeps the rounding digit, while 5–9 increases it. Replace lower places by zeros. An exact multiple stays as it is.

A suitable unit depends on the decision. For a report, nearest lakh may be clear; for buying enough boxes, deliberately round the number of boxes up when there is a remainder. Round each original number directly to the requested place rather than repeatedly rounding intermediate results.

Estimation shortcut

Pick compatible values you can calculate mentally. When the decision needs a guarantee, use bounds: at most 198 items costing at most ₹49 per item must cost less than 200 × ₹50 = ₹10,000. An ordinary nearest-value estimate alone does not give that guarantee.

6. Operations, products and mental methods

  • Add or subtract: align place values and carry or borrow across zeros. Check a subtraction by adding the difference back to the smaller quantity.
  • Multiply: break a factor into convenient parts. For instance, \(247 \times 98 = 247 \times (100-2) = 24{,}700-494 = 24{,}206\).
  • Regroup factors: factor a number to create 10, 100 or 1,000. For example, \(125 \times 32 = 125 \times 8 \times 4 = 4{,}000\). Doubling one factor and halving the other also keeps their product unchanged when done exactly.
  • Divide: estimate the quotient, then check that divisor × quotient + remainder gives the original dividend and that the remainder is smaller than the divisor. Include the unit and explain the remainder in context.

How long is the product?

Two positive numbers with \(m\) and \(n\) digits have a product with either \(m+n-1\) or \(m+n\) digits. The smallest three-digit times two-digit product is 100 × 10 = 1,000. The greatest is 999 × 99 = 98,901. Everything between them has four or five digits. To predict the possibilities for other digit counts, multiply the smallest and largest numbers of those lengths.

Multiplying by 10, 100 or 1,000 shifts all whole-number digits one, two or three places left. In 700 × 800, \(7 \times 8\) is multiplied by \(100 \times 100\), so the result is 5,60,000. When the leading product also ends in zero, keep that zero too: 60 × 50 = 3,000.

7. Investigate with large numbers

Compare to a familiar scale

If a floor is 3 m tall, a 72 m tower is roughly 24 floors high. Compare like units first and state the assumed height per floor.

Capacity and rates

At 40 passengers per bus, 3,000 buses hold 1,20,000 people. That is 30,000 fewer than a crowd of 1,50,000. Compare passengers with passengers, then decide what the result means.

Calculate in stages

At two counts per second, one hour gives 7,200 counts and 10 hours gives 72,000. Leave room for breaks when interpreting the model.

Digit puzzles

Count digits in blocks: 1–9 uses 9 digits and 10–99 uses 180. To find the 75th digit of the stream 123…, remove the first 9. The 66th digit in the two-digit block is the second digit of its 33rd number, 42. It is 2.

Limited buttons and number cards

Using only +10,000 and +100 from zero, 37,400 takes 3 ten-thousand presses and 74 hundred presses. A target such as 12,450 is impossible with these buttons because all reachable totals are multiples of 100. For number cards, build near the target and compare the absolute differences.

Toothpick digits and word puzzles

On a seven-segment display, the digit 1 needs two segments and no digit uses fewer. With exactly 12 segments, six digits is the maximum length and only 111111 achieves it. For a word puzzle, choose a spelling convention first and use a consistent way to count letters.

Examination tip

Show your assumptions, units and comparison. Check whether a rounded quantity was supplied as an estimate. In a question about changing digits or moving sticks, state what is allowed before deciding which number is greatest.

9. Worked examples

These original examples follow the textbook concepts. Open one at a time and try the question before reading the steps.

Example 1 · Read an Indian number

Question: Write 7040809 with Indian commas and name it.

  1. Rule: Group the last three digits first, then work left in pairs.
  2. Work: 70 / 40 / 809 gives seventy lakh, forty thousand, eight hundred nine.

Final answer: 70,40,809

Example 2 · Recognise face and place value

Question: Find the face and place value of 7 in 2,73,14,608.

  1. Rule: The face value is the digit itself. The place value is the digit times the value of its column.
  2. Work: The 7 is in the ten-lakh column, so multiply 7 by 10,00,000.

Final answer: Face value 7; place value 70,00,000.

Example 3 · Expand a number

Question: Expand 63,05,407.

  1. Rule: Write each nonzero digit as its place contribution.
  2. Work: The nonzero positions are ten lakh, lakh, thousand, hundred and ones.

Final answer: 60,00,000 + 3,00,000 + 5,000 + 400 + 7

Example 4 · Rebuild from place values

Question: Write 8,00,00,000 + 30,00,000 + 2,000 + 90 + 4 in standard form.

  1. Rule: Add contributions column by column; insert 0 where a column is empty.
  2. Work: Combine the groups: 8 crore, 30 lakh, 2 thousand and 94.

Final answer: 8,30,02,094

Example 5 · Cross a boundary

Question: Give the predecessor and successor of 10,00,000.

  1. Rule: Subtract 1 for the predecessor and add 1 for the successor.
  2. Work: Subtracting borrows across all six zeros. Adding changes only the ones digit.

Final answer: 9,99,999 and 10,00,001

Example 6 · Build the next place

Question: How many ten-thousands make 3 lakhs?

  1. Rule: Each lakh contains ten ten-thousands.
  2. Work: Compute 3,00,000 ÷ 10,000 = 30.

Final answer: 30 ten-thousands

Example 7 · Regroup internationally

Question: Write 3,45,67,890 with international commas and say it aloud.

  1. Rule: Remove commas and regroup in threes from the right.
  2. Work: 34 / 567 / 890 gives thirty-four million, five hundred sixty-seven thousand, eight hundred ninety.

Final answer: 34,567,890

Example 8 · Name a number with empty groups

Question: Write two billion, seventy million, and fifteen as digits.

  1. Rule: Write each group as a three-digit block after the leading group.
  2. Work: The blocks are 2 / 070 / 000 / 015.

Final answer: 2,070,000,015

Example 9 · Convert lakhs to millions

Question: Express 35 lakh in millions.

  1. Rule: One million equals 10 lakh.
  2. Work: Divide the count of lakhs by 10: 35 ÷ 10 = 3.5.

Final answer: 3.5 million

Example 10 · Convert billions to crores

Question: Express 2.4 billion in crores.

  1. Rule: One billion equals 100 crore.
  2. Work: Multiply 2.4 by 100.

Final answer: 240 crore

Example 11 · Compare with zeros in the middle

Question: Compare 4,09,89,000 and 4,10,02,000.

  1. Rule: For equal digit counts, inspect digits from left to right.
  2. Work: The crore digits agree. The ten-lakh digits are 0 and 1, so stop there.

Final answer: 4,09,89,000 < 4,10,02,000

Example 12 · Order three populations

Question: Order 9,95,100; 10,05,010; 9,59,900 from least to greatest.

  1. Rule: Compare digit count first and resolve ties from the left.
  2. Work: Both six-digit counts come before the seven-digit count. Within the first pair, 59 thousand is smaller than 95 thousand.

Final answer: 9,59,900 < 9,95,100 < 10,05,010

Example 13 · Arrange digits with a zero

Question: Make the least and greatest five-digit numbers from 7, 0, 2, 7, 4.

  1. Rule: The leading digit of a five-digit number must be nonzero.
  2. Work: For the smallest, place 2 first, then 0, 4, 7, 7. For the greatest, use descending order.

Final answer: 20,477 and 77,420

Example 14 · Round one number at two scales

Question: Round 6,78,459 to the nearest 10 and 100.

  1. Rule: Check the digit immediately to the right of the requested place.
  2. Work: The ones digit 9 raises the tens. The tens digit 5 raises the hundreds when rounding the original number.

Final answer: 6,78,460 and 6,78,500

Example 15 · Use nearest neighbours

Question: Round 4,37,501 to the nearest 1,000.

  1. Rule: Compare distances to consecutive multiples of 1,000.
  2. Work: The neighbours are 4,37,000 and 4,38,000. Their distances are 501 and 499.

Final answer: 4,38,000

Example 16 · Round across digit groups

Question: Round 19,95,000 to the nearest 10,000 and lakh.

  1. Rule: Use the original number at each scale; midpoint values go up.
  2. Work: It is halfway from 19,90,000 to 20,00,000 and closer to 20,00,000 than to 19,00,000.

Final answer: 20,00,000 at both scales

Example 17 · Round to crore

Question: Estimate 7,49,99,999 to the nearest crore.

  1. Rule: The midpoint between 7 and 8 crore is 7,50,00,000.
  2. Work: The given value is 1 less than the midpoint, so use the lower crore.

Final answer: 7,00,00,000

Example 18 · Budget arithmetic

Question: A city allocates ₹3,45,75,000 and adds ₹82,50,000. It spends ₹1,67,25,000. What remains?

  1. Rule: Add the allocations and subtract spending.
  2. Work: The total is ₹4,28,25,000. Subtract ₹1,67,25,000; check by adding the remainder back.

Final answer: ₹2,61,00,000

Example 19 · Pack with a remainder

Question: Pack 2,45,075 books in cartons of 125. How many full cartons and how many books remain?

  1. Rule: Divide and check that the remainder is less than the carton size.
  2. Work: 125 × 1,960 = 2,45,000; subtract this from 2,45,075.

Final answer: 1,960 full cartons and 75 books left

Example 20 · Estimate a product

Question: Estimate 198 × 5,040 with compatible values and check the exact answer.

  1. Rule: Round to convenient factors, then use compensation for exact multiplication.
  2. Work: 200 × 5,000 = 10,00,000. Exactly, (200 − 2) × 5,040 = 10,08,000 − 10,080.

Final answer: Estimate 10,00,000; exact 9,97,920.

Example 21 · Multiply near a round number

Question: Compute 4,608 × 99 mentally.

  1. Rule: Use 99 = 100 − 1 and distribute.
  2. Work: 4,608 × 100 = 4,60,800. Subtract 4,608.

Final answer: 4,56,192

Example 22 · Use an equivalent product

Question: Calculate 48 × 25 without long multiplication.

  1. Rule: Multiplying by 25 is multiplying by 100 and dividing by 4.
  2. Work: 48 ÷ 4 = 12, then 12 × 100.

Final answer: 1,200

Example 23 · Track powers of ten in a product

Question: Calculate 700 × 8,000.

  1. Rule: Separate 7 × 8 from the factors of ten.
  2. Work: 7 × 8 = 56; 100 × 1,000 = 1,00,000. Multiply 56 by 1,00,000.

Final answer: 56,00,000

Example 24 · Check the number of digits

Question: Can two four-digit whole numbers multiply to a six-digit answer?

  1. Rule: Use the smallest possible factors to test the lower bound.
  2. Work: 1,000 × 1,000 = 10,00,000 has seven digits. The greatest four-digit product is 9,999 × 9,999 = 9,99,80,001, which has eight digits.

Final answer: No; possible lengths are seven and eight.

Example 25 · Estimate a count from a sample

Question: A 50 g sample contains about 320 grains. Estimate the number in a 2 kg bag.

  1. Rule: Assume the sample represents the bag and use matching units.
  2. Work: 2 kg = 2,000 g, which is 40 lots of 50 g. Multiply 40 × 320.

Final answer: About 12,800 grains

Example 26 · Explain why estimates differ

Question: Two pupils estimate 4,96,800 + 3,46,200. One rounds to lakhs, the other to ten-thousands. Compare the results.

  1. Rule: Changing the rounding place changes the accuracy.
  2. Work: At lakh scale, 5,00,000 + 3,00,000 = 8,00,000. At ten-thousand scale, 5,00,000 + 3,50,000 = 8,50,000. The exact sum is 8,43,000, so the errors are 43,000 and 7,000.

Final answer: 8,50,000 is closer for these data.

Example 27 · Choose a useful unit

Question: A model space probe travels 18,500 km each day for 36 days. Give the distance in lakh kilometres.

  1. Rule: Calculate the distance, then divide by one lakh.
  2. Work: 18,500 × (30 + 6) = 5,55,000 + 1,11,000 = 6,66,000 km.

Final answer: 6.66 lakh km

11. Quick revision and exam preparation

Place value → Read

Face value = digit. Place value = digit × column value. A move left multiplies the column value by 10.

Group → Convert

Indian grouping uses 3 then 2 from the right. International uses 3 throughout. One million = 10 lakh; one billion = 100 crore.

Digits → Order

Compare length, then compare from the left. For the least number made from given digits, start with the least nonzero one.

Neighbours → Round

Locate the multiples at the requested place, compare distances and use the higher neighbour for midpoints.

Factors → Calculate

Use place value, compensation or pairs like 4 × 25 and 8 × 125. Check exact answers against a quick estimate.

Units → Model

Use the same units, state your assumptions, and compare a large figure with a familiar reference.

What to practise for a school assessment

Give special attention to converting names, ordering close numbers, rounding at the right place, explaining product lengths, and interpreting a word problem. The practice bank includes competency, case-study and higher-order questions for those skills.

Last-minute checklist

  • Can I write lakh, crore, million and billion in digits and explain how they convert?
  • Did I label the system when writing commas and name the unit when rounding?
  • Did I check the first differing digit instead of comparing the last group?
  • Did I carry, borrow or account for any remainder? Are the units and the scale plausible?

For a final recap, revisit the Indian chart, the international chart and conversion table, and the rounding table.
