Before you start

How can a compass produce a perfect right angle? Why do three hexagons fit together around a point? Can a grid with an even area still refuse every domino arrangement? This chapter uses geometric reasoning to explain what you can draw and what you can tile.

What you’ll learn

  • Construct and justify bisectors, angles, parallels, arches and hexagons.
  • Use congruence and equal compass radii as evidence.
  • Design tilings and use area and colouring to prove impossibility.

Plan your study

Allow about 100–120 minutes for the notes and construction practice. Have a pencil, compass, straightedge and grid paper ready.

Review Parallel and Intersecting Lines, A Tale of Three Intersecting Lines, and Geometric Twins.

6.1 · Eyes, Perpendicular Bisectors and Right Angles

Construction tools

Use an unmarked straightedge to join known points and extend lines. Use a compass to transfer an equal distance or draw a circle. Keep the same compass opening whenever two radii must be equal. A measured starting segment may be supplied. A protractor can check the result after a ruler-and-compass construction.

A symmetrical eye

Let X and Y be the endpoints of an eye. A centre for either arc must be the same distance from X and Y. Choose A and B at mirror positions on the perpendicular bisector. Use the same opening from each centre to draw the shorter arc from X to Y on the opposite side. Reflection across XY interchanges the centres and arcs.

Bisection and the perpendicular bisector

Bisection divides a quantity into two equal parts. If M bisects XY, then XM = MY. The perpendicular bisector also passes through M at 90°.

  1. Draw XY and open the compass to more than half of XY.
  2. Draw arcs centred at X above and below XY. Without changing the opening, repeat from Y.
  3. Mark both intersections P and Q. Join PQ and label M where it crosses XY.

Both P and Q are equally far from X and Y. If M is the midpoint of XY, triangles PMX and PMY have three equal corresponding sides, by SSS. The angles at M are equal and form a straight angle, so each is 90°. Apply the same reasoning on the other side. Conversely every point on the perpendicular bisector has equal distances from X and Y, using SAS with the two right angles.

Exam tip. Use a radius \(r>\frac{XY}{2}\). At exactly half of XY the arcs touch once. A smaller opening gives no intersection. Equal openings are essential within a pair. Valid pairs can use different common openings and give points on the same bisector.

A 90° angle at O on a given line

Draw an arc centred at O that cuts the line at X and Y. Then OX = OY. Construct the perpendicular bisector of XY and extend it through O. For a point P outside the line, use an arc centred at P that intersects the line twice. Construct the perpendicular bisector of the chord to obtain a perpendicular through P.

Śulba-Sūtras rope method

The NCERT chapter connects these constructions to geometry recorded in the Śulba-Sūtras. Mark the midpoint of the working length of a folded rope. Fasten the ends to two pegs, X and Y, and pull the midpoint taut to one side. Mark P. Pull it taut to the other side and mark Q. Each rope half has the same length, so each mark is equally far from X and Y. Line PQ is their perpendicular bisector.

Use the perpendicular bisector simulator and rope lab to see why the radius or half-rope length must exceed half the peg spacing.

6.1 · Angle Bisection, Copying and Parallel Lines

Bisect an angle

For ∠AOB, draw an arc centred at O that meets the rays at X and Y. Use a common radius from X and Y to find a point P inside the angle, distinct from O. Draw OP. Triangles OXP and OYP have OX = OY, XP = YP and common OP. By SSS congruence, the angles at O are equal.

Bisect a 90° angle to get 45°. Bisect a constructed 60° angle to get 30°, then bisect 30° to get 15°. Mark auxiliary arcs so someone can see how each angle was obtained.

Repeating units and copying an angle

A repeated angle gives consistent direction changes in a geometric border. To copy ∠AOB at T, draw a ray from T. Draw arcs of one radius at O and T. Label the original ray intersections X and Y and the corresponding baseline mark X′. Set the compass to chord XY. With centre X′, cut the arc about T at Y′. Choose the intended side of the ray and draw TY′.

Copying just the arc radius leaves many possible directions. Copying the chord as well fixes the angle up to reflection by SSS. When repeating units along a straight line, transfer both the required distances and any angles that control direction.

Parallel through an external point

Draw a transversal through the external point P to meet the given line at Q. At P copy the angle made by the transversal and the line at Q into the corresponding position. Draw the second line through P and extend it. The converse of the corresponding-angle property proves the lines parallel. An alternate-interior-angle construction also works if the positions are chosen correctly.

6.1 · Arches, Regular Hexagons and Geometric Art

Trefoil and pointed arches

Choose a horizontal support AB and its perpendicular bisector for a symmetry axis. For a trefoil, make equal support angles at A and B and transfer equal lengths to place reflected upper support points. On these equal support chords, construct reflected circular lobes with a matching compass opening. Add the centre lobe with its centre on the symmetry axis. Keep joins visible in the construction drawing and trace only the chosen arcs in the finished design.

For a pointed arch use two reflected circle arcs. With endpoints A and B as the centres and radius AB, their upper intersection C is the apex. Trace the arc from A to C centred at B and the arc from C to B centred at A. The textbook also explores pointed outlines built on two equal support segments and their midpoints. Changing an opening consistently changes the rise. An opening no greater than half the centre spacing cannot create a positive-rise apex.

A regular hexagon from six equal compass steps

Draw a circle with centre O. Mark A and keep the compass set to radius OA. Mark B where the arc from A cuts the circle. Repeat around the circle in one direction until A, B, C, D, E and F are marked. Join adjacent marks. Each triangle formed by a side and two radii is equilateral because all three sides equal the radius. Its central angle is 60°, six steps fill 360°, and each polygon corner is two 60° angles, or 120°.

To construct 60° at A, mark B on the starting ray. Draw equal-radius arcs with radius AB centred at both A and B. Join A to one intersection C. Triangle ABC is equilateral, so its angle at A is 60°. Bisect that angle to find 30° and bisect again to find 15°.

Stars, inflexed arcs and repeated patterns

Join alternate vertices of a regular hexagon to get two overlapping equilateral triangles and a six-point star. Their side directions create 60° corner triangles. Repeat a circle arc, rotate the same motif around a centre, or reflect one half about a symmetry axis. In an inflexed-arc design, use compass centres on opposite sides of a support so successive arcs bend in different directions.

Try a second arch

Materials. A compass, ruler and paper.

  1. Keep the same endpoints and symmetry axis.
  2. Try a new equal radius above half the endpoint distance.
  3. Compare the positions of the top intersection and the visible joins.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

Both arrangements remain symmetrical because reflection interchanges the centres and arcs.

6.2 · Tangrams, Finite Tilings and Tiling the Plane

What counts as a tiling?

A region is tiled when every point is covered, interiors of pieces do not overlap, and no piece extends outside the boundary. Different tilings can use different arrangements. Edge-to-edge plane tilings also need compatible shared edges. A 360° sum at one vertex is necessary there, and a repeating pattern establishes coverage past that vertex.

Tangrams

A square tangram has seven pieces: two large right isosceles triangles, one medium triangle, two small triangles, one square and one parallelogram. Slide, rotate or turn a piece over without stretching it. The small triangles give a convenient area unit. The large triangles use four units each, the medium triangle, square and parallelogram two each, and the two small triangles one each. The total is 16 small-triangle units.

Regular polygons and vertex angles

Regular tileInterior angleTiles around an edge-to-edge vertex
Equilateral triangle60°6
Square90°4
Regular hexagon120°3
Regular pentagon108°Three leave 36°. Four overlap by 72°.

Equilateral triangles, squares and regular hexagons each repeat to tile the plane. A regular pentagon cannot do so in a single-shape edge-to-edge tiling by the 360° test. That conclusion does not rule out non-regular pentagons. Some mixed arrangements work, such as alternating squares and equilateral triangles in patterns that extend consistently. A matching sum alone never proves that the whole plane is tiled.

Make a pattern that repeats

Look for a small unit you can slide across the plane with shared edges. On a finite floor, check the entire boundary as well as the tiling inside. Comparing tile area with floor area is an initial check. You still need containment and no overlaps.

6.2 · Grid Tilings and the Colouring Method

Dominoes on a grid

A standard domino covers two squares sharing an edge. You can turn it to place it horizontally or vertically. A rectangle with at least one even dimension can be tiled: pair cells along an even side. If both dimensions are odd, the area is odd and every domino covers exactly two cells, so a tiling is impossible.

Chessboard colouring

Colour grid cells alternately dark and light. Every edge-adjacent pair has one square of each colour. Any complete cover therefore requires equal counts. A 5 × 3 board has eight dark cells and seven light cells if the top-left is dark. Remove a light square to leave eight dark and six light. Although 14 is even, this board cannot be covered.

Equal colour counts are necessary, but a grid with isolated areas can still fail. For example, a row of four cells with its two middle cells removed leaves two squares of opposite colours. They cannot share one domino because they have no common edge. A completed placement is proof of possibility. A contradiction with parity or colour counts is proof of impossibility.

Switch the missing square

Materials. A 5 × 3 grid and cut-out dominoes.

  1. Remove the corner (row 1, column 1).
  2. Pair the rest of row 1 horizontally, and pair rows 2–3 and 4–5 vertically.
  3. Now remove row 1, column 2 instead, keeping the corner.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

The first patch has a valid tiling of seven dominoes. The second leaves an 8–6 colour imbalance and is impossible.

Rules and construction answer format

Construction or tilingProperty to cite
Perpendicular bisectorEquidistant points lie on it. It meets the segment at its midpoint at 90°.
Angle bisector or copyThree matching lengths give SSS and equal angles.
Parallel linesEqual corresponding angles with a common transversal.
Regular hexagonSide equals radius. Central angle 60°, vertex angle 120°.
Tiles around a pointAngular sum 360°, plus matching edges where relevant.
Domino colouringEach domino covers adjacent opposite colours.

Present your construction in an exam

  1. State what is given and the required construction.
  2. List your instruments and sketch a rough figure where helpful.
  3. Write the construction steps in order, naming each centre, compass opening and intersection.
  4. Draw a clear final figure. Keep useful arcs and mark equal lengths, angles or the right-angle square.
  5. Prove the required result using a property such as SSS, reflecting equal lengths, or the parallel-angle converse.
  6. Write the conclusion in a complete sentence with the labelled result.

Exam tip. A diagram can suggest an answer. Use the given or constructed relationships in your justification.

Basic

Example 1 · Name the bisected parts

Given or required. XY is 14 cm and M is its midpoint

  1. Divide the length by 2
  2. Equal parts are 7 cm each

Answer. XM = MY = 7 cm

Example 2 · Choose the compass opening

Given or required. XY = 10 cm

  1. Half of XY is 5 cm
  2. 6 cm exceeds 5 cm, so the arcs intersect twice

Answer. Use 6 cm

Example 3 · Identify a valid eye centre

Given or required. AX = AY = 5 cm

  1. A is equidistant from X and Y
  2. Its reflection B also has BX = BY = 5 cm

Answer. Use A and B for reflected arcs

Example 4 · Recognise a bisected angle

Given or required. ∠AOB = 84°

  1. The rays to the sides make equal halves
  2. 84° ÷ 2 = 42°

Answer. 42° each

Example 5 · Construct a 45° ray

Given or required. A line and a marked point O

  1. Construct a perpendicular through O
  2. Use equal-ray marks and SSS to bisect the 90° angle

Answer. 45°

Example 6 · Find an equilateral angle

Given or required. Triangle with three equal sides

  1. Equal sides give equal angles
  2. 180° ÷ 3 = 60°

Answer. 60°

Example 7 · Find the hexagon interior angle

Given or required. Six equilateral triangles meet at O

  1. At a hexagon vertex two triangle angles meet
  2. Each is 60°

Answer. 120°

Example 8 · Find domino count

Given or required. A 4 × 6 rectangle

  1. Area = 24 unit squares
  2. Divide by area 2 per domino

Answer. 12 dominoes

Example 9 · Decide whether four squares fit

Given or required. Each corner contributes 90°

  1. 4 × 90° = 360°
  2. Equal square edges match in a grid

Answer. They fit and extend in a square grid

Intermediate

Example 10 · Construct the perpendicular bisector

Given or required. XY = 8 cm

  1. Choose radius 5 cm
  2. Mark P and Q from two pairs of equal-radius arcs
  3. Join PQ and mark the crossing M
  4. PX = PY and QX = QY place P and Q on the perpendicular bisector

Answer. XM = MY = 4 cm and PQ ⟂ XY

Example 11 · Diagnose tangent arcs

Given or required. XY = 12 cm, opening 6 cm

  1. 6 + 6 = 12, so the circles touch at the midpoint
  2. One point alone leaves the bisector direction undetermined
  3. Increase the radius to 7 cm

Answer. Use an opening greater than 6 cm

Example 12 · Perpendicular at a chosen point

Given or required. O lies on line l

  1. Centre the compass at O to mark X and Y equally far away
  2. Draw equal arcs of radius greater than OX from X and Y
  3. Join the upper intersection to O

Answer. The result is perpendicular to l at O

Example 13 · Perpendicular through an outside point

Given or required. P lies off l

  1. Choose a radius from P that cuts l at X and Y
  2. P is equidistant from X and Y
  3. Find the midpoint M by endpoint arcs and join PM

Answer. PM ⟂ l

Example 14 · Use the rope method

Given or required. Pegs 8 m apart, working rope length 10 m

  1. Each half is 5 m, greater than 4 m
  2. Pull the midpoint taut on both sides
  3. Each mark stays 5 m from both pegs

Answer. Joining marks gives the perpendicular bisector

Example 15 · Justify an angle bisector

Given or required. Equal marks X and Y on the rays and XP = YP

  1. OX = OY by the first compass opening
  2. XP = YP by the second opening
  3. OP is common, giving SSS congruence

Answer. ∠XOP = ∠POY

Example 16 · Copy a 68° angle

Given or required. The source angle is 68°

  1. Use equal-radius arcs at the source and target vertices
  2. Transfer the source chord onto the target arc
  3. Connect the target vertex to the cut point
  4. SSS preserves the angle

Answer. The copy is 68°

Example 17 · Construct a parallel

Given or required. P lies outside line l

  1. Draw a transversal PQ to l
  2. Copy the angle at Q into corresponding position at P
  3. Extend the new ray through P

Answer. New line ∥ l by the corresponding-angle converse

Example 18 · Construct 15°

Given or required. A starting ray

  1. Make an equilateral triangle to obtain 60°
  2. Bisect 60° to get 30°
  3. Bisect 30° to get 15°

Answer. The final ray makes 15°

Example 19 · Construct a regular hexagon

Given or required. Circle of radius 3 cm

  1. Keep compass at 3 cm and step six times around the circle
  2. Join adjacent points
  3. The six equal 60° central angles close the walk

Answer. Each side is 3 cm, perimeter 18 cm

Example 20 · Find a tangram part

Given or required. Smallest triangle area is 3 cm²

  1. The square uses 16 small-triangle units
  2. A large triangle uses 4 units

Answer. Whole area 48 cm²; each large triangle 12 cm²

Example 21 · Test pentagons at a point

Given or required. Each regular pentagon angle is 108°

  1. 3 × 108° = 324°, leaving 36°
  2. 4 × 108° = 432°, exceeding 360°

Answer. Regular pentagons alone fail the edge-to-edge test

Example 28 · Compare two arch radii

Given or required. Same endpoint centres, two valid radii r and s with s greater than r

  1. Apex lies above the midpoint of the segment between centres
  2. For a fixed half-span, more radius means a larger vertical distance
  3. Each apex is on the same symmetry axis

True or false with justification · Questions 21–25

  1. Every bisector of a segment must be perpendicular. Basic

    Show hint

    A line can pass through the midpoint at a slant.

    Reveal solution

    False. Passing through the midpoint bisects the segment. A perpendicular bisector also makes 90°.

  2. A common compass radius longer than half the baseline produces two intersections. Basic

    Show hint

    Compare the sum of radii with the centre distance.

    Reveal solution

    True for distinct centres. There is one intersection on each side.

  3. A regular hexagon has 120° interior angles. Basic

    Show hint

    Use two 60° corners per vertex.

    Reveal solution

    True. 60° + 60° = 120°.

  4. Equal black and white counts prove every region can be domino-tiled. Basic

    Show hint

    Think of isolated cells.

    Reveal solution

    False. Opposite-colour squares may be separated.

  5. Square tiles can cover the whole plane in an edge-to-edge pattern. Basic

    Show hint

    Repeat rows of squares.

    Reveal solution

    True. Row lengths and side lengths match, and four corners fit at every vertex.

Short answer · Questions 36–45

  1. Describe how to construct the perpendicular bisector of a 6 cm segment. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Use a common opening exceeding 3 cm.

    Reveal solution

    Use 4 cm from each endpoint. Draw intersecting arcs above and below, join the intersections, and mark the midpoint. Equal endpoint distances prove the result.

  2. Explain the exact 90° construction at O on a line. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Mark two points equally far from O.

    Reveal solution

    Use the compass from O to mark X and Y. Bisect XY perpendicularly. Because O is its midpoint, the result passes through O.

  3. Describe how to construct a 30° angle without a protractor. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Start with an equilateral triangle.

    Reveal solution

    Draw an equilateral triangle by equal arcs from a baseline. One vertex is 60°. Use equal arcs from the two rays and join their intersection to the vertex to bisect it.

  4. If a 12 cm rope is fixed to pegs 10 cm apart, can its midpoint be pulled taut above the line? Intermediate

    Show hint

    Compare 6 cm and half the peg gap.

    Reveal solution

    Yes. Each half is 6 cm, greater than 5 cm. The two equal circles intersect on both sides.

  5. Why do circles of radius 5 cm from endpoints 12 cm apart fail to intersect? Intermediate

    Show hint

    Compare their combined reach.

    Reveal solution

    The total radii are 10 cm, less than centre spacing 12 cm, so the circles do not meet.

  6. How does a transferred chord help copy an angle? Intermediate

    Show hint

    Identify two radii plus one chord.

    Reveal solution

    Equal radius marks give the first two matching sides. Equal chord gives the third side. SSS preserves the angle at the vertex.

  7. Why do three hexagonal tiles fit at a vertex? Intermediate

    Show hint

    Each angle is 120°.

    Reveal solution

    3 × 120° = 360°. Regular hexagons also have equal side lengths, which match in a honeycomb.

  8. Can a 3 × 8 rectangle be domino-tiled? Show how. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Pair along the even dimension.

    Reveal solution

    Pair columns 1–2, 3–4, 5–6 and 7–8 in each row. Three rows need 12 dominoes.

  9. Can a regular pentagon share a full edge-to-edge vertex with only identical copies? Intermediate

    Show hint

    Compare three and four copies.

    Reveal solution

    Three give 324° and four give 432°, so there is no whole number of copies making 360°.

  10. A tangram has total area 32 cm². Find one large triangle’s area. Intermediate

    Show hint

    A large triangle is four of 16 area units.

    Reveal solution

    32 ÷ 16 = 2 cm² per small-triangle unit. One large triangle is 4 × 2 = 8 cm².

Long answer · Questions 46–50

  1. Construct and justify a perpendicular from outside point P to line l. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Make P equidistant from two points on l.

    Reveal solution

    Draw a circle about P that cuts l twice at X,Y. Draw equal endpoint arcs to construct the perpendicular bisector. P lies on it because PX = PY. Join P to the midpoint of XY to obtain the perpendicular.

  2. Explain a compass construction for the parallel through P to line l. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Copy an angle at the transversal’s intersection.

    Reveal solution

    Draw PQ meeting l. Use equal-radius arcs at Q and P; copy the chord between intersections onto the target arc in corresponding position. Draw the new line through P. By SSS the copied angle matches, so the corresponding-angle converse establishes parallelism.

  3. Explain why walking a radius around a circle produces exactly six regular hexagon sides. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Look at the first sector as a triangle.

    Reveal solution

    Two radii and the transferred radius chord are all equal. Each sector gives 60°. Six fill 360° exactly. Equal sides and angles of 120° confirm a regular hexagon.

  4. A 4 × 4 board loses its opposite corners. Prove or disprove a domino cover. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Opposite corners of this even-sided square have the same colour.

    Reveal solution

    A full board has 8 dark and 8 light. Removing two same-colour corners leaves 6 of that colour and 8 of the other. Every domino uses one of each, so no cover exists.

  5. Show how a 5 × 3 rectangle with its top-left square missing can be tiled. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Pair the two surviving cells of row 1.

    Reveal solution

    Place one horizontal domino in row 1. Pair rows 2–3, and rows 4–5, with three vertical pieces in each pair. The 7 dominoes cover all 14 surviving cells.

    Show hint

    Reflect centres about the supporting segment.

    Reveal solution

    Use endpoint arcs of one radius above 8 cm to locate the bisector. Choose centre A on it and reflect to B across the baseline. Each centre is equally far from both endpoints and reflection makes the outline symmetric.

  2. A 6 × 5 floor fits domino tiles. Compare horizontal-only and vertical-only plans. Advanced

    Show hint

    Each row has 5 cells; each column has 6.

    Reveal solution

    Horizontal-only fails because a row has odd length. Vertical-only works by pairing rows. The 30 cells require 15 dominoes.

  3. At a design corner are a hexagon and two equilateral triangles already. How many more equilateral triangles fill the angle? Advanced

    Show hint

    Add 120° + 60° + 60°.

    Reveal solution

    The total is 240°. Another 120° is needed, so add two equilateral triangles. A repeating tiling still needs edge compatibility.

  4. An accurately drawn perpendicular has an unmarked crossing on a baseline. How would you distinguish an arbitrary perpendicular from a perpendicular bisector? Advanced

    Show hint

    Locate and check the midpoint.

    Reveal solution

    Compare the crossing’s distances to the segment endpoints. It is the perpendicular bisector exactly when those distances are equal.

  5. An architect changes only one of two reflected arch compass openings. Predict the effect. Advanced

    Show hint

    Reflection preserves matching radii.

    Reveal solution

    Unequal openings break the radius equality under reflection. The curves generally cease to be mirror images, even if some points still line up.

Case study · Questions 61–62

  1. School mosaic: a pattern uses triangles, squares and hexagons. One corner has a triangle, two squares and a hexagon. Check the angle sum and explain whether that proves a whole tiling. Advanced

    Show hint

    Add 60, 90, 90 and 120.

    Reveal solution

    The sum is 360°, so the corner has no angular gap. To prove a tiling you must also show compatible shared edges and a pattern that covers the entire region.

    Show hint

    Use parity, colouring and a construction.

    Reveal solution

    Full patch has 15 cells, so impossible. Removing light leaves 8 dark and 6 light, also impossible. Removing the dark corner leaves equal counts and the row-pairing construction uses 7 dominoes.

HOTS · Questions 63–67

  1. Can you find the perpendicular bisector using two arc intersections on the same side of XY? Advanced

    Show hint

    Try two valid equal-radius settings.

    Reveal solution

    Yes. Use one common opening for the first pair and a different common opening for the second pair. The two marks on the same side are distinct and each equidistant from X and Y.

  2. Four unit squares form a row; its two middle cells are missing. Can a domino cover the remainder? Advanced

    Show hint

    Think about adjacency.

    Reveal solution

    No. The remaining squares have opposite colours and even area, but are not edge adjacent.

  3. Find another mixed angle combination with exactly 360° using at least one triangle, one square and one hexagon. Advanced

    Show hint

    Begin with 60 + 90 + 120 = 270.

    Reveal solution

    Add one more 90° square. The sum is 60 + 90 + 120 + 90 = 360. It is a local arrangement, so further checks are needed for tiling.

  4. If both side lengths of a rectangle are odd, can rotating dominoes fix its parity problem? Advanced

    Show hint

    Rotation does not change tile area.

    Reveal solution

    No. Product of odd integers is odd and each domino covers two cells in either orientation.

  5. One 60° ray and one 45° ray share the same baseline. Use them to check an independently constructed 15° angle. Advanced

    Show hint

    Subtract the angles on the same side of the baseline.

    Reveal solution

    The angle between them is 60° − 45° = 15°. This agrees with bisecting 60° twice.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Choose a valid radius
Incorrect. Set the opening exactly halfway across the segment.
Correction. Use more than half its length to obtain two distinct intersections.
Keep openings equal
Incorrect. Draw endpoint arcs with different openings and presume equidistance.
Correction. Use the same opening for both endpoints within one pair.
Keep two intersections
Incorrect. Join one arc intersection to an arbitrary point on the baseline.
Correction. Use both marked intersections to locate the line.
Find corresponding positions
Incorrect. Copy an angle without noting which side of the transversal it is on.
Correction. Choose matching positions and prove the parallel-angle converse applies.
Transfer the chord
Incorrect. Copy the arc radius and draw any second ray.
Correction. Transfer the chord as the third side for SSS.
Keep ray marks equal
Incorrect. Choose two different distances from the angle vertex.
Correction. Cut both rays with the same arc centred at the vertex.
Use the correct hexagon angle
Incorrect. Label the interior corner as 60°.
Correction. 60° is the central angle; the interior corner is 120°.
Avoid measurement shortcuts
Incorrect. Draw 30° using only a protractor when a compass construction is required.
Correction. Construct 60° first and bisect it using arcs.
Check beyond angle sums
Incorrect. Assume a 360° corner proves the entire region is tiled.
Correction. Check matching edges, gaps, overlaps and the boundary.
Check adjacency
Incorrect. Place a domino across two diagonal cells.
Correction. A domino covers only edge-adjacent cells.
Distinguish parity and colouring
Incorrect. Assume even area proves a domino cover.
Correction. Check opposite-colour counts and either find a placement or prove impossibility.
Keep parts rigid
Incorrect. Stretch a tangram triangle to reach the boundary.
Correction. Slide, rotate or turn the same shape over without resizing.

Quick revision and flashcards

Download the revision map

When do equal endpoint arcs intersect twice?

When the common radius is larger than half the segment.

Why does an angle bisector work?

SSS congruence makes the two angles at the vertex equal.

What makes the copied line parallel?

Equal corresponding angles with the same transversal.

What angles appear in a regular hexagon construction?

60° at the centre and 120° at a corner.

Which regular shapes tile the plane on their own?

Equilateral triangles, squares and regular hexagons can form edge-to-edge tilings.

What does the chessboard test prove?

Unequal colours prove impossibility. Equal counts only pass one necessary test.
