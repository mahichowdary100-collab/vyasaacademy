1. How expressions describe a situation

An arithmetic expression combines numbers with operations and, when needed, brackets. It gives a way to describe a quantity before calculating it. A number such as 24 names a value. An expression such as 4 × 6 tells you how to get it. An equation such as 4 × 6 = 24 states that two sides have the same value. An equation can be true or false.

Shopping

One ₹48 pouch and three ₹12 pencils cost \(48+3\times12=84\) rupees.

Distances

Four laps of 250 m followed by 80 m gives \(4\times250+80=1{,}080\) m.

Sharing

Sharing six bundles of nine cards among three groups gives \((6\times9)\div3=18\) cards per group.

Learning objectives

  • Write expressions for real situations and explain the operation signs.
  • Identify signed terms, read brackets and evaluate mixed operations in the correct order.
  • Compare and rewrite expressions using valid properties, then explain why the value stays the same.
  • Use mental shortcuts, interpret units and check common mistakes.

2. Simple expressions and their values

Four operations
SignActionExample
+Add quantities or join groups18 + 7 = 25
−Subtract, compare differences, or show a negative sign18 − 7 = 11
×Multiply to count equal groups or scale a quantity6 × 7 = 42
÷ or /Share equally or count equal groups42 ÷ 7 = 6

To translate a statement, identify the quantities, their units and which calculation is repeated. “Five less than 32” gives \(32-5\); “five times the sum of 32 and 7” gives \(5\times(32+7)\). “Share 32 equally among four groups” gives \(32\div4\). Order matters for subtraction and division.

For a quick comparison, look for common pieces before evaluating. If both expressions add 46, the one adding it to 73 is greater than the one adding it to 70. When subtraction has a common starting number, the expression that subtracts more gives less.

Write an expression before calculating

If two adults pay ₹55 each and a child pays ₹25, write \(2\times55+25\), not \(2\times(55+25)\). Only the adult fare is repeated. Then calculate the cost as ₹135.

3. Identify terms and carry their signs

To see the terms, rewrite subtraction as adding the opposite. Then split at top-level addition signs. A plus sign inside a bracket stays inside its group. A product or quotient stays together until evaluated. The signed terms of \(24-3\times5+8\) are \(24\), \(-(3\times5)\), and \(8\).

+24−(3 × 5)+8

Evaluate each term and then add: \(24+(-15)+8 = 9+8 = 17\). You can pair 24 and 8 first to get 32, then subtract 15. Both methods respect the sign of the middle term.

Signed-number reminder

Adding a negative value moves left on a number line. Subtracting a negative value is adding its opposite: \(8-(-3)=8+3=11\). When adding opposite signs, subtract the smaller magnitude from the larger and keep the sign of the larger magnitude. For products and quotients, like signs give a positive result and unlike signs give a negative result for nonzero operands. These multiplication and division sign rules are a supporting preview of the fuller integer work in Part II.

4. Order of operations and BODMAS

Without brackets, multiplication and division belong inside terms before the addition or subtraction between terms. BODMAS helps you remember the order. The letters D and M share one priority level, as do A and S.

BODMAS quick-reference chart
PriorityOperationsRule
1BracketsWork from the innermost group out.
2Orders (powers)The mnemonic includes powers. Powers are not a main calculation topic in this chapter.
3Division and multiplicationSame priority. Work left to right within the group.
4Addition and subtractionSame priority. Work left to right within the group.

Why the order matters

\(36\div6\times2=6\times2=12\). To make the product come first, write \(36\div(6\times2)=36\div12=3\). Likewise \(20-8+3=12+3=15\), while \(20-(8+3)=20-11=9\).

A fraction bar groups its numerator and its denominator. For example, \(\dfrac{18+6}{3+1}=\dfrac{24}{4}=6\). Using slash notation, write (18 + 6) / (3 + 1) to retain both groups. Dividing by zero has no defined value.

Check equal priority

5. Brackets and changing the grouping

Brackets tell the reader which calculation belongs together. In a nested expression, begin with the innermost brackets. A bare minus in front of an additive group reverses all signs in that group, because you are adding its opposite. A plus in front of the group preserves each sign.

How the position of brackets changes an expression
ExpressionFirst calculationValue
7 + 5 × 35 × 3 = 1522
(7 + 5) × 37 + 5 = 1236
35 − (9 + 6)9 + 6 = 1520
35 − (9 − 6)9 − 6 = 332
24 − [3 + (8 − 5)]8 − 5 = 318

Keep the meaning of the group

\(60-(20-7)=60-20+7=47\). In \(60\div(20-7)\), the bracket is the whole divisor. Work it out first; it cannot simply be removed. In a product such as \(6\times(20-7)\), you may evaluate the bracket first or distribute the 6 to both terms.

To obtain a target, try grouping a different pair but keep the original numbers and operation signs in order. With \(6+5\times4\), the usual value is 26. Moving the sum into a bracket makes \((6+5)\times4=44\).

6. Equivalent expressions and operation properties

Two numerical expressions are equivalent when they have the same value. You can check both sides by calculation or transform one into the other using a property. Carry every sign with its term when rearranging. Matching digits by appearance does not prove equivalence.

What each property allows
PropertyAddition or multiplicationSubtraction or division
Commutative · swap7 + 4 = 4 + 7; 7 × 4 = 4 × 77 − 4 ≠ 4 − 7; 12 ÷ 4 ≠ 4 ÷ 12
Associative · regroup(2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4); (2 × 3) × 4 = 2 × (3 × 4)Grouping can change the value; see the counterexamples below.
Distributive · multiply each term6 × (10 + 3) = 6 × 10 + 6 × 3; 6 × (10 − 3) = 6 × 10 − 6 × 3Works for multiplication across a bracketed sum or difference. It is not a general rule for division across the divisor.
Identity · leave unchanged14 + 0 = 14; 14 × 1 = 1414 − 0 = 14 and 14 ÷ 1 = 14 hold on the right. Reversing operands does not give the same general rule.

Subtracting and dividing are not generally associative. For example, \((30-12)-6=12\), but \(30-(12-6)=24\). Likewise \((48\div8)\div2=3\), but \(48\div(8\div2)=12\). Rewriting subtraction as addition of signed terms is what allows you to regroup those terms safely.

The distributive property works with a sum or a difference and with the common factor on either side of a product. You can reverse the rule to factor: \(9\times42+9\times8=9\times(42+8)=450\). A division may be split over a sum in the numerator with a shared nonzero divisor. Do not split a sum in the denominator: \(48\div(4+2)=8\), whereas \(48\div4+48\div2=36\).

7. Simplify, tinker and build expressions

Tinker with a sum

Move 2 from 27 to 48: \(48+27=50+25=75\). Opposite changes of the same size keep a sum constant.

Tinker with a difference

Shift both numbers by 3: \(76-37=79-40=39\). Equal changes preserve the difference.

Tinker with a product

If \(32\times15=480\), then \(34\times15=480+2\times15=510\). Increasing a factor adds copies of the other one.

Compare by structure

\(14\times(30+5)\) and \(14\times30+5\) share \(14\times30\); their remaining parts are 70 and 5, so the first is larger by 65.

Use arrays, classroom groups, repeated payments or a rectangular area to find what each term means. Two different groupings can describe the same collection. For instance, 4 rows of 7 counters and 4 rows of 3 counters make 4 rows of 10 when placed side by side.

Expression engineer

Use four 5s and the four operations as needed to build a target. Try 5, 10 and 20. For 5, use \(5+(5-5)\times5=5\). For 10 use \(5+5+5-5=10\); for 20 use \(5+5+5+5=20\). Decide in advance which digits, signs and brackets are allowed, then check each proposal against the rules.

    9. Step-by-step solved examples

    Try each question before opening the solution. The sets progress from 9 basic examples to 12 intermediate and 9 advanced examples.

    Basic · Examples 1–9

    Example 1 · A number, an expression and an equation

    Question: Classify 19, 12 + 7, and 12 + 7 = 19.

    1. Rule: An equals sign states equality; operations form an arithmetic expression.
    2. Work: 19 names a value. 12 + 7 tells what to calculate. The equality is true because 12 plus 7 gives 19.

    Final answer: Number; arithmetic expression; true equation.

    Example 2 · Write a shopping expression

    Question: A notebook costs ₹42 and an eraser costs ₹8. Write a total.

    1. Rule: Add amounts with the same unit.
    2. Work: Write 42 + 8. Count 8 more from 42 to get 50.

    Final answer: 42 + 8 = 50 rupees.

    Example 3 · Use a difference

    Question: A walker has covered 760 m of a 1,250 m circuit. How much remains?

    1. Rule: Subtract distance covered from total distance.
    2. Work: 1,250 − 760 = (1,250 − 750) − 10 = 500 − 10.

    Final answer: 490 m.

    Example 4 · Share equally

    Question: Divide 84 counters among six pupils.

    1. Rule: Division by the number of groups gives the size of each group.
    2. Work: 84 ÷ 6 = (60 + 24) ÷ 6 = 10 + 4. Check 14 × 6 = 84.

    Final answer: 14 counters each.

    Example 5 · Translate an ordered phrase

    Question: Write and evaluate “7 less than 20”.

    1. Rule: Less than names the amount to take away from the second quantity.
    2. Work: Start at 20, subtract 7, and arrive at 13.

    Final answer: 20 − 7 = 13.

    Example 6 · Split signed terms

    Question: List the terms of 28 − 3 × 4 + 9 and find its value.

    1. Rule: Replace subtraction by adding the opposite of the entire product.
    2. Work: The terms are 28, −(3 × 4), and 9. Their values are 28, −12, and 9. Add 28 + (−12) = 16, then add 9.

    Final answer: 25.

    Example 7 · Do a product first

    Question: Evaluate 11 + 5 × 6.

    1. Rule: Multiplication takes precedence over addition.
    2. Work: 5 × 6 = 30, so the expression becomes 11 + 30.

    Final answer: 41.

    Example 8 · Use brackets first

    Question: Evaluate (11 + 5) × 6.

    1. Rule: The bracket specifies a sum before multiplication.
    2. Work: 11 + 5 = 16. Multiply 16 × 6 = 16 × (5 + 1) = 80 + 16.

    Final answer: 96.

    Example 9 · Compare without totals

    Question: Compare 152 + 97 with 152 + 101.

    1. Rule: With a common addend, compare the other addends.
    2. Work: The second expression adds 4 more. Therefore it is greater by 4.

    Final answer: 152 + 97 < 152 + 101.

    Intermediate · Examples 10–21

    Example 10 · Handle division and multiplication

    Question: Evaluate 48 ÷ 6 × 2.

    1. Rule: Equal precedence: work left to right.
    2. Work: Divide 48 ÷ 6 = 8 first. Then multiply 8 × 2. Multiplying 6 × 2 first would change the grouping.

    Final answer: 16.

    Example 11 · Handle subtraction and addition

    Question: Evaluate 33 − 12 + 5.

    1. Rule: Subtract and add from left to right, or add signed terms.
    2. Work: 33 − 12 = 21; 21 + 5 = 26. Equivalently, combine −12 and +5 to get −7.

    Final answer: 26.

    Example 12 · Keep the signs attached

    Question: Simplify −13 + 24 − 7.

    1. Rule: Take every signed term with it when regrouping.
    2. Work: Rewrite as (−13) + 24 + (−7). Group the two negative terms to make −20. Add 24.

    Final answer: 4.

    Example 13 · Remove plus brackets

    Question: Simplify 19 + (12 − 8).

    1. Rule: A plus in front keeps the signs inside.
    2. Work: 19 + 12 − 8 = 31 − 8 = 23. Alternatively, the bracket gives 4 first.

    Final answer: 23.

    Example 14 · Remove minus brackets

    Question: Simplify 64 − (27 − 9).

    1. Rule: A minus in front changes both signs.
    2. Work: 64 − 27 + 9 = 37 + 9 = 46. Check by doing 27 − 9 = 18 first.

    Final answer: 46.

    Example 15 · Work inside nested brackets

    Question: Evaluate 75 − [10 + (8 − 5) × 4].

    1. Rule: Start with the innermost grouping.
    2. Work: 8 − 5 = 3, then 3 × 4 = 12. The square bracket is 10 + 12 = 22. Subtract 22 from 75.

    Final answer: 53.

    Example 16 · Make a target

    Question: Add brackets to 9 + 3 × 4 to make 48.

    1. Rule: Change which quantities are multiplied.
    2. Work: Without brackets the result is 9 + 12 = 21. Group 9 + 3 to get 12, then multiply by 4.

    Final answer: (9 + 3) × 4 = 48.

    Example 17 · Distribute over addition

    Question: Evaluate 18 × (20 + 3) mentally.

    1. Rule: Multiply each term in the bracket by 18.
    2. Work: 18 × 20 = 360; 18 × 3 = 54; add them.

    Final answer: 414.

    Example 18 · Distribute over subtraction

    Question: Calculate 47 × 98 without long multiplication.

    1. Rule: Use 98 = 100 − 2.
    2. Work: 47 × 100 − 47 × 2 = 4,700 − 94 = 4,606.

    Final answer: 4,606.

    Example 19 · Factor a common multiplier

    Question: Evaluate 7 × 38 + 7 × 12.

    1. Rule: Reverse distribution.
    2. Work: Each addend has factor 7, so take 7 × (38 + 12), then 7 × 50.

    Final answer: 350.

    Example 20 · Choose useful factor groups

    Question: Simplify 25 × 9 × 4.

    1. Rule: Multiplication is commutative and associative.
    2. Work: Reorder to (25 × 4) × 9. The first product is 100.

    Final answer: 900.

    Example 21 · Check an identity

    Question: What numbers fill 53 + □ = 53 and 53 × □ = 53?

    1. Rule: The additive identity is 0 and the multiplicative identity is 1.
    2. Work: Adding zero changes no amount. Multiplying by one gives one copy of 53.

    Final answer: 0 and 1, respectively.

    Advanced and competency based · Examples 22–30

    Example 22 · Model an arrangement

    Question: There are 8 rows of 6 chairs and 7 loose chairs. Another room has 8 rows of 7 chairs. Which room has more?

    1. Rule: Build the expressions from the quantities in each arrangement.
    2. Work: First room: 8 × 6 + 7 = 48 + 7 = 55. Second: 8 × 7 = 56. Their difference is 56 − 55.

    Final answer: The second room has 1 more chair.

    Example 23 · Calculate change

    Question: Three markers at ₹18 each and a ₹35 folder are paid for with ₹100. Find the change.

    1. Rule: Subtract the entire bill, using a bracket.
    2. Work: 100 − (3 × 18 + 35) = 100 − (54 + 35) = 100 − 89.

    Final answer: ₹11.

    Example 24 · Use a fixed discount

    Question: A club buys four kits at ₹125 and receives ₹45 off the whole order. It then pays ₹30 delivery. Find the final payment.

    1. Rule: Apply the discount once, then add delivery.
    2. Work: 4 × 125 − 45 + 30 = 500 − 45 + 30 = 455 + 30.

    Final answer: ₹485.

    Example 25 · Tinker with a sum and difference

    Question: Compare 58 + 47 with 60 + 45, and compare 58 − 47 with 60 − 49.

    1. Rule: Opposite changes preserve a sum; equal changes preserve a difference.
    2. Work: First pair: transfer 2 from 47 to 58, keeping 105. Second pair: add 2 to both numbers; each difference is 11.

    Final answer: Both pairs are equal.

    Example 26 · Tinker with a product

    Question: Given 24 × 35 = 840, find 27 × 35 without multiplying from scratch.

    1. Rule: The first factor increased by 3, so add three additional copies of 35.
    2. Work: 27 × 35 = (24 + 3) × 35 = 840 + 105.

    Final answer: 945.

    Example 27 · Interpret distance and time

    Question: A shuttle goes 18 km from town to school and returns along the same route. It makes three round trips, then travels 7 km to refuel. Find the total distance.

    1. Rule: The pair of 18 km legs is repeated three times.
    2. Work: 3 × (18 + 18) + 7 = 3 × 36 + 7 = 108 + 7.

    Final answer: 115 km.

    Example 28 · Track money with signed terms

    Question: A balance is ₹120. Three payments of ₹52 and a refund of ₹25 follow. Find the arithmetic balance.

    1. Rule: Treat money leaving as negative and a refund as positive.
    2. Work: 120 − 3 × 52 + 25 = 120 − 156 + 25 = −36 + 25.

    Final answer: −₹11, an ₹11 shortfall.

    Example 29 · Compare structures before calculating

    Question: Which is larger: 6 × (22 + 5) or 6 × 22 + 5? By how much?

    1. Rule: Distribute and cancel the shared product.
    2. Work: The first is 6 × 22 + 30; the second is 6 × 22 + 5. Compare 30 and 5 after cancelling the shared term.

    Final answer: The first is larger by 25.

    Example 30 · Design an expression

    Question: Use 2, 5, 6 and 9 once each to make 16 using operations and brackets.

    1. Rule: Build a factor 8 so that multiplication by the remaining 2 gives 16.
    2. Work: From 9 and 5 form 14, then subtract 6 to get 8. Group that calculation before multiplying by 2. Without the group the product 6 × 2 would occur first.

    Final answer: (9 + 5 − 6) × 2 = 16.

    11. Quick revision and exam preparation

    Read → Translate

    Identify quantities, operations and units. Use brackets when an entire quantity repeats or is taken away.

    Split → Evaluate

    Rewrite subtraction as adding an opposite. Start inside brackets, then do × and ÷, then + and −. Equal-priority operations run left to right.

    Rewrite → Compare

    Reorder signed addends, regroup products, or distribute across all terms. Compare any parts left after shared terms cancel.

    Check → Explain

    Use the answer’s size, units, sign and context. Check with a second equivalent expression when practical.

    Assessment checklist

    1. Read the word order carefully, especially “less than” and “divide by”.
    2. Write one valid equality per line. Do not use “=” between unequal intermediate values.
    3. Check every sign when a minus precedes a bracket. Keep all factors when distributing.
    4. In assertion–reason questions, test the two statements separately before checking whether the reason explains the assertion.
    5. Review the BODMAS chart, property table and worked examples.
