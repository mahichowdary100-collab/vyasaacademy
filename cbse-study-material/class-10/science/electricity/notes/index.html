1. Introduction to Electricity

Electric Charge

Electric charge is the property of matter that makes it experience or produce electric force. The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C). The charge of one electron is \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}\), so one coulomb contains about \(6.25 \times 10^{18}\) electrons.

When charges flow through a conductor we get an electric current. In a metal wire, the free (mobile) electrons carry the charge. Their drift from the negative terminal to the positive terminal of a cell is what we call current inside the wire.

Electric circuit: a continuous, closed path made of conducting wires and devices such as cells, switches and bulbs, along which electric current flows. If the path is broken anywhere (switch open, or a loose wire), the current stops — this is an open circuit. When the path is complete, current flows — a closed circuit.

Conventional direction of current: by convention, current is taken to flow from the positive terminal of the cell, through the external circuit, to the negative terminal. This is opposite to the direction of electron flow (electrons actually move from negative to positive). The convention was fixed before electrons were discovered, and it is still used while drawing circuit diagrams.

Electric Current — Definition and Unit

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-section of a conductor.

\[I = \frac{Q}{t}\]

where \(I\) = electric current, \(Q\) = net charge flowing, \(t\) = time taken.

SI unit: ampere (A). \(1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{C/s}\). A current of 1 A means 1 coulomb of charge flows past a point every second. Smaller units are often used: \(1\ \text{mA} = 10^{-3}\ \text{A}\) and \(1\ \text{μA} = 10^{-6}\ \text{A}\).

Examples:

  • If 2 C of charge flows through a wire in 4 s, then \(I = 2/4 = 0.5\ \text{A}\).
  • A 0.2 A current for 10 s carries \(Q = I t = 0.2 \times 10 = 2\ \text{C}\) of charge.

Every simple circuit needs a source of energy (cell or battery), conducting wires, a control device (switch or key) and a load (such as a bulb or resistor).

2. Electric Potential and Potential Difference

Electric Potential

The electric potential of a point in an electric field is the work done in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point. A cell raises the potential of the charge at its positive terminal relative to its negative terminal, giving the charges electric potential energy.

Potential difference: the difference in electric potential between two points in a circuit. It is the work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to the other. A cell (or battery) creates a potential difference across a circuit, and charges flow because of this difference — from higher to lower potential outside the cell. The potential difference across the two terminals of a cell is also called the voltage of the cell.

Potential Difference — Formula and Unit

\[V = \frac{W}{Q}\]

where \(V\) = potential difference, \(W\) = work done in moving charge \(Q\) between the two points.

SI unit: volt (V). \(1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J/C}\).

The potential difference between two points is 1 volt when 1 joule of work is done in moving 1 coulomb of charge from one point to the other.

Examples:

  • If 5 J of work is done in moving 2 C of charge between two points, the potential difference is \(V = 5/2 = 2.5\ \text{V}\).
  • A 12 V battery supplies 12 J of energy to every coulomb of charge that passes through it.

A battery is a combination of one or more cells. The potential difference of a cell is fixed by the chemical reactions inside it (for example, a common dry cell gives about 1.5 V). The higher the potential difference, the more work is available to push charge around the circuit, so a bulb connected to a higher-voltage source gets more energy per coulomb.

Common Confusion

Charge does not get "used up" in a circuit. The current is the same throughout a series circuit; what changes along the wire is the potential (energy per unit charge), which drops as charges pass through the load.

3. Electric Circuit and Its Components

A simple electric circuit consists of a cell (or battery), a switch, connecting wires, a load such as a resistor (or bulb), and two meters commonly used in experiments — an ammeter and a voltmeter. The next three points tell you exactly how each meter must be connected.

Connecting the Meters

Ammeter: connected in series with the component, so that the same current flowing through the circuit also passes through the ammeter. It has low resistance.

Voltmeter: connected in parallel (across the component), so that it measures the potential difference between the two ends of the component. It has very high resistance, so it draws only a negligible current.

In Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 notice the two simple rules again: the ammeter is in series (current enters and leaves the ammeter on the same line) and the voltmeter is in parallel (it forms a separate branch across the component). Reversing these connections gives wrong readings and can even damage the meters.

4. Electric Current

Conventional Current vs Electron Flow

Conventional current is taken to flow from the positive terminal to the negative terminal, outside the cell (that is, through the external circuit).

Electron flow is the actual motion of free electrons, which drift from the negative terminal to the positive terminal in the external circuit — opposite to the conventional direction. Conventionally we still use the direction of conventional current while labelling arrowheads in circuit diagrams.

Charge and current relationship: if a net charge \(Q\) flows in time \(t\), the current is \(I = Q/t\), and a steady current \(I\) in time \(t\) carries charge \(Q = It\). If each electron carries \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}\), the number of electrons that flow when charge \(Q\) passes is

\[n = \frac{Q}{e}\]

  • Example: charge \(Q = 3\ \text{C}\) flowing in \(1.5\ \text{s}\) gives \(I = 3/1.5 = 2\ \text{A}\).
  • Example: the number of electrons crossing a point each second for a 1 A current is \(n = 1/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) \approx 6.25 \times 10^{18}\).

Current in series circuits: in a series circuit the same current flows through every component, because there is only one path. If the current entering the circuit is \(I\), the same \(I\) flows through each resistor. This is why an ammeter placed anywhere in series reads the same value.

Measuring Current with an Ammeter

An ammeter is a device that measures current. It is connected in series with the circuit component whose current we want; the meter’s reading is the current flowing through it (and hence through the series circuit). An ideal ammeter has zero resistance so that it does not disturb the circuit. A milliammeter measures small currents (in mA).

Never Do This

Never connect an ammeter across a component (in parallel) — its low resistance would draw a very large current and damage both the ammeter and the circuit.

5. Ohm’s Law

Ohm’s Law

According to Ohm’s law, the potential difference across the ends of a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the current flowing through it, provided the physical conditions (such as temperature) remain constant.

\[V \quad \propto \quad I\]

\[V = I R\]

where the constant of proportionality \(R\) is the resistance of the conductor.

Meaning of resistance: resistance is the property of a conductor that opposes the flow of current. The larger the resistance, the smaller the current for a given potential difference. By rearranging \(V = IR\),

\[R = \frac{V}{I}\]

SI unit: ohm (Ω). \(1\ \text{Ω} = 1\ \text{V/A}\). A conductor has a resistance of 1 ohm if a potential difference of 1 V across it produces a current of 1 A.

Varying the rheostat changes the current. For each setting, the voltmeter records \(V\) and the ammeter records \(I\). For an ohmic (metallic) conductor at constant temperature, the ratio \(V/I\) stays the same, and the V–I graph is a straight line passing through the origin, as shown below. The slope of this line gives the resistance.

Some conductors (for example a filament lamp as its temperature rises, or a semiconductor diode) do not follow Ohm’s law; their V–I graph is not a straight line. Such devices are called non-ohmic conductors.

  • Example: a current of 2 A flows through a 5 Ω resistor, so \(V = I R = 2 \times 5 = 10\ \text{V}\).
  • Example: if 12 V across a conductor gives 3 A, then \(R = 12/3 = 4\ \text{Ω}\).

6. Resistance

Resistance

Resistance is the property of a conductor that opposes the flow of electric current through it. From Ohm’s law, \(R = V/I\). The SI unit of resistance is the ohm (Ω); \(1\ \text{Ω} = 1\ \text{V/A}\).

Factors on which the Resistance of a Conductor Depends

For a wire of length \(l\) and uniform cross-sectional area \(A\), the resistance depends on four factors:

  • Length \(l\): at constant temperature, resistance is directly proportional to length — \(R \propto l\). A longer wire offers more resistance because the charges to collide with atoms along a longer path.
  • Cross-sectional area \(A\): resistance is inversely proportional to the area of cross-section — \(R \propto 1/A\). A thicker wire offers less resistance because there are more free electrons available.
  • Nature of material: for the same length and thickness, different materials have different resistances; this is described by their resistivity \(\rho\).
  • Temperature: for most metallic conductors the resistance increases as temperature rises (due to more vigorous vibration of atoms). For alloys like nichrome and manganin, the change of resistance with temperature is very small.
Combining the Factors

\[R = \frac{\rho l}{A}\]

where \(R\) = resistance of the wire, \(l\) = length of wire, \(A\) = area of cross-section, and \(\rho\) = resistivity of the material (a constant for a given material at a given temperature).

Worked reference: a copper wire of length \(l = 10\ \text{m}\) and area \(A = 1\ \text{mm}^2 = 1 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\) has \(R = (1.62 \times 10^{-8})(10)/(1 \times 10^{-6}) = 0.162\ \text{Ω}\). The same dimensions in nichrome give about \(1.0 \times 10^{-6}(10)/(1 \times 10^{-6}) = 10\ \text{Ω}\) — over sixty times larger, which is why nichrome is used for heating coils.

Unit Conversions You Must Know

\(1\ \text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\); \(1\ \text{cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\); \(1\ \text{m}^2 = 10^4\ \text{cm}^2\). Convert length to metres and area to square metres before substituting in \(R = \rho l/A\).

  • Example: doubling the length of a wire doubles its resistance (if the area is unchanged).
  • Example: doubling the diameter doubles the radius, so the area becomes 4 times and the resistance becomes \(\frac{1}{4}\) of the original.

7. Resistivity

Resistivity

The resistivity of a material is the resistance of a wire of that material of unit length and unit cross-sectional area. It is a characteristic property of the material, independent of the dimensions (length and thickness) of the conductor.

\[\rho = \frac{R A}{l}\]

SI unit: ohm-metre (Ω m). It depends only on the nature of the material (and its temperature).

Notice the difference: the resistance of a wire changes with its length and thickness, but resistivity of the material stays the same for the same material at the same temperature.

8. Resistors in Series

When resistors are connected one after the other so that the same current passes through each, they are said to be in series.

Equivalent Resistance in Series

\[R = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\]

The equivalent resistance of a series combination is the sum of the individual resistances. It is always greater than the largest individual resistance.

In a series circuit:

  • The same current flows through every resistor (one path only).
  • The potential difference divides among the resistors — \(V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3\), where each \(V_i = I R_i\).
  • The total potential difference of the source equals the sum of the drops across the resistors.

Examples:

  • Resistors of 5 Ω, 10 Ω and 15 Ω in series give \(R = 5 + 10 + 15 = 30\ \text{Ω}\).
  • A battery of 12 V joined to 4 Ω and 8 Ω in series gives \(R = 12\ \text{Ω}\) and circuit current \(I = 12/12 = 1\ \text{A}\). The drops are \(1 \times 4 = 4\ \text{V}\) and \(1 \times 8 = 8\ \text{V}\), adding to 12 V.

Disadvantage in household wiring: if any appliance in a series circuit fails or is switched off, the circuit breaks and every other appliance stops working. That is why household circuits use parallel wiring instead (section 10).

9. Resistors in Parallel

When two or more resistors are connected between the same two points, each providing an independent path for the current, they are said to be in parallel.

Equivalent Resistance in Parallel

\[\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}\]

For exactly two resistors in parallel:

\[R = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\]

The equivalent resistance of a parallel combination is always less than the smallest individual resistance.

In a parallel circuit:

  • The same potential difference exists across every branch (each branch is joined to the same two points).
  • The current divides among the branches — \(I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3\), with a larger current in the branch of smaller resistance.
  • Adding a resistor in parallel gives the current an extra path, so the total resistance of the combination decreases.

Examples:

  • Two resistors of 2 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel give \(R = (2 \times 3)/(2 + 3) = 6/5 = 1.2\ \text{Ω}\), which is smaller than both 2 Ω and 3 Ω.
  • Two 12 Ω resistors in parallel give \(R = 144/24 = 6\ \text{Ω}\). For \(n\) equal resistors \(R\) each, the parallel combination gives \(R/n\).

Advantage in household wiring: every appliance receives the full mains voltage and works independently; switching one appliance on or off does not affect the others.

11. Heating Effect of Electric Current

Heating Effect

When current flows through a conductor, the conductor becomes hot. This happens because the mobile electrons collide with the vibrating atoms of the conductor and transfer energy to them; the conductor heats up. This conversion of electrical energy into heat is called the heating effect of electric current (Joule heating).

Heat Produced — Joule’s Law of Heating

\[H = I^2 R t\]

Equivalent forms (using \(V = IR\)):

\[H = V I t\]

\[H = \frac{V^2 t}{R}\]

where \(H\) = heat produced (joule, J), \(I\) = current (ampere, A), \(R\) = resistance (ohm, Ω), \(V\) = potential difference (volt, V), \(t\) = time (second, s).

Notice the heat is proportional to the square of the current — doubling the current makes the heat four times larger. The formula \(I^2 R t\) works for any circuit; the forms \(VIt\) and \(V^2 t/R\) follow by substituting Ohm’s law.

  • Example: a current of 2 A through a 5 Ω resistor for 60 s produces \(H = 4 \times 5 \times 60 = 1200\ \text{J}\).
  • Example: an electric iron draws 5 A at 220 V for 2 minutes (120 s), giving \(H = 220 \times 5 \times 120 = 132{,}000\ \text{J}\).
  • Example: across a 6 Ω coil with 12 V applied for 5 minutes (300 s), \(H = (144/6) \times 300 = 7200\ \text{J}\).

Why Heating Elements Use Nichrome

Heating elements are made from materials with high resistivity (so that a short wire can give the required resistance and heat), a high melting point (so the element does not melt while red-hot), and the ability to withstand high temperature without oxidising. Nichrome is used for heating elements because it has all these properties. (Details of exact alloy composition are not specified in the Class 10 syllabus.)

Electric Fuse

A fuse is a safety device consisting of a short thin wire of a material having high resistance and a low melting point. If the current exceeds a safe value, the wire heats up to its melting point and melts, breaking the circuit and protecting the appliance and wiring from damage. The fuse is always connected in series with the live wire of the appliance.

The rating of a fuse (for example, 5 A, 10 A) is the maximum safe current it can pass. The fuse must have a rating slightly above the normal current drawn by the appliance, so that it does not melt during normal use but melts during a fault.

13. Electric Power

Electric Power

Electric power is the rate at which electrical energy is consumed (or work is done) in a circuit.

\[P = \frac{W}{t}\]

For electric circuits, using \(V = W/Q\) and \(I = Q/t\):

\[P = V I\]

Using Ohm’s law \(V = IR\) we also get:

\[P = I^2 R\]

\[P = \frac{V^2}{R}\]

Units of Power

SI unit: watt (W). \(1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s} = 1\ \text{V A}\).

Kilowatt (kW): \(1\ \text{kW} = 1000\ \text{W}\).

A 100 W bulb consumes 100 joules of electrical energy every second. Appliance power ratings (such as 60 W bulb, 1000 W iron) tell us the rate at which each appliance consumes energy.

How to choose the right power formula:

  • Use \(P = VI\) when \(V\) and \(I\) are known (or easy to find).
  • Use \(P = I^2 R\) when \(I\) and \(R\) are known (for example, a resistor carrying a known current).
  • Use \(P = V^2/R\) when \(V\) and \(R\) are known (for example, an electric bulb rated in volts and watts).
  • Example: a bulb draws 0.5 A at 220 V, so \(P = 220 \times 0.5 = 110\ \text{W}\).
  • Example: a 2 kW heater on a 220 V supply draws \(I = P/V = 2000/220 \approx 9.1\ \text{A}\).
  • Example: a device rated 100 W operating at 220 V has resistance \(R = V^2/P = 220^2/100 = 484\ \text{Ω}\).

14. Electrical Energy and the Kilowatt-Hour

Electrical energy is the total work (or heat) done by the current over a period of time. It is power multiplied by time in which the power is used:

Electrical Energy

\[E = P t\]

where \(E\) = electrical energy consumed, \(P\) = power of the appliance, \(t\) = time it is used.

Commercial unit: The joule is a very small unit for household consumption, so electricity boards measure energy in the kilowatt-hour (kWh), also written as one "unit".

\[1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{J}\]

15. Electricity Bills and Energy Consumption

To find the energy consumed by appliances, we convert the power of each appliance to kilowatts, multiply by the time used (in hours), and add the kilowatt-hours of all appliances. The reading of an electricity meter (difference between two readings) gives the units consumed for billing.

Steps to Solve a Bill or Consumption Problem

1. List each appliance with its power (W) and daily hours of use.

2. Convert each power to kW by dividing by 1000.

3. Find energy used in kWh = kW × hours.

4. Add all appliances to get total kWh (units) per day, then scale to the required period (for example × 30 days).

5. Multiply the total kWh by the tariff (rate per unit) to get the bill.

Worked example: A household uses a 1000 W iron for 2 h a day, four 60 W bulbs for 5 h a day, and a 40 W fan for 8 h a day. Energy per day:

  • Iron: \(1000\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{kW}\), energy = \(1 \times 2 = 2\ \text{kWh}\).
  • Bulbs: \(4 \times 60\ \text{W} = 240\ \text{W} = 0.24\ \text{kW}\), energy = \(0.24 \times 5 = 1.2\ \text{kWh}\).
  • Fan: \(40\ \text{W} = 0.04\ \text{kW}\), energy = \(0.04 \times 8 = 0.32\ \text{kWh}\).
  • Total per day = \(2 + 1.2 + 0.32 = 3.52\ \text{kWh}\).

For 30 days the total is \(3.52 \times 30 = 105.6\ \text{kWh}\). At a hypothetical tariff of ₹6 per unit, the bill is \(105.6 \times 6 =\) ₹633.6. (Real tariffs vary by state and slab; any tariff used in an exam or worksheet is always given in the question.)

18. Solved Numerical Examples

Attempt each problem on your own first, then check the working. Always write the Given, the Formula, then substitute with units, and end with a boxed final answer with its unit.

Electric Current and Charge

Example 1

A charge of 2 C flows through a wire in 4 s. Calculate the electric current.

Given: \(Q = 2\ \text{C}\), \(t = 4\ \text{s}\).

Formula: \(I = Q/t\).

Solution: \(I = 2/4 = 0.5\ \text{A}\).

Answer: 0.5 A (in the direction of conventional current).

Example 2

A current of 2 A flows through a conductor for 10 s. Find the charge that flows past a point in the conductor.

Given: \(I = 2\ \text{A}\), \(t = 10\ \text{s}\).

Formula: \(Q = I t\).

Solution: \(Q = 2 \times 10 = 20\ \text{C}\).

Answer: 20 C.

Example 3

90 C of charge flows through a bulb in one minute. What is the current?

Given: \(Q = 90\ \text{C}\), \(t = 60\ \text{s}\).

Formula: \(I = Q/t\).

Solution: \(I = 90/60 = 1.5\ \text{A}\).

Answer: 1.5 A.

Example 4

An ammeter reads 1.5 A. How many electrons cross a given cross-section of the wire each second?

Given: \(I = 1.5\ \text{A}\), \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}\), \(t = 1\ \text{s}\).

Formula: \(Q = I t = n e\), so \(n = I t/e\).

Solution: \(n = 1.5/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 9.375 \times 10^{18}\).

Answer: about \(9.4 \times 10^{18}\) electrons per second.

Example 5

\(6.25 \times 10^{18}\) electrons pass a point in 2 s. Find the current.

Given: \(n = 6.25 \times 10^{18}\), \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}\), \(t = 2\ \text{s}\).

Formula: \(Q = n e\), \(I = Q/t\).

Solution: \(Q = 6.25 \times 10^{18} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 1\ \text{C}\). Then \(I = 1/2 = 0.5\ \text{A}\).

Answer: 0.5 A.

Example 18

Three resistors of 5 Ω, 10 Ω and 15 Ω are joined in series. Find the equivalent resistance.

Given: \(R_1 = 5\), \(R_2 = 10\), \(R_3 = 15\ \text{Ω}\).

Formula: \(R = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\).

Solution: \(R = 5 + 10 + 15 = 30\ \text{Ω}\).

Answer: 30 Ω.

Example 19

Resistors of 4 Ω and 6 Ω are in series across a 20 V battery. Find the equivalent resistance, the circuit current, and the potential difference across each resistor.

Given: \(R_1 = 4\), \(R_2 = 6\ \text{Ω}\), \(V = 20\ \text{V}\).

Formula: \(R = R_1 + R_2\); \(I = V/R\); \(V_i = I R_i\).

Solution: \(R = 10\ \text{Ω}\). \(I = 20/10 = 2\ \text{A}\). \(V_1 = 2 \times 4 = 8\ \text{V}\), \(V_2 = 2 \times 6 = 12\ \text{V}\). Check: \(8 + 12 = 20\ \text{V}\).

Answer: R = 10 Ω, I = 2 A, 8 V across 4 Ω and 12 V across 6 Ω.

Example 20

Resistors of 3 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent resistance.

Given: \(R_1 = 3\), \(R_2 = 6\ \text{Ω}\).

Formula: \(R = R_1 R_2/(R_1 + R_2)\).

Solution: \(R = (3 \times 6)/(3 + 6) = 18/9 = 2\ \text{Ω}\).

Answer: 2 Ω (less than both 3 Ω and 6 Ω).

Example 21

Two resistors each of 20 Ω are joined in parallel. What is the equivalent resistance?

Given: \(R_1 = R_2 = 20\ \text{Ω}\).

Formula: \(R = R/n\) for n equal resistors in parallel.

Solution: \(R = 20/2 = 10\ \text{Ω}\).

Answer: 10 Ω.

Example 22

Resistors of 4 Ω, 12 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in parallel. Find their combined resistance.

Given: \(R_1 = 4\), \(R_2 = 12\), \(R_3 = 6\ \text{Ω}\).

Solution: \(1/R = 1/4 + 1/12 + 1/6 = 3/12 + 1/12 + 2/12 = 6/12 = 1/2\), so \(R = 2\ \text{Ω}\).

Answer: 2 Ω.

An electric iron draws 5 A at 220 V for 2 minutes. Find the heat produced.

12 V is applied across a 6 Ω heating coil for 5 minutes. Find the heat generated.

A resistor carrying 2 A produces 1000 J of heat in 50 s. Find its resistance.

19. Application-Based Examples

These questions connect the formulas to real appliances and house wiring. Explain the physics, then compute.

Q1.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

Why are household electric circuits wired in parallel and not in series?

Answer:

In parallel, each appliance gets the full mains voltage, works independently, and can be switched on or off without disturbing others. In series, all appliances share one current and if one fails, the whole circuit (and every appliance) stops working. Parallel wiring also allows each branch to be fused separately.

Q2.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

An electric heater becomes red-hot while the wires that carry the same current to it stay cool. Why?

Answer:

The heat produced is \(H = I^2Rt\). The same current flows through the wire and the element, but the heating element has a much higher resistance than the connecting wire (it is made of a high-resistivity material), so it produces far more heat. Also the wires are kept short and thick (low resistance).

Q3.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A circuit protected by a 5 A fuse carries a 1000 W electric iron at 220 V. Is the fuse suitable? What happens if a second 1000 W iron is switched on?

Answer:

Current of one iron \(= 1000/220 \approx 4.5\ \text{A}\), below 5 A, so one iron is fine. With two irons the current is about 9.1 A, exceeds 5 A, and the fuse wire melts, breaking the circuit to protect the wiring.

Q4.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A 60 W bulb and a 100 W bulb (both rated for 220 V) are connected in series to a 220 V supply. Which one glows brighter? Explain.

Answer:

Series means the same current flows through both. The bulb with higher resistance glows brighter (\(P = I^2R\)). From \(R = V^2/P\), the 60 W bulb has higher resistance (about 807 Ω vs 484 Ω), so the 60 W bulb glows brighter in series, even though normally (at rated voltage) the 100 W bulb would be brighter.

Q5.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

Why are heating coils made of nichrome instead of copper?

Answer:

Nichrome has high resistivity, so a short coil gives enough resistance (and hence heat, \(H = I^2Rt\)) without a very long wire. It also has a high melting point and does not readily oxidise at the high working temperature of a heater. Copper, being a good conductor, has too low a resistance to heat up enough.

Q6.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A shop replaces three 100 W bulbs (used 6 h a day) with three 20 W LED bulbs. Find the energy saved per day.

Answer:

Old load \(= 300\ \text{W} = 0.3\ \text{kW}\), energy \(= 0.3 \times 6 = 1.8\ \text{kWh/day}\). LED load \(= 60\ \text{W} = 0.06\ \text{kW}\), energy \(= 0.06 \times 6 = 0.36\ \text{kWh/day}\). Saved \(= 1.8 - 0.36 = 1.44\ \text{kWh/day}\), i.e. about 43 kWh per month.

Q7.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

In an old-style series string of decorative lights each bulb is rated for only part of the mains voltage. Why is the string wired in series, and what is the drawback?

Answer:

In series the mains voltage divides equally among the bulbs, so each bulb can be low-voltage and small. The drawback is that a single burnt-out bulb breaks the series circuit and the whole string goes dark (until that bulb is replaced).

Q8.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

An electric iron is rated 1000 W at 220 V. If the voltage of the supply dips to 200 V, what happens to the heat produced?

Answer:

The resistance of the element is fixed, so \(P = V^2/R\). At 200 V the power becomes \(P = 1000 \times (200/220)^2 \approx 826\ \text{W}\). The iron gets hotter more slowly (less heat per second) when the supply voltage falls.

20. Competency and Reasoning Questions

C1.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

Two ohmic conductors A and B are tested. On a V–I graph, the line for A is steeper than the line for B. Which conductor has the larger resistance?

Answer:

Resistance is the slope \(V/I\) of the V–I graph. The steeper line (A) has the larger resistance, because a given change in current needs a larger change in potential difference.

C2.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

You must halve the resistance of a fixed metal wire without changing its material or its temperature. Which single change is correct: (a) double its length, (b) halve its cross-sectional area, (c) halve its length, (d) double its area?

Answer:

(c) halve its length. Since \(R = \rho l/A\) and \(\rho\) is fixed, halving \(l\) halves \(R\). Doubling the area would also halve \(R\), but that option was not listed alone; among the given choices, (c) is correct.

C3.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

Two identical bulbs can be joined to a battery either in series or in parallel. In which arrangement is the total power (and hence the total light) greater?

Answer:

Parallel. Each bulb gets the full battery voltage, so each dissipates \(P = V^2/R\); total power is \(2V^2/R\). In series each bulb gets \(V/2\), giving total power \(V^2/(2R)\) — four times smaller.

C4.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A cell is connected to a bulb and a rheostat in series. The rheostat resistance is gradually increased. What happens to the ammeter reading and the brightness of the bulb?

Answer:

Total resistance increases, so the current (\(I = V/R\)) decreases; the ammeter reading falls and the bulb glows dimmer, because less power \(I^2R_{\text{bulb}}\) is available to it.

C5.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A fixed resistor is connected to a battery whose voltage is held constant. The resistor value is halved. How does the heat produced per second change?

Answer:

Voltage is fixed, so use \(P = V^2/R\). Halving \(R\) doubles \(P\), so the heat produced per second doubles.

C6.

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

In a home, many appliances are switched on at once and the fuse of a circuit blows. Explain the sequence of events with physics.

Answer:

Switching on more appliances in parallel raises the total current drawn (\(I = P/V\), with each appliance adding to the load). When this current exceeds the fuse rating, the fuse wire heats beyond its melting point (\(H = I^2Rt\)) and melts, opening the circuit and cutting off the current, protecting the wiring from overheating and fire.

21. Case-Based Learning

Read each case, answer the questions step by step, and check the working. All tariffs are hypothetical.

Case Study 1

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A family uses 4 LED bulbs (each 20 W) for 6 h, a 1000 W iron for 1 h, a 150 W television for 3 h and a 500 W water pump for 1 h every day.

Q1. Find the total electrical energy consumed in a day (kWh).

Answer:

LEDs: \(4 \times 20\ \text{W} = 80\ \text{W} = 0.08\ \text{kW}\), energy \(= 0.08 \times 6 = 0.48\ \text{kWh}\). Iron: \(1.0 \times 1 = 1.0\ \text{kWh}\). TV: \(0.15 \times 3 = 0.45\ \text{kWh}\). Pump: \(0.5 \times 1 = 0.5\ \text{kWh}\). Total \(= 0.48 + 1 + 0.45 + 0.5 = 2.43\ \text{kWh/day}\).

Q2. If the tariff is ₹6 per unit (hypothetical), find the bill for 30 days.

Answer:

Monthly energy \(= 2.43 \times 30 = 72.9\ \text{kWh}\). Bill \(= 72.9 \times 6 =\) ₹437.4.

Q3. Which appliance consumes the most energy in a day and why?

Answer:

The iron (1 kWh), because although used for only 1 h, its power is much larger (1000 W) than the other loads.

Q4. By how much does the monthly bill fall if the same lights are used for only 4 h each day?

Answer:

LEDs save \(0.08 \times 2 = 0.16\ \text{kWh/day}\), i.e. \(0.16 \times 30 = 4.8\ \text{kWh}\) per month. Saving \(= 4.8 \times 6 =\) ₹28.8 per month.

Case Study 2

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A household drawing from a 220 V supply has four 100 W bulbs, two 80 W fans and one 1000 W heater. The mains circuit carries a 5 A fuse.

Q1. Calculate the current drawn when all appliances run together.

Answer:

Total power \(= (4 \times 100) + (2 \times 80) + 1000 = 400 + 160 + 1000 = 1560\ \text{W}\). Current \(= 1560/220 \approx 7.1\ \text{A}\).

Q2. Will the 5 A fuse blow? Suggest a suitable fuse rating.

Answer:

Yes — 7.1 A exceeds 5 A, so the 5 A fuse melts. A fuse rated about 10 A would be suitable, since it is above the normal load but still protects the wiring.

Q3. Why must the fuse always be in series with the live wire?

Answer:

So that the entire current of the circuit passes through it; whenever the current is too high, the fuse melts and disconnects the live supply immediately, making the circuit safe.

Q4. Would fitting a 15 A fuse in a circuit whose wires can carry only 10 A be a safe idea?

Answer:

No. The fuse rating must match the current capacity of the wiring; a higher-rated fuse would not break the circuit before the wires overheat, defeating the safety purpose.

Case Study 3

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A decorative string contains 12 identical small bulbs connected in series and is meant for a 220 V mains supply.

Q1. What voltage does each bulb receive?

Answer:

In series the voltage divides equally, so each bulb receives \(220/12 \approx 18.3\ \text{V}\).

Q2. Why is each bulb small and low-voltage possible in this arrangement?

Answer:

Because series connection delivers only a fraction of the mains voltage to each bulb, allowing small, cheap low-voltage bulbs to be used.

Q3. What happens to the whole string if one bulb burns out, and why?

Answer:

The entire string goes dark because the series path is broken and no current can flow through any bulb.

Q4. How would the design change if the same bulbs were wired in parallel to 220 V?

Answer:

Each bulb in parallel must be rated for the full 220 V, and a failure of one bulb would not affect the others.

Case Study 4

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

An electric kettle has an element rated 1000 W at 220 V.

Q1. Find the resistance of the element.

Answer:

\(R = V^2/P = 220^2/1000 = 48{,}400/1000 = 48.4\ \text{Ω}\).

Q2. What current does the kettle draw during normal operation?

Answer:

\(I = P/V = 1000/220 \approx 4.55\ \text{A}\).

Q3. How much heat does it produce in 5 minutes?

Answer:

\(H = P t = 1000 \times 300 = 300{,}000\ \text{J} = 3 \times 10^5\ \text{J}\).

Q4. If the coil is replaced by one of double the resistance, what power does the kettle now have at the same 220 V?

Answer:

\(P = V^2/R = 220^2/96.8 = 500\ \text{W}\) — power halves when resistance doubles at constant voltage.

Case Study 5

Original — CBSE Exam-Style

A shop runs 3 fans of 80 W for 8 h, 6 tube lights of 40 W for 10 h and a 2000 W geyser for 2 h each day on a 220 V, 15 A single circuit.

Q1. Find the total connected load.

Answer:

Fans: \(3 \times 80 = 240\ \text{W}\). Tubes: \(6 \times 40 = 240\ \text{W}\). Geyser: \(2000\ \text{W}\). Total \(= 240 + 240 + 2000 = 2480\ \text{W}\).

Q2. Does the 15 A fuse remain safe when everything runs together?

Answer:

Current \(= 2480/220 \approx 11.3\ \text{A}\), which is below 15 A, so the fuse does not blow during normal simultaneous use.

Q3. Find the energy consumed in kWh in a day.

Answer:

Fans: \(0.24 \times 8 = 1.92\ \text{kWh}\). Tubes: \(0.24 \times 10 = 2.4\ \text{kWh}\). Geyser: \(2 \times 2 = 4\ \text{kWh}\). Total \(= 8.32\ \text{kWh/day}\).

Q4. At ₹5 per unit (hypothetical), what is the monthly bill?

Answer:

Monthly energy \(= 8.32 \times 30 = 249.6\ \text{kWh}\). Bill \(= 249.6 \times 5 =\) ₹1248.

24. Exam Tips

  • Solve numericals systematically: write Given (with units), choose the formula, substitute, calculate, and state the final answer with its unit. This layout earns marks even if the last step errs.
  • Identify the formula from the data: look at which quantities you are given. V and I → \(P = VI\); I and R → \(P = I^2R\); V and R → \(P = V^2/R\). The same logic works for \(H\) and \(R\).
  • Draw circuit diagrams neatly: use straight lines for wires, standard symbols (learn Fig. 11), label every component, and indicate the ammeter in series and voltmeter in parallel.
  • Equivalent resistance: check whether the resistors are chained (series → add) or between the same two points (parallel → reciprocals). Use \(R_1R_2/(R_1+R_2)\) for two parallel resistors.
  • Handle units carefully: convert all lengths to metres, areas to m², power to kilowatts (in billing sums), and seconds/hours according to the unit asked.
  • Electricity-bill questions: find kWh by kW × hours, add for all appliances, multiply by the (given) tariff per unit. The tariff is always provided in the question.
  • Present answers clearly: box the final value with its unit, and add a one-line interpretation ("so the 5 A fuse is unsuitable") to show understanding.
  • Interpreting V–I graphs: remember slope = resistance; steeper line = larger resistance; a straight line through the origin = ohmic conductor at constant temperature.

One-line pointers

  • Conventional current: positive to negative; electrons flow the opposite way.
  • Ammeter in series; voltmeter in parallel.
  • V–I graph is a straight line through the origin for an ohmic conductor.
  • Equivalent resistance is larger than the largest resistor in series, smaller than the smallest in parallel.
  • Heat depends on \(I^2\), so doubling current quadruples heating.
  • Household wiring is parallel so appliances work independently at full voltage.
  • Fuse (series, live wire) melts when current exceeds its rating.
  • Energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h); multiply by the tariff for the bill.
  • Alloys like nichrome (high resistivity, high melting point) are used for heating elements.
