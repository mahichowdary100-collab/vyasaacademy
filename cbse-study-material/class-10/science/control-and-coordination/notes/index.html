1. Introduction to Control and Coordination

All living organisms must detect and respond to changes in their surroundings to survive. Control and coordination help different parts of the body work together and produce an appropriate response to a stimulus.

Control

Control means regulating different functions so they happen at the right time and in the right order.

Coordination

Coordination is the working together of different organs of the body to carry out a particular activity smoothly.

Living beings respond to stimuli (changes in the environment). The body needs to maintain coordination between organs and respond appropriately to keep itself safe and functioning.

Stimulus, Receptor, Coordinator, Effector and Response

The basic pathway of response in an organism is:

Stimulus → Receptor → Coordinator → Effector → Response

  • Stimulus: Any change in the environment that produces a response (e.g. heat, light, touch, sound).
  • Receptor: A sense organ that detects the stimulus (e.g. skin detects heat, eyes detect light).
  • Coordinator: Part that receives information from the receptor and decides the response (brain or spinal cord).
  • Effector: Muscles or glands that carry out the response.
  • Response: The action taken by the body (e.g. withdrawing hand from a hot object).

Animals use the nervous system and endocrine (hormonal) system for control and coordination. Plants mainly use chemical coordination through plant hormones.

2. Nervous System

The nervous system is a network of nerves that carries messages between different parts of the body and the brain/spinal cord. It helps us react quickly to changes in our surroundings.

Neuron – The Structural and Functional Unit of the Nervous System

Neuron

A neuron (nerve cell) is the basic structural and functional unit of the nervous system. It carries nerve impulses (electrical signals) from one part of the body to another.

Structure of a Neuron

A typical neuron has three main parts: Cell body, Dendrites and Axon.

  • Cell body (cyton): Contains the nucleus and cytoplasm. It controls the activities of the neuron.
  • Dendrites: Short, branched extensions that receive nerve impulses from other neurons and carry them towards the cell body.
  • Axon: A long, single extension that carries nerve impulses away from the cell body to other neurons, muscles or glands.
  • Nerve endings: The branched ends of the axon where impulses are passed on to the next neuron (at a synapse).
  • Myelin sheath (protective covering): Insulates the axon and helps in faster transmission of impulses (at Class 10 level, basic understanding is sufficient).

Direction of nerve impulse: Dendrites → Cell body → Axon → Nerve endings

Synapse

Synapse

A synapse is the small gap between the nerve endings of one neuron and the dendrites of the next neuron, where nerve impulses are transmitted from one neuron to another.

At the synapse, the electrical impulse is converted into a chemical signal for transmission across the gap. This ensures impulses travel in one direction only.

Pathway of Nerve Impulse (General)

Receptor → Sensory Neuron → Central Nervous System (CNS) → Motor Neuron → Effector → Response

  • Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (sense organs) to the CNS (brain and spinal cord).
  • Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles/glands).
  • Relay/Interneurons (introduced at appropriate level) connect sensory and motor neurons inside the CNS.

3. Reflex Action

Reflex Action

A reflex action is an automatic, sudden and involuntary response to a stimulus that occurs without the involvement of conscious thought (thinking).

Examples: Withdrawing hand immediately from a hot object, blinking when something comes near the eye, coughing, sneezing, knee-jerk reflex.

Importance of Reflex Actions

Reflex actions are very fast and help protect the body from danger by providing an immediate response, without waiting for the brain to think.

Reflex Arc

Reflex Arc

The path taken by the nerve impulse during a reflex action is called the reflex arc. It usually involves the spinal cord (not the brain) for quick response.

Pathway of a reflex arc:

Stimulus → Receptor → Sensory Neuron → Spinal Cord → Motor Neuron → Effector → Response

  • Stimulus: Heat from a hot object.
  • Receptor: Skin receptors detect heat.
  • Sensory Neuron: Carries impulse from skin to spinal cord.
  • Spinal Cord (Coordinator): Processes information and sends a response via motor neuron.
  • Motor Neuron: Carries impulse from spinal cord to muscles.
  • Effector: Muscles of the hand/arm.
  • Response: Hand is withdrawn immediately.

Reflex Action vs Voluntary Action

Automatic, involuntary, suddenConscious, intentional, under our control

4. Human Brain

The brain is the main coordinating centre of the body. It receives information from sense organs, processes it, and sends instructions to muscles/glands for appropriate response. It also controls thinking, memory, reasoning, emotions and balance.

Central Nervous System (CNS)

The CNS consists of the brain and spinal cord. It receives, processes and coordinates information.

Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

The PNS consists of all nerves that arise from the brain and spinal cord and connect the CNS to all parts of the body.

Major Parts of the Human Brain

The human brain is divided into three main regions: Forebrain, Midbrain and Hindbrain.

  • Forebrain: Largest part of the brain. Includes cerebrum, thalamus and hypothalamus.
  • Midbrain: Small region connecting forebrain and hindbrain. Controls reflexes related to vision and hearing.
  • Hindbrain: Lower part of the brain. Includes cerebellum, pons and medulla oblongata.

5. Protection of the Brain

The brain is a very delicate and vital organ. It is protected by:

  • Skull (Cranium): A hard bony box that encloses and protects the brain from mechanical injury.
  • Meninges: Three protective membranes that cover the brain and spinal cord (Class 10 level: they provide cushioning and protection).
  • Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF): A fluid present between the meninges and inside the brain cavities. It acts as a shock absorber, cushions the brain from jolts, and also provides nourishment.

6. Coordination in Plants

Unlike animals, plants do not have a nervous system or nerves. They still respond to changes in their environment (stimuli) through chemical coordination using plant hormones.

Plant Coordination

Plants respond to stimuli by showing growth movements or non-growth movements. These responses are usually slower than animal responses.

Types of Plant Movements

  • Growth-based movements: Movements caused by unequal growth of plant parts (e.g. bending of shoot towards light).
  • Non-growth movements: Movements not caused by growth (e.g. folding of leaves in touch-me-not plant) – mainly nastic movements (brief mention at Class 10 level).

Tropisms

Tropism

A tropism is a directional growth movement of a plant part in response to an external stimulus. The movement can be towards (positive) or away from (negative) the stimulus.

Positive Tropism

Growth of a plant part towards the stimulus.

Negative Tropism

Growth of a plant part away from the stimulus.
