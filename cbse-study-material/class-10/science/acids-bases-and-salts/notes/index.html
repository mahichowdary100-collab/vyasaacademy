Class 10 Science – Chapter 2: Acids, Bases and Salts

Understand what acids, bases and salts are, how acids and bases behave in water, how indicators and the pH scale work, and how common salts such as baking soda and washing soda are prepared and used.

Notes Practice Paper Quiz Chapter Test

1. Introduction – Acids, Bases and Salts

Every drink, food item and cleaning product around you is classified in chemistry by its nature: acidic, basic or neutral. Substances like lemon juice and vinegar taste sour, while substances like soap solution feel slippery. These everyday observations are linked to chemical properties.

Acid

An acid is a substance that tastes sour, turns blue litmus red, and produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) when dissolved in water. Example: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulphuric acid (H₂SO₁₀).

Base

A base is a substance that tastes bitter, turns red litmus blue, feels soapy or slippery to touch, and produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻) when dissolved in water. Bases that dissolve in water are called alkalis. Example: sodium hydroxide (NaOH), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂).

Salt

A salt is a compound formed when the hydrogen ions of an acid are replaced by metal (or ammonium) ions. Most salts are formed by the neutralisation of an acid with a base. Example: sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₁₀), potassium nitrate (KNO₃).

How acids and bases differ

In water, an acid releases hydrogen ions (H⁺), while a base releases hydroxide ions (OH⁻). These two ions react together to form water, which is why acids and bases neutralise each other. Along with their different tastes and litmus tests, this ionic behaviour is the fundamental difference between them.

Scientific vs everyday descriptions

Saying "lemon juice is acidic" is an everyday description based on taste. In science, we say lemon juice is acidic because it contains citric acid, which releases hydrogen ions (H⁺) in water. Never define acids and bases by taste alone, because many acids and bases are dangerous to taste or touch.

2. Acids and Bases in Terms of Ions

When acids and bases dissolve in water, they break apart (dissociate) into positively and negatively charged particles called ions. This ionic behaviour in aqueous solution is what gives acids and bases their characteristic properties.

What are ions?

An ion is a charged particle that forms when a neutral atom or a group of atoms gains or loses electrons. A positively charged ion is a cation (like H⁺), and a negatively charged ion is an anion (like Cl⁻).

The hydrogen ion (H⁺) and hydronium ion (H₃O⁺)

When an acid dissolves in water, it produces hydrogen ions (H⁺). A hydrogen ion is simply a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron. Because it is a bare proton, H⁺ does not remain free in water — it quickly combines with a water molecule to form the hydronium ion (H₃O⁺).

\[ H^+ + H_2O \rightarrow H_3O^+ \]

Example – Hydrochloric acid in water

\[ HCl(aq) \rightarrow H^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) \]

Hydrochloric acid dissociates in water to give hydrogen ions and chloride ions. The hydrogen ions are the reason HCl behaves as an acid.

The hydroxide ion (OH⁻)

When a base dissolves in water, it releases hydroxide ions (OH⁻). Sodium hydroxide, for example, dissociates completely in water.

Example – Sodium hydroxide in water

\[ NaOH(aq) \rightarrow Na^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \]

The hydroxide ions are the reason NaOH behaves as a base. In a base, metal ions are not released directly; the characteristic ion is OH⁻.

Why aqueous solutions matter

Pure HCl gas and pure H₂SO₁₀ show acidic behaviour only in the presence of water. It is the dissociation into H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) that gives an acid its acidic character, and dissociation into OH⁻ that gives a base its basic character. In the absence of water (for example, in dry benzene or in the dry gaseous state), these ions are not formed and the substances do not show acidic or basic behaviour.

Evidence – Dry HCl does not conduct electricity

Dry hydrogen chloride gas does not change the colour of dry blue litmus and does not conduct electricity. But when water is added, HCl dissociates into H⁺ and Cl⁻, the solution conducts electricity and turns blue litmus red. This proves that the acidic behaviour of HCl develops only in aqueous solution.

Common Mistake

Students write "acid produces hydrogen gas (H₂)". Acids produce hydrogen ions (H⁺), which in water exist as hydronium ions (H₃O⁺). Hydrogen gas (H₂) is a different substance produced only when acids react with active metals.

3. Chemical Properties of Acids

A. Reaction of acids with metals

Acids react with active metals (metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series) to form a salt and hydrogen gas.

General pattern: Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas

Reaction Balanced equation Observation
Zinc + dilute hydrochloric acid \[ Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow ZnCl_2(aq) + H_2(g) \] Bubbles of hydrogen gas; salt (zinc chloride) forms
Zinc + dilute sulphuric acid \[ Zn(s) + H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow ZnSO_4(aq) + H_2(g) \] Hydrogen evolves; zinc sulphate forms
Iron + dilute sulphuric acid \[ Fe(s) + H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow FeSO_4(aq) + H_2(g) \] Hydrogen evolves; light green ferrous sulphate forms
Magnesium + dilute hydrochloric acid \[ Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow MgCl_2(aq) + H_2(g) \] Fast bubbling; magnesium dissolves

Testing for hydrogen gas

Bring a burning splinter (or a lighted match) near the mouth of the test tube. If hydrogen is present, it burns with a characteristic pop sound, because hydrogen is a highly combustible gas.

Common Mistake

Students write copper + hydrochloric acid gives hydrogen. Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so copper does not react with dilute HCl or dilute H₂SO₁₀. Only metals more reactive than hydrogen (Zn, Mg, Fe, Al) displace hydrogen from dilute acids.

B. Reaction of acids with metal carbonates

Acids react with metal carbonates to form a salt, carbon dioxide gas and water.

General pattern: Acid + Metal carbonate → Salt + Water + Carbon dioxide

Reaction Balanced equation Observation
Hydrochloric acid + sodium carbonate \[ Na_2CO_3(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow 2NaCl(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g) \] Brisk effervescence (bubbling) of CO₂
Sulphuric acid + calcium carbonate (marble) \[ CaCO_3(s) + H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow CaSO_4(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g) \] Effervescence; gas turns lime water milky

Testing for carbon dioxide

Pass the gas through lime water (calcium hydroxide solution). Carbon dioxide turns lime water milky because insoluble calcium carbonate is formed.

\[ Ca(OH)_2(aq) + CO_2(g) \rightarrow CaCO_3(s) + H_2O(l) \]

C. Reaction of acids with metal hydrogencarbonates

Acids react with metal hydrogencarbonates (bicarbonates) to form a salt, carbon dioxide gas and water.

General pattern: Acid + Metal hydrogencarbonate → Salt + Water + Carbon dioxide

Example – Sodium hydrogencarbonate + hydrochloric acid

\[ NaHCO_3(s) + HCl(aq) \rightarrow NaCl(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g) \]

Baking soda reacts with acid (for example, in cooking) with brisk effervescence, giving off carbon dioxide. The gas again turns lime water milky.

D. Reaction of acids with bases (neutralisation)

Acids react with bases to form a salt and water. This reaction is called neutralisation, because the acidic (H⁺) and basic (OH⁻) ions combine to form neutral water.

\[ H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l) \]

Reaction Balanced equation
Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide \[ HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) \rightarrow NaCl(aq) + H_2O(l) \]
Sulphuric acid + potassium hydroxide \[ H_2SO_4(aq) + 2KOH(aq) \rightarrow K_2SO_4(aq) + 2H_2O(l) \]
Nitric acid + sodium hydroxide \[ HNO_3(aq) + NaOH(aq) \rightarrow NaNO_3(aq) + H_2O(l) \]

E. Reaction of acids with metal oxides

Metal oxides are basic oxides: in water they form bases (for example, CaO in water gives Ca(OH)₂). That is why they react with acids like a base would, to form a salt and water.

General pattern: Acid + Metal oxide → Salt + Water

Reaction Balanced equation Observation
Copper(II) oxide + dilute hydrochloric acid \[ CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CuCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) \] Blue-green solution of copper(II) chloride; black oxide dissolves
Calcium oxide + dilute hydrochloric acid \[ CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) \] Quicklime dissolves; calcium chloride solution forms
Exam Tip

Metal oxides are basic oxides, so acid + metal oxide always gives salt + water (no gas). This is why acids are used to remove rust and scale in industry.

4. Chemical Properties of Bases

A. Reaction of bases with acids (neutralisation)

Bases neutralise acids to form a salt and water, exactly as in the acid + base section above (the same reaction viewed from the base side).

Example – Sodium hydroxide + hydrochloric acid

\[ NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) \rightarrow NaCl(aq) + H_2O(l) \]

Sodium hydroxide is used in antacid-like neutralisations and in the laboratory to show that OH⁻ of the base and H⁺ of the acid combine to give water.

B. Reaction of bases with non-metal oxides

Non-metal oxides such as CO₂ and SO₂ are acidic oxides — they form acids with water. That is why they react with bases to form a salt and water.

General pattern: Base + Non-metal oxide → Salt + Water

Reaction Balanced equation Observation
Carbon dioxide + calcium hydroxide (lime water) \[ Ca(OH)_2(aq) + CO_2(g) \rightarrow CaCO_3(s) + H_2O(l) \] Lime water turns milky (white precipitate of CaCO₃)
Sulphur dioxide + sodium hydroxide \[ 2NaOH(aq) + SO_2(g) \rightarrow Na_2SO_3(aq) + H_2O(l) \] Acidic gas SO₂ is neutralised by the alkali
Exam Tip

Remember the pairing: metal oxides are basic (react with acids), non-metal oxides are acidic (react with bases). This rule helps you predict reactions quickly.

C. Reaction of bases with certain metals

Some metals react with strong alkalis like sodium hydroxide to form a salt and hydrogen gas. The metals that do this are amphoteric metals such as zinc and aluminium, which can react with both acids and bases.

Reaction Balanced equation Observation
Zinc + sodium hydroxide solution (hot, concentrated) \[ Zn(s) + 2NaOH(aq) \rightarrow Na_2ZnO_2(aq) + H_2(g) \] Hydrogen gas evolves; sodium zincate forms
Aluminium + sodium hydroxide solution \[ 2Al(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaAlO_2(aq) + 3H_2(g) \] Hydrogen gas evolves; sodium aluminate forms
Common Mistake

Most metals do NOT react with bases. Only amphoteric metals like zinc and aluminium react with strong alkalis. Do not generalise "metals react with bases" to all metals.

5. What do All Acids and All Bases have in Common?

All acids, though they are different compounds, behave alike as a class. Same for bases. The reason lies in the ions they produce in water.

What all acids have in common

Every acid produces H⁺ ions when dissolved in water. In water these exist as hydronium ions (H₃O⁺). It is these hydrogen ions that:

  • give the sour taste of acids,
  • turn blue litmus red,
  • make the solution conduct electricity, and
  • react with bases to form water.
Examples – different acids, same ion

\[ HCl(aq) \rightarrow H^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) \]

\[ H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow 2H^+(aq) + SO_4^{2-}(aq) \]

\[ HNO_3(aq) \rightarrow H^+(aq) + NO_3^-(aq) \]

What all bases have in common

Every base produces OH⁻ (hydroxide) ions when dissolved in water. These hydroxide ions:

  • give the bitter taste of bases,
  • turn red litmus blue,
  • make the solution soapy/slippery, and
  • combine with H⁺ to form water.
Key idea

Acids (in aqueous solution) produce H⁺/H₃O⁺ ions; bases (in aqueous solution) produce OH⁻ ions. The acidic character comes from H⁺ (or H₃O⁺); the basic character comes from OH⁻.

Why acids behave acidically only in water

Hydrogen ions do not exist free in the dry substance. Dry HCl gas has no H⁺ ions. Only when it dissolves in water do H⁺ ions (as H₃O⁺) form, so only the aqueous solution shows acidic behaviour. The same is true for bases — their OH⁻ ions appear only in aqueous solution.

6. Indicators

An indicator is a substance whose colour (or smell) changes depending on whether it is in an acidic or a basic medium. Indicators help us detect acids and bases without tasting them.

Types of indicators

  • Natural indicators – obtained from nature, e.g. litmus, turmeric, red cabbage juice, china rose (hibiscus) petals.
  • Synthetic indicators – made in the laboratory, e.g. methyl orange, phenolphthalein.
  • Olfactory indicators – substances whose smell changes in acidic or basic medium, e.g. onion, vanilla essence, clove oil.

Litmus

Litmus is extracted from lichens. It is available as blue and red litmus papers. Blue litmus turns red in an acid; red litmus turns blue in a base. Litmus gives no colour change in a neutral solution.

Methyl orange

Methyl orange is red in an acidic medium and yellow in a basic medium.

Phenolphthalein

Phenolphthalein is colourless in an acidic and neutral medium and turns pink in a basic medium.

Turmeric

Turmeric is yellow in an acidic and neutral medium and turns red in a basic medium. This is why turmeric stains on cloth turn red when washed with soap (a base).

Olfactory indicators

Onion juice has a pungent smell. Onion juice loses its smell in a basic medium but keeps its smell in an acidic medium. Vanilla essence loses its characteristic smell in a basic medium but retains it in an acidic medium. Because the change is detected by smell, these are called olfactory indicators.

Exam Tip

Memorise the pairs: methyl orange (red ↔ yellow), phenolphthalein (colourless ↔ pink), turmeric (yellow ↔ red), litmus (red ↔ blue). Cards above frame these as "acid → base" transitions.

7. The pH Scale

Litmus and indicators tell us only whether a solution is acidic or basic. To measure how strong an acid or base is, we use the pH scale.

pH

pH is a number that indicates the acidic or basic strength of a solution. It is measured on a scale from 0 to 14. pH stands for "power of hydrogen".

The pH scale

  • pH = 0 to 6 – acidic solution. The smaller the pH, the stronger the acid.
  • pH = 7 – neutral solution (pure water).
  • pH = 8 to 14 – basic solution. The larger the pH, the stronger the base.
pH value Nature of solution Example (approximate pH)
~1Strongly acidicGastric juice in the stomach
~2–3AcidicLemon juice, vinegar
~5–6Weakly acidicTea, milk, rain water
7NeutralPure water
~8–9Weakly basicBaking soda solution, saliva
~10–12BasicSoap, washing soda solution
~13–14Strongly basicSodium hydroxide solution
Summary rule

Lower pH (below 7) → greater acidic character. pH = 7 → neutral. Higher pH (above 7) → greater basic character.

Everyday applications of pH

  • Farmers test soil pH to decide whether to add a base (lime) or an acid to soil.
  • pH of skin-care products is kept close to the natural pH of skin.
  • pH indicators (pH paper) are used in water-testing and swimming pools.
  • Mild base solutions are used to neutralise acid spills; mild acid solutions neutralise base spills.

8. Importance of pH in Everyday Life

A. pH in our digestive system

Our stomach produces hydrochloric acid (gastric juice) which helps digest food. This acid has a very low pH (about 1.0–2.0). Excess acid causes acidity or heartburn. To relieve it, people take antacids — mild bases such as magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia, Mg(OH)₂) or sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda, NaHCO₃) that neutralise the excess stomach acid.

\[ Mg(OH)_2(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow MgCl_2(aq) + 2H_2O(l) \]

B. pH and tooth decay

The mouth has bacteria that break down the sugar in food into acids. These acids lower the pH of the mouth below 5.5. Tooth enamel (made of calcium phosphate) is attacked by this acid, and decay starts. Brushing after meals removes the food and the acid; using a toothpaste that is basic neutralises the excess acid, protecting the enamel.

C. Self-defence by plants and animals

Many organisms use acids and bases to defend themselves:

  • A bee sting injects methanoic (formic) acid into the skin, causing pain. Applying a mild base such as baking soda (NaHCO₃) neutralises the acid.
  • A wasp sting is basic. Applying a mild acid such as vinegar (acetic acid) neutralises it.
  • Nettle plants release methanoic acid on their stinging hairs; rubbing a dock leaf (a mild base) or baking soda paste gives relief.

D. pH of soil

Plants grow best in soil of a suitable pH (generally near neutral, about 6.5–7). If soil is too acidic, farmers add a weak base like lime (calcium oxide/hydroxide) to neutralise it. If soil is too basic, organic manure or a suitable acid treatment is used. This is why fertilisers and soil testing are important in agriculture.

Common Mistake

Do not say "an antacid is a weak acid". Antacids are bases that neutralise stomach acid. A substance that lowers acidity must be basic, not acidic.

9. Salts

Salt

A salt is a compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is (partly or completely) replaced by a metal or by the ammonium ion. Salts are usually crystalline solids and are ionic compounds.

Formation of salts – neutralisation

Salts are most commonly formed by the neutralisation of an acid with a base: acid + base → salt + water.

Acid Base Salt formed Equation
HClNaOHSodium chloride\[ HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O \]
H₂SO₁₀NaOHSodium sulphate\[ H_2SO_4 + 2NaOH \rightarrow Na_2SO_4 + 2H_2O \]
HNO₃KOHPotassium nitrate\[ HNO_3 + KOH \rightarrow KNO_3 + H_2O \]
H₂SO₁₀Ca(OH)₂Calcium sulphate\[ H_2SO_4 + Ca(OH)_2 \rightarrow CaSO_4 + 2H_2O \]

Types of salts (Class 10 level)

  • Normal salts – formed when all the hydrogen of the acid is replaced. Example: NaCl, Na₂SO₁₀, KNO₃.
  • Acid salts – formed when only part of the hydrogen of a polyprotic acid is replaced. Example: sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃), sodium bisulphate (NaHSO₁₀).
  • Basic salts – contain hydroxide groups as well as a metal. Example: basic copper carbonate CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂.
  • Salt hydrates (hydrated salts) – salts containing water of crystallisation. Example: blue vitriol CuSO₁₀·5H₂O, washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.

10. Common Salts and their Preparation

A. Sodium chloride (common salt)

Formula: NaCl

Common salt is found dissolved in sea water and in underground salt deposits (rock salt). Sea water is evaporated in large shallow beds to obtain salt. Rock salt is mined.

Sodium chloride is the starting material for the manufacture of sodium hydroxide, sodium carbonate and bleaching powder.

Important uses:

  • Flavouring and preserving food.
  • Used for making sodium hydroxide, washing soda and bleaching powder.
  • Used on icy roads in winter to lower the freezing point.

B. Sodium hydroxide (chlor-alkali process)

Formula: NaOH

When an electric current is passed through an aqueous solution of sodium chloride (brine), it decomposes to give chlorine, hydrogen gas and sodium hydroxide. This is called the chlor-alkali process.

\[ 2NaCl(aq) + 2H_2O(l) \xrightarrow{electricity} 2NaOH(aq) + Cl_2(g) + H_2(g) \]

The products are highly useful:

  • Chlorine (Cl₂) – used for water purification, bleaching, and making PVC, insecticides and HCl.
  • Hydrogen (H₂) – used for making ammonia (Haber process) and as a fuel.
  • Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) – used for making soap, paper, artificial rayon and in degreasing.

C. Bleaching powder

Formula: CaOCl₂ (calcium oxychloride)

Bleaching powder is prepared by passing chlorine gas over dry slaked lime (calcium hydroxide).

\[ Ca(OH)_2 + Cl_2 \rightarrow CaOCl_2 + H_2O \]

Properties: bleaching action, oxidising power, reacts with acids to release chlorine, used to disinfect water.

Uses: bleaching of cotton and linen, disinfecting water, removing stains, and as an oxidising agent.

D. Baking soda

Chemical name: Sodium hydrogencarbonate (sodium bicarbonate)

Formula: NaHCO₃

Baking soda is prepared by passing carbon dioxide through brine saturated with ammonia (the Solvay-type process).

\[ NaCl(aq) + NH_3(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g) \rightarrow NaHCO_3(s) + NH_4Cl(aq) \]

Properties and everyday use:

  • It is used in baking (batter rises as CO₂ is released).
  • It is a mild base and is used in antacids.
  • It develops a heating effect: on heating it decomposes to give sodium carbonate, water and carbon dioxide.

\[ 2NaHCO_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} Na_2CO_3(s) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g) \]

E. Washing soda

Chemical name: Sodium carbonate decahydrate

Formula: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Washing soda is obtained by heating baking soda (the above decomposition), which gives anhydrous sodium carbonate, then dissolving it in water and recrystallising it (adding water of crystallisation).

\[ Na_2CO_3(s) + 10H_2O(l) \rightarrow Na_2CO_3 \cdot 10H_2O(s) \]

Uses: washing of clothes, making of glass and soap, softening of hard water, and as a laboratory reagent.

Common Mistake

Do not confuse baking soda (NaHCO₃, sodium hydrogencarbonate) with washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, sodium carbonate decahydrate) or with sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃). They have different formulae and different uses.

11. Crystallisation

Crystallisation

Crystallisation is the process in which a pure solid is obtained as regular, well-shaped crystals from its hot saturated solution by cooling.

Crystallisation is used to:

  • separate a pure solid from a solution, for example common salt from sea water or crystals of copper sulphate from its solution;
  • purify solids that are contaminated (e.g. impure salt) — the impurities, being more soluble or less soluble, remain in the mother liquor;
  • obtain crystals with a definite shape and water of crystallisation (e.g. blue vitriol, potash alum).
Example – preparing copper sulphate crystals

A hot saturated solution of impure copper sulphate is prepared. On slow cooling, blue crystals of copper sulphate (CuSO₁₀·5H₂O, blue vitriol) separate out. The impurities stay dissolved in the solution and can be filtered off.

12. Water of Crystallisation

Water of Crystallisation

The fixed number of water molecules that are chemically combined with each formula unit of a salt in its crystal structure is called Water of Crystallisation.

Examples

Effect of heating

On heating, hydrated salts lose their water of crystallisation and become anhydrous. For example:

  • Hydrated copper sulphate (blue) loses water to become white anhydrous copper sulphate.

\[ CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} CuSO_4(s) + 5H_2O(g) \]

  • Adding a few drops of water to the white anhydrous copper sulphate turns it blue again (test for water).
  • Washing soda and other hydrates similarly lose water on heating.
Exam Tip

"Blue ↔ white" copper sulphate is a favourite question: blue = hydrated CuSO₁₀·5H₂O, white = anhydrous CuSO₁₀. The blue colour returns on adding water, which is used as a test for the presence of water.

16. Application-Based Learning

Here are everyday situations that use the ideas of this chapter. Work out the answer yourself before reading it.

App 1 – Why is baking soda added to batter for idli, dosa and cake?

Baking soda is sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃). When heated (or when acid is present), it releases carbon dioxide gas. The gas gets trapped in the batter, making it fluffy and light. The CO₂ released causes the rising of the batter.

App 2 – Why does a mother treat acidity at home with baking soda in water?

Acidity is caused by excess HCl in the stomach. Baking soda is a mild base. It neutralises the excess acid (acid + base → salt + water + CO₂), giving relief. (It is for quick relief only; prolonged use is harmful.)

App 3 – Why does a farmer add quicklime or slaked lime to acidic soil?

Acidic soil has a low pH. Lime is CaO (or Ca(OH)₂), a base. It neutralises the excess soil acid, bringing the pH close to neutral, which is best for most crops.

App 4 – Why does a bee sting sting less when baking soda paste is applied?

A bee sting injects methanoic (formic) acid. Baking soda is a mild base that neutralises the acid, reducing the irritation.

App 5 – Why does a wasp sting sting more when baking soda is applied?

A wasp sting is basic. Applying a base makes the area more basic; instead a mild acid like vinegar should be used to neutralise it.

App 6 – Why is food preserved by adding acidic substances like vinegar?

Acidic media inhibit the growth of many bacteria and micro-organisms. Vinegar (acetic acid) used in pickling makes the medium acidic, preserving the food longer.

App 7 – Why does rain water turn blue litmus slightly red after a while?

Rain water dissolves CO₂ (and other gases) from the air, forming carbonic acid (weak acid), so its pH is slightly below 7. On a polluted day, oxides of sulphur/nitrogen make it even more acidic (acid rain).

App 8 – Why is white anhydrous copper sulphate used as a test for water?

White anhydrous CuSO₁₀ turns blue on adding water, forming CuSO₁₀·5H₂O. This is a quick test to detect the presence of water in a substance.

App 9 – Why does a swab of turmeric applied on the skin turn red when soap is used?

Soap is basic. Turmeric is a natural indicator — it turns red in a basic medium. So the basic soap makes the yellow turmeric turn red.

App 10 – Why does toothpaste help prevent tooth decay?

Bacteria produce acid in the mouth which lowers pH below 5.5 and attacks enamel. Toothpaste is basic; it neutralises the acid and restores a safe pH, protecting the enamel.

17. Competency and Reasoning Examples

Reason 1 – How would you prove that hydrogen chloride gas shows acidic behaviour only in water?

Take dry blue litmus and dry HCl gas — dry HCl does not change the colour of dry blue litmus. Also, dry HCl does not conduct electricity. When water is added, HCl dissociates into H⁺ (H₃O⁺) and Cl⁻, turning blue litmus red and conducting electricity. Hence acidity appears only in aqueous medium.

Reason 2 – A student wrote "copper reacts with dilute HCl to give hydrogen gas". Why is it wrong?

Copper is less reactive than hydrogen (it lies below hydrogen in the reactivity series), so it cannot displace hydrogen from its dilute acids. Copper does not react with dilute HCl or dilute H₂SO₁₀.

Reason 3 – Why is a glass of curd not recommended for a patient who is taking antacids?

Curd is acidic. Antacids are bases that neutralise excess stomach acid. Taking more acid (curd) would reduce the effect of the antacid and may cause discomfort.

Reason 4 – Same concentration of lemon juice and HCl — which has a lower pH and why?

HCl is a strong acid that ionises almost completely, giving more H⁺ ions (lower pH). Lemon juice contains citric acid, a weak acid that ionises only partially, giving fewer H⁺ ions (higher pH). So the HCl solution has the lower pH.

Reason 5 – Why does lime water turn milky when you exhale into it?

The breath contains carbon dioxide, an acidic non-metal oxide. CO₂ reacts with Ca(OH)₂ (lime water) to give insoluble calcium carbonate, which makes the lime water milky. Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O.

Reason 6 – A cloth soaked in turmeric solution turns red on the parts where soap is applied. Explain.

Turmeric is a natural indicator that is yellow in acid/neutral and red in a base. Soap solution is basic, so the turmeric-touched area turns red. Washing with soap/water leaves a red spot.

Reason 7 – Why does zinc react with dilute HCl but not with pure dry hydrogen chloride gas?

Zn reacts with the H⁺ ions present in aqueous HCl. Dry HCl gas has no free H⁺ ions, so there is nothing to react with. Acidity requires aqueous medium.

Reason 8 – Why are factory waste (acidic) and sewage treated before they are released into rivers?

Acidic factory waste would kill aquatic life and make rivers unfit. It is neutralised with a base before release, so that the treated water is near-neutral and safe for the ecosystem.

18. Case-Based Questions

Case 1 – Soil health for a farmer

A farmer finds that his field gives poor yield. A soil test shows the pH of the soil is 4.5.

Q(i): Is this soil acidic, basic or neutral? Ans: Acidic (pH < 7).

Q(ii): Suggest a remedy. Ans: Add a base — quicklime (CaO) or slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂) — to neutralise the acidity and raise the pH.

Q(iii): Ten days later the pH was measured as 7.2. What does this tell us? Ans: The pH is now near neutral, suitable for most crops; the lime treatment was effective.

Case 2 – Tooth decay

School students observe stains on teeth after eating sweets. Their dentist says the pH of the mouth drops below 5.5 after sugary food.

Q(i): What happens when pH drops below 5.5? Ans: The acid produced by bacteria attacks tooth enamel (calcium phosphate), starting decay.

Q(ii): Why do basic toothpastes protect teeth? Ans: They neutralise the mouth acid and raise the pH back to a safe level.

Q(iii): What routine removes the acid? Ans: Rinsing and brushing after meals removes food and acid before decay begins.

Case 3 – Acidity relief

After a heavy meal, a person feels burning in the chest. The doctor prescribes an antacid tablet. Its ingredients include magnesium hydroxide.

Q(i): What is the cause of the burning? Ans: Excess hydrochloric acid produced in the stomach.

Q(ii): Write the reaction of the antacid with the stomach acid. Ans: Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O (neutralisation).

Q(iii): Why is milk of magnesia only a mild base and not caustic soda? Ans: Caustic soda (NaOH) is a strong base that would harm the stomach lining; Mg(OH)₂ is a safe, weak base that only neutralises the excess acid.

Case 4 – Baking in the kitchen

A baker adds a spoonful of baking soda to the cake batter. During baking, the cake rises and becomes spongy.

Q(i): Why does the batter rise? Ans: On heating, baking soda releases CO₂ gas, which expands and gets trapped, making the cake spongy.

Q(ii): Write the decomposition reaction. Ans: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.

Q(iii): The baker complains the cake tastes soapy. Suggest a remedy and reason. Ans: Na₂CO₃ left in the cake is a base giving a soapy taste. Use an acid ingredient (curd, lemon juice) along with baking soda to neutralise it.

Case 5 – A blue crystal turns white

A student heats blue copper sulphate crystals in a dry test tube. White powder forms and water droplets appear at the mouth of the tube.

Q(i): Why did the crystals turn white? Ans: The water of crystallisation (5H₂O) of CuSO₁₀·5H₂O was driven off, leaving white anhydrous CuSO₁₀.

Q(ii): Write the equation. Ans: CuSO₁₀·5H₂O → CuSO₁₀ + 5H₂O.

Q(iii): How will the student regain blue colour, and what does the test confirm? Ans: Add a few drops of water — anhydrous CuSO₁₀ turns blue again. This confirms the presence of water.

19. Solved Examples

Basic level

Q1 – Which ions do all acids produce in water?

Solution: All acids produce H⁺ ions in water (present as hydronium ions, H₃O⁺). This is the common feature of all acids.

Q2 – What is the colour of blue litmus in (a) lemon juice (b) soap solution?

Solution: (a) Lemon juice is acidic, so blue litmus turns red. (b) Soap solution is basic, so blue litmus stays blue (no change).

Q3 – A solution has pH 4. Is it acidic, basic or neutral? Will phenolphthalein show any colour in it?

Solution: pH 4 is acidic (below 7). Phenolphthalein remains colourless in an acidic solution.

Q4 – Give the chemical formula of (a) baking soda (b) washing soda.

Solution: (a) Baking soda is sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO₃. (b) Washing soda is sodium carbonate decahydrate, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.

Q5 – Name the gas evolved when dilute HCl is added to (a) zinc (b) sodium carbonate.

Solution: (a) With zinc — hydrogen gas (H₂). (b) With sodium carbonate — carbon dioxide gas (CO₂).

Moderate level

Q6 – Balance: zinc + dilute sulphuric acid.

Solution: Zn(s) + H₂SO₁₀(aq) → ZnSO₁₀(aq) + H₂(g). It is already balanced: 1 Zn, 2 H, 1 SO₁₀ on each side.

Q7 – Explain why HCl in dry state does not turn blue litmus red but its aqueous solution does.

Solution: HCl molecules exist without free ions in the dry state — there is no H⁺ ion, so no acidic behaviour. As an aqueous solution, HCl dissociates into H⁺ (H₃O⁺) and Cl⁻ ions; the H⁺ ions turn blue litmus red.

Q8 – Two juices A (pH 2) and B (pH 6) are compared. Which is more acidic and why?

Solution: A with pH 2 is more acidic because the pH value is lower — the H⁺ ion concentration is higher.

Q9 – Write the reaction that occurs when CO₂ is passed through lime water and the milkiness formed.

Solution: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l). The insoluble white calcium carbonate makes the lime water milky.

Q10 – A metal M reacts with dilute HCl to give hydrogen gas but does not react with dilute H₂SO₁₀ slowly. Name a suitable metal.

Solution: A metal above hydrogen in the reactivity series, e.g. zinc (Zn), reacts with dilute HCl to give H₂ gas. (Zn reacts with H₂SO₁₀ too; both reactions give hydrogen.)

Q11 – Why is bleaching powder used in water treatment stations?

Solution: Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) releases chlorine which disinfects water and kills harmful micro-organisms. It is also an oxidising agent and bleaches fabrics.

Q12 – A student added a few drops of phenolphthalein to soap solution and to common salt solution. What colours are seen?

Solution: Soap solution is basic, so phenolphthalein turns pink. Common salt solution is neutral, so phenolphthalein remains colourless.

Application level

Q13 – A waiter uses sodium hydrogencarbonate solution to clean a stain of tomato (acidic) on a table. Explain.

Solution: Tomato stain is acidic. Washing with a mild base (baking soda solution) neutralises the acid, helping to lift the stain.

Q14 – A child is stung by a bee. Her elder sister rubs baking soda paste on the spot. Justify.

Solution: Bee sting injects methanoic (formic) acid. Baking soda (NaHCO₃) is a mild base that neutralises it, easing pain.

Q15 – A washerman treats hard water with washing soda before washing clothes. Why?

Solution: Washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) removes hardness ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) converting them to insoluble carbonates, so soap lathers freely and cleans better.

Q16 – A farmer adds gypsum (CaSO₁₀·2H₂O) to improve his soil. Give one reason soil may need a base-content adjustment and how gypsum helps as a source of calcium.

Solution: Gypsum supplies calcium (and sulphur) to the soil and improves its structure. It is also used in agriculture to reclaim alkaline/sodic soils. Calcium ions help healthy plant growth.

Q17 – A road cleaner spreads common salt on icy roads in winter. Explain the purpose.

Solution: Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so the ice melts and does not freeze again easily, making the road safer.

Competency level

Q18 – A test tube holds white anhydrous copper sulphate and a second holds wet sand. How would you tell which holds water?

Solution: Sprinkle a few drops of the suspected liquid on white anhydrous CuSO₁₀. If it turns blue, the liquid contains water (CuSO₁₀·5H₂O is blue). (Alternatively use a cobalt chloride paper which turns pink on water.)

Q19 – A student tests four solutions and records: I is red with methyl orange but colourless with phenolphthalein; II is yellow with methyl orange and pink with phenolphthalein. Classify each.

Solution: Solution I is acidic (methyl orange red; phenolphthalein colourless). Solution II is basic (methyl orange yellow; phenolphthalein pink).

Q20 – Two solutions have pH 5 and pH 9. If equal drops of universal indicator are added, what colours appear?

Solution: pH 5 is acidic — indicator gives an orange/red shade; pH 9 is basic — indicator gives a blue shade. (Universal indicator turns red/orange in acid and blue/violet in base.)

Q21 – A solution X gives hydrogen gas with zinc but does not affect red litmus. Another solution Y turns red litmus blue. Which is an acid?

Solution: Solution Y is basic (turns red litmus blue). Solution X reacts with Zn giving hydrogen — that shows it has H⁺ ions; but if it does not affect red litmus, X is not strongly acidic. Since acids (with H⁺) produce H₂ with Zn, X behaves as an acid while Y is a base. Litmus and H₃O⁺ concentration together identify the nature.

Case-based level

Q22 – A water plant uses bleaching powder to treat drinking water. Give the formula of bleaching powder and one other use.

Solution: Formula CaOCl₂ (calcium oxychloride). Other use: bleaching cotton and linen (or as an oxidising agent).

Q23 – A bakery writes on its packet: ingredients — maida, sugar, baking soda, curd. Why is curd or lemon juice used along with baking soda?

Solution: Curd/lemon juice are acids. They react with baking soda to release CO₂ gas — the batter rises. Also, the acid neutralises any leftover Na₂CO₃ so the cake is not soapy in taste.

Q24 – A doctor advises a patient to take milk of magnesia after acidity. Which chemical is it and what is its nature?

Solution: Milk of magnesia is magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)₂ — a mild base. It neutralises excess stomach HCl, giving relief from acidity.

Q25 – A tea estate receives acidic rain (pH 4.5). How can the manager decide treatment for the tea soil?

Solution: The manager should test the soil pH. If too acidic, add a base (lime, CaO or Ca(OH)₂) to neutralise and raise pH to the level suitable for tea growth (mildly acidic, ~5–6), monitoring pH after treatment.

20. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  1. 1
    Wrong formulae. Learn by heart: HCl, H₂SO₁₀, HNO₃, NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂. A wrong formula costs marks even when the concept is right.
  2. 2
    Unbalanced equations. Balance atoms on both sides. For example, H₂SO₁₀ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₁₀ + 2H₂O (the "2" on NaOH and H₂O is easy to forget).
  3. 3
    Confusing baking soda and washing soda. Baking soda = NaHCO₃ (sodium hydrogencarbonate); washing soda = Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (sodium carbonate decahydrate).
  4. 4
    Writing "acid gives hydrogen gas" instead of hydrogen ions. Acids release H⁺ ions (as H₃O⁺) in water; H₂ gas appears only in particular reactions like acid + active metal.
  5. 5
    Forgetting the pop test. H₂ burns with a pop; CO₂ turns lime water milky. Mixing up the two tests is a classic error.
  6. 6
    Indicator colour mix-ups. Turmeric red in base; phenolphthalein pink in base; methyl orange red in acid, yellow in base. Frequently inverted by students.
  7. 7
    pH of pure water written as "water is pH 0". Pure water is pH 7 (neutral).
  8. 8
    Thinking the bee sting should be treated with an acid. Bee sting is acid — treat with a mild base. Wasp sting is base — treat with a mild acid.
  9. 9
    Chlor-alkali products. Remember the three products of electrolysing brine: NaOH (cathode side), Cl₂ and H₂ (anode side). Do not drop any.
  10. 10
    Water of crystallisation. CuSO₁₀·5H₂O loses 5H₂O on heating (blue → white). The number "5" is part of the formula — keep it in the equation.
  11. 11
    Metal oxide vs non-metal oxide. Metal oxides are basic (react with acids); non-metal oxides are acidic (react with bases). Mixing this reverses the reaction products.
  12. 12
    Not writing states or conditions. Add (s), (aq), (g), Δ (heat) or "electricity" where asked; the exam rewards complete, precise equations.
  13. 13
    Calling all bases soluble. A base that dissolves in water is an alkali; not all bases dissolve (e.g. Cu(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble).
  14. 14
    Antacid direction. Antacids are bases, not acids. They lower acidity in the stomach.

21. Board Exam Tips

  • Read the whole question before writing; many errors come from answering only part of a 3-mark question.
  • Write balanced equations with physical states (s), (aq), (g) and the arrow conditions (Δ, electricity).
  • Structure long answers: define, give the equation, then the observation. For example, for "acid + metal" write the pattern, one equation, and the pop test.
  • Learn the formulae table fully — it directly answers 1-mark formula questions.
  • pH questions: state pH = 7 neutral, < 7 acidic, > 7 basic, then justify with ions (H⁺ vs OH⁻).
  • Everyday-life applications (bee sting, tooth decay, soil pH, antacids, baking soda in cooking) come up frequently — summarise each in one or two lines.
  • Common name ↔ chemical name ↔ formula must be a reflex: quicklime CaO, slaked lime Ca(OH)₂, blue vitriol CuSO₁₀·5H₂O, etc.
  • Practise case-based questions: the pattern is a short situation + 3 questions; answer each independently using the chapter concepts.
  • Time management: attempt 1-mark questions first, then the equation-based questions, leaving the descriptive ones for last with neat headings.
  • Underline key terms like hydronium ion, water of crystallisation, neutralisation — markers reward precision.
Board Exam Alert

For reaction-based questions, the marker expects: correct reactants, balanced equation, correct products, and a valid observation. A neat, clean presentation with the diagrams (covered test tube, lime water bubbler) earns full credit.

22. Last-Minute Revision

  1. Acids: sour taste, blue litmus → red, produce H⁺/H₃O⁺ in water.
  2. Bases: bitter taste, soapy feel, red litmus → blue, produce OH⁻ in water.
  3. Hydronium ion: H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ — this is the acid ion actually present in water.
  4. Acid + metal: salt + H₂ (pop test: burning splinter pops).
  5. Acid + carbonate / hydrogencarbonate: salt + H₂O + CO₂ (CO₂ turns lime water milky).
  6. Neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water; H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
  7. Metal oxides are basic; non-metal oxides are acidic.
  8. Indicators (acid → base): methyl orange red → yellow; phenolphthalein colourless → pink; turmeric yellow → red; litmus red ↔ blue.
  9. Olfactory indicators: onion, vanilla, clove oil (smell changes in base).
  10. pH scale 0–14: < 7 acidic (lower = stronger), 7 neutral, > 7 basic (higher = stronger).
  11. Stomach: HCl ~pH 1–2; antacid = mild base (Mg(OH)₂, baking soda).
  12. Tooth decay: acid below pH 5.5 attacks enamel; basic toothpaste neutralises.
  13. Bee sting: methanoic acid → treat with baking soda; wasp sting: basic → treat with vinegar.
  14. Soil pH: too acidic → add lime; too basic → add manure/mild acid.
  15. Chlor-alkali: brine electrolysis → NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂.
  16. Bleaching powder CaOCl₂: chlorine + dry slaked lime; bleaches and disinfects.
  17. Baking soda NaHCO₃: on heating → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.
  18. Washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O: from Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O.
  19. Water of crystallisation: CuSO₁₀·5H₂O → (heating) CuSO₁₀ + 5H₂O; blue ↔ white test.
  20. Crystallisation: pure crystals from hot saturated solution; used to purify salts and obtain hydrated crystals.
