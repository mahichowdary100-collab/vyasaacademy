Class 10 Mathematics – Chapter 9: Some Applications of Trigonometry

Trigonometry becomes a practical measuring tool in this chapter. Learn how a line of sight, an angle of elevation or depression, and a right triangle help estimate heights and distances that are difficult to measure directly. These original notes explain the diagrams, formulas, worked methods and checks step by step.

1. The measuring idea

Some distances are awkward to measure directly: the height of a tower, the width of a river, or the distance from a viewing point to the foot of a structure. If we can measure an angle and one suitable length, trigonometric ratios let us calculate the unknown side of a right triangle.

Chapter focus

Represent the situation with a clear right-angled triangle, identify the known angle and side, select a ratio, and solve for the required height or distance.

  • Height is a vertical distance.
  • Horizontal distance is measured along a level line.
  • Line of sight is the straight line from the observer's eye to the object being viewed.

2. Line of sight, elevation and depression

The line of sight and a horizontal line through the observer form the angle used in the problem.

Angle of elevation

When the observer looks upward, the angle between the horizontal through the observer and the upward line of sight is the angle of elevation.

Angle of depression

When the observer looks downward, the angle between the horizontal through the observer and the downward line of sight is the angle of depression.

Why the two angles match

The horizontal through the observer and the ground are parallel. The line of sight is a transversal, so the angle of depression equals the alternate interior angle of elevation. This lets us use the same right-triangle ratios.

3. Build the right-triangle model

  1. Mark the top and foot of the object, the observer and the line of sight.
  2. Draw a horizontal from the observer and a vertical through the object's foot. A vertical and horizontal meet at \(90^\circ\).
  3. Mark the measured angle at the observer. For a depression angle, use its equal angle of elevation at the ground point.
  4. Label known and unknown sides. Add the observer's eye height separately if the observer is above ground.

4. Choosing a trigonometric ratio

For an acute angle \(\theta\), name sides relative to that angle: opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse. The hypotenuse is always opposite the right angle.

Side pairRatioUseful rearrangement
Opposite and adjacent\(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\)\(\text{opposite}=\text{adjacent}\tan\theta\)
Opposite and hypotenuse\(\sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\)\(\text{opposite}=\text{hypotenuse}\sin\theta\)
Adjacent and hypotenuse\(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\)\(\text{adjacent}=\text{hypotenuse}\cos\theta\)
Most common setup

If vertical height is \(h\), horizontal distance is \(d\), and elevation angle is \(\theta\), then

\[\tan\theta=\frac{h}{d},\qquad h=d\tan\theta,\qquad d=\frac{h}{\tan\theta}.\]

Use sine or cosine when the given length is the line of sight (the hypotenuse). Choose the ratio connecting the two sides in the question.

5. A reliable solution method

1
Sketch and label. Mark the right angle, horizontal, line of sight and measured angle.
2
Translate the wording. Decide whether the angle is elevation or depression and whether height is measured from eye level or ground.
3
Choose a ratio. Identify opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse relative to the marked angle.
4
Solve exactly. Substitute known values and retain exact forms such as \(8\sqrt3\) until the last step.
5
Check and state. Adjust for eye height if needed, include units and check whether the result fits the diagram.

6. Step-by-step examples

Example 1: Find a tower's height

An observer stands \(24\text{ m}\) from a tower's foot. Its top is seen at \(30^\circ\). Find its height.

Let the height be \(h\). Tangent connects opposite and adjacent sides:

\[\tan30^\circ=\frac{h}{24}\Rightarrow h=24\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3}=8\sqrt3\text{ m}\approx13.86\text{ m}.\]

Answer: \(8\sqrt3\text{ m}\) (about \(13.86\text{ m}\)).

Example 2: Include eye height

A student's eye is \(1.6\text{ m}\) above level ground. From \(18\text{ m}\) away, the building top is at \(45^\circ\). Find the building's height.

Height above eye level is \(18\tan45^\circ=18\text{ m}\). Add eye height:

\[H=18+1.6=19.6\text{ m}.\]

Answer: \(19.6\text{ m}\).

Example 3: Horizontal distance from depression

From a \(24\text{ m}\)-high tower, the angle of depression to a point on level ground is \(30^\circ\). Find its distance from the tower's foot.

The equal elevation angle at the ground point is \(30^\circ\). If distance is \(d\):

\[\tan30^\circ=\frac{24}{d}\Rightarrow d=24\sqrt3\text{ m}\approx41.57\text{ m}.\]

Answer: \(24\sqrt3\text{ m}\) (about \(41.57\text{ m}\)).

Example 4: Two observation points

From the nearer point, a tower is seen at \(60^\circ\); from a point \(16\text{ m}\) farther along the same level path, it is seen at \(30^\circ\). Find the nearer distance and tower height.

Let the nearer distance be \(x\text{ m}\) and height be \(h\text{ m}\). Then

\[h=x\tan60^\circ=\sqrt3x,\qquad h=\(x+16\)\tan30^\circ=\frac{x+16}{\sqrt3}.\]

Equating gives \(3x=x+16\), so \(x=8\). Thus \(h=8\sqrt3\text{ m}\).

Answer: nearer distance \(8\text{ m}\); height \(8\sqrt3\text{ m}\) (about \(13.86\text{ m}\)).

Example 5: Flagstaff on a building

From a ground point, the roof of a \(12\text{ m}\)-high building is seen at \(30^\circ\), and the flagstaff top at \(45^\circ\). Find the flagstaff height.

First find the horizontal distance using the roof:

\[\tan30^\circ=\frac{12}{d}\Rightarrow d=12\sqrt3\text{ m}.\]

Total height to the top is \(d\tan45^\circ=12\sqrt3\text{ m}\). Subtract building height:

\[12\sqrt3-12=12\(\sqrt3-1\)\text{ m}\approx8.78\text{ m}.\]

Answer: (12\(\sqrt3-1\)\text{ m}) (about \(8.78\text{ m}\)).

Example 6: Compare two shadow lengths

A vertical pole casts shadows when the sun's elevation is \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\). The longer shadow is \(18\text{ m}\) longer. Find the pole's height.

For height \(h\), shadow lengths are \(h\cot30^\circ=h\sqrt3\) and \(h\cot60^\circ=\frac{h}{\sqrt3}\). So

\[h\sqrt3-\frac{h}{\sqrt3}=18\Rightarrow\frac{2h}{\sqrt3}=18\Rightarrow h=9\sqrt3\text{ m}\approx15.59\text{ m}.\]

Answer: \(9\sqrt3\text{ m}\) (about \(15.59\text{ m}\)).

Example 7: A level span with two markers

An observer is \(6\text{ m}\) above a level span. Angles of depression to markers on opposite sides of the point directly below are \(45^\circ\) and \(30^\circ\). Find the distance between them.

Horizontal distances from the foot point are \(6\cot45^\circ=6\text{ m}\) and \(6\cot30^\circ=6\sqrt3\text{ m}\). The markers are on opposite sides, so add:

\[D=6+6\sqrt3=6\(1+\sqrt3\)\text{ m}\approx16.39\text{ m}.\]

Answer: (6\(1+\sqrt3\)\text{ m}) (about \(16.39\text{ m}\)).

Example 8: Ladder against a wall

A \(5\text{ m}\) ladder makes a \(60^\circ\) angle with level ground. Find its vertical reach and the foot's distance from the wall.

The ladder is the hypotenuse:

\[\text{height}=5\sin60^\circ=\frac{5\sqrt3}{2}\text{ m},\qquad\text{distance}=5\cos60^\circ=2.5\text{ m}.\]

Answer: reach \(\frac{5\sqrt3}{2}\text{ m}\) (about \(4.33\text{ m}\)); foot distance \(2.5\text{ m}\).

7. Common mistakes

  • Measuring from the vertical: elevation and depression are measured from a horizontal line.
  • Mixing the side ratio: tangent uses vertical height over horizontal distance.
  • Forgetting eye height: add it when converting eye-level height to total height from ground.
  • Adding/subtracting the wrong heights: check if the question asks total height or only a part above a roof/platform.
  • Adding distances on the same side: use the difference for separation on one side; add only if the required span crosses the foot point.
  • Rounding too soon: keep exact radicals until the final line.
  • Omitting units: state metres or the units given.

8. Examination tips

  • Draw a labelled right triangle, even if none is provided.
  • Write opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse relative to the marked angle before selecting a ratio.
  • For depression, copy the angle to the ground point using parallel horizontals.
  • For multiple observation points, set a variable for the nearer distance and equate expressions for the same height.
  • Keep exact values such as \(24\sqrt3\), adding a decimal only when useful or requested.
  • Say whether the result is total height, horizontal distance or line-of-sight length.

9. Quick revision

Elevation: upward line of sight from observer's horizontal.

Depression: downward line of sight from observer's horizontal.

Corresponding angles: depression = elevation.

\[\tan\theta=\frac{\text{vertical height}}{\text{horizontal distance}}\]

Eye height: total object height = height above eye level + observer's eye height.

Line-of-sight length: use sine for vertical height and cosine for horizontal distance.

10. Self-practice

Original revision prompts; these do not reproduce textbook questions.

  1. From a point \(15\text{ m}\) from a pole's foot, its top is seen at \(45^\circ\). Find its height.
  2. A \(20\text{ m}\) tower is viewed at \(30^\circ\). Find the horizontal distance.
  3. An observer's eye is \(1.5\text{ m}\) above ground and \(10\text{ m}\) from a wall. The top is at \(60^\circ\). Find the wall height.
  4. At \(12\text{ m}\) from a tower, elevation is \(30^\circ\). Find its height exactly and approximately.
  5. A \(10\text{ m}\) cable from level ground to a pole top makes \(30^\circ\) with the ground. Find height and horizontal distance.
Check your answers

  2. \(20\sqrt3\text{ m}\approx34.64\text{ m}\)
  3. \(10\sqrt3+1.5\text{ m}\approx18.82\text{ m}\)
  4. \(4\sqrt3\text{ m}\approx6.93\text{ m}\)
  5. Height \(5\text{ m}\); horizontal distance \(5\sqrt3\text{ m}\)
