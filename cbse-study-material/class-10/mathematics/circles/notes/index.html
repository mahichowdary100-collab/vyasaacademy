Class 10 Mathematics – Chapter 10: Circles

This chapter studies how a straight line meets a circle and what happens when the line just touches it. Learn the radius–tangent relationship, how the number of tangents depends on a point's position, and why two tangents from the same external point have equal lengths. Original diagrams and worked examples show each idea step by step.

1. A straight line and a circle

A straight line can meet a circle in three different ways. The number of common points tells us which case we have.

No common point

The line lies outside the circle and does not meet it.

Two common points

The line cuts the circle at two distinct points. It is called a secant.

One common point

The line touches the circle at exactly one point. It is called a tangent.

A tangent's unique common point with the circle is its point of contact or point of tangency.

2. Tangent and point of contact

Imagine moving a secant so that its two intersection points get closer together. In the limiting position, they coincide and the line touches the circle at one point: the tangent.

In the diagram, line \(PT\) touches the circle at \(T\), whose centre is \(O\). Segment \(OT\) is the radius drawn to the point of contact.

3. The tangent is perpendicular to the radius

Theorem 10.1

The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.

If \(PT\) is tangent at \(T\) to a circle with centre \(O\), then \(OT\perp PT\), so \(\angle OTP=90^\circ\).

Useful converse: if a line through a point \(T\) on the circle is perpendicular to radius \(OT\), that line is the tangent at \(T\). The point must lie on the circle; being perpendicular to a radius somewhere else is not enough.

Diagram check

Mark the right angle where the radius ends on the circle. Do not place it at the centre or at the external point.

4. Number of tangents from a point

Whether a tangent can be drawn through a point depends on where that point lies relative to the circle.

Position of point PNumber of tangentsReason
Inside the circle0A line through P cuts the circle at two points.
On the circle1There is one tangent at the point of contact.
Outside the circle2Two distinct tangent lines can be drawn from P.

The length of a tangent from an external point is the distance from that point to the point of contact, measured along the tangent segment.

5. Equal tangents from an external point

Theorem 10.2

The lengths of the two tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.

Proof using RHS congruence

Let \(PA\) and \(PB\) touch the circle with centre \(O\) at \(A\) and \(B\). Join \(OA\), \(OB\) and \(OP\).

1
Radii: \(OA=OB\), because both are radii of the same circle.
2
Right angles: \(OA\perp PA\) and \(OB\perp PB\), so \(\angle OAP=\angle OBP=90^\circ\).
3
Common hypotenuse: \(OP\) is common to right triangles \(\triangle OAP\) and \(\triangle OBP\).
4
Congruence and result: the triangles are congruent by RHS. Therefore, corresponding sides \(PA=PB\) \(CPCT\).

The same congruence also shows that \(OP\) bisects the angle between the two tangent segments.

6. Tangent length and Pythagoras

Join the centre \(O\) to the external point \(P\) and to the point of contact \(T\). Because \(OT\perp PT\), triangle \(OTP\) is right-angled at \(T\).

Tangent-length relationship

For radius \(r=OT\), tangent length \(t=PT\), and centre-to-external-point distance \(d=OP\):

\[d^2=r^2+t^2,\qquad t=\sqrt{d^2-r^2},\qquad d=\sqrt{r^2+t^2}.\]

The tangent is the shorter leg in the right triangle; \(OP\), opposite the right angle, is the hypotenuse. A useful check is \(OP>r\) for an external point.

7. Step-by-step examples

Example 1: State the angle at contact

A tangent touches a circle at \(T\). The centre is \(O\). Find the angle between radius \(OT\) and the tangent.

By Theorem 10.1, the tangent at a point is perpendicular to the radius through that point.

\[\angle OTP=90^\circ.\]

Answer: \(90^\circ\).

Example 2: Find a tangent length

A circle has radius \(5\text{ cm}\). The distance from its centre to an external point \(P\) is \(13\text{ cm}\). Find the tangent length.

Let the tangent length be \(t\). Radius and tangent are perpendicular at contact, so \(OP\) is the hypotenuse:

\[t^2+5^2=13^2\Rightarrow t^2=169-25=144\Rightarrow t=12.\]

Lengths are positive, so take the positive square root.

Answer: \(12\text{ cm}\).

Example 3: Find the centre-to-point distance

A circle's radius is \(7\text{ cm}\), and a tangent segment from an external point is \(24\text{ cm}\). Find the distance from the centre to that point.

Use the right triangle formed by the radius and tangent:

\[OP^2=7^2+24^2=49+576=625\Rightarrow OP=25\text{ cm}.\]

Answer: \(25\text{ cm}\).

Example 4: Use equal tangents to find a variable

Two tangents from \(P\) have lengths \(4x+1\text{ cm}\) and \(7x-11\text{ cm}\). Find \(x\) and the tangent length.

Both are drawn from the same external point, so their lengths are equal:

\[4x+1=7x-11\Rightarrow12=3x\Rightarrow x=4.\]

Substitution gives (4\(4\)+1=17\text{ cm}); the other expression also gives \(28-11=17\text{ cm}\).

Answer: \(x=4\); each tangent is \(17\text{ cm}\).

Example 5: A chord tangent to an inner circle

Two concentric circles have radii \(13\text{ cm}\) and \(5\text{ cm}\). A chord of the larger circle touches the smaller circle. Find the chord length.

The perpendicular from the common centre to the chord reaches its midpoint. Its length equals the inner radius, \(5\text{ cm}\). If half the chord is \(x\), the right triangle has hypotenuse \(13\) and legs \(5,x\):

\[x^2+5^2=13^2\Rightarrow x^2=144\Rightarrow x=12\text{ cm}.\]

The full chord is twice its half:

\[AB=2x=24\text{ cm}.\]

Answer: \(24\text{ cm}\).

Example 6: Find the angle at the centre

Two tangents from \(P\) touch a circle at \(A\) and \(B\). The angle between the tangents is \(60^\circ\). Find \(\angle AOB\).

Radii to the contacts are perpendicular to the tangents. The four angles of quadrilateral \(OAPB\) sum to \(360^\circ\):

\[\angle AOB+90^\circ+60^\circ+90^\circ=360^\circ. \]

Therefore \(\angle AOB=120^\circ\).

Answer: \(120^\circ\).

Example 7: Check whether a line is tangent

A circle has radius \(8\text{ cm}\). The perpendicular distance from the centre to a line is also \(8\text{ cm}\). What is the line's relationship to the circle?

The foot of the perpendicular is \(8\text{ cm}\) from the centre, equal to the radius. Therefore that foot lies on the circle, and the line is perpendicular to the radius at the circle. By the converse tangent criterion, it is a tangent.

Answer: the line is tangent to the circle at the foot of the perpendicular.

8. Common mistakes

  • Using the right-angle theorem at the wrong point: the radius must end at the tangent's point of contact.
  • Calling a secant a tangent: a secant has two intersections; a tangent has one.
  • Claiming every perpendicular line is tangent: it must be perpendicular at a point on the circle.
  • Using equal tangent lengths from different points: the theorem compares two tangents drawn from the same external point.
  • Putting the wrong side as hypotenuse: \(OP\), opposite the right angle at contact, is the hypotenuse.
  • Forgetting that length is positive: reject a negative square root for a segment length.
  • Confusing radius and diameter: use \(r\), not \(2r\), in the right triangle from the centre to contact.

9. Examination tips

  • Mark the contact point and right angle before applying Pythagoras.
  • For equal tangents, label both segments from the same external point and cite Theorem 10.2.
  • For the proof, state the three RHS facts: equal radii, right angles, and common hypotenuse.
  • When a chord touches an inner concentric circle, the inner radius is perpendicular to the chord and reaches its midpoint.
  • Write a short reason beside each equality: radii of same circle, tangent-radius theorem, Pythagoras, or RHS/CPCT.
  • Include units with every calculated length.

10. Quick revision

Secant: a line meeting a circle at two points.

Tangent: a line meeting a circle at one point.

Theorem 10.1: the radius through the contact point is perpendicular to the tangent there.

Number of tangents: inside \(0\); on circle \(1\); outside \(2\).

Theorem 10.2: tangent lengths from the same external point are equal.

\[OP^2=OT^2+PT^2,\qquad PT=\sqrt{OP^2-OT^2}.\]

11. Self-practice

Original revision problems; use the chapter theorems and right-triangle reasoning.

  1. A circle has radius \(10\text{ cm}\). An external point is \(26\text{ cm}\) from the centre. Find a tangent length.
  2. Two tangent segments from \(P\) have lengths \(3x+2\) and \(5x-10\). Find \(x\) and their common length.
  3. The angle between two tangents from one external point is \(70^\circ\). Find the angle between the radii to the points of contact.
  4. Concentric circles have radii \(12\text{ cm}\) and \(5\text{ cm}\). Find the length of a chord of the larger circle tangent to the smaller circle.
  5. A point is \(4\text{ cm}\) from the centre of a circle of radius \(6\text{ cm}\). How many tangents can pass through it?
  6. The radius of a circle is \(11\text{ cm}\). A line is \(11\text{ cm}\) from the centre, measured perpendicularly. Classify the line.
Check your answers
  1. \(24\text{ cm}\)
  2. \(x=6\); each tangent is \(20\) units.
  3. \(110^\circ\)
  4. \(2\sqrt{119}\text{ cm}\) (about \(21.82\text{ cm}\)).
  5. Zero; the point is inside the circle.
  6. Tangent, because the perpendicular distance from the centre equals the radius.
