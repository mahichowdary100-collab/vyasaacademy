Class 10 Mathematics – Chapter 6: Triangles

This chapter is about similarity: figures that share the same shape but not necessarily the same size. From shadows cast by buildings and scale drawings of maps to huge banners and miniature models, similar triangles let us find distances we can hardly measure directly. In Chapter 5 – Arithmetic Progressions you worked with steady patterns and fixed jumps; in Chapter 2 – Polynomials you compared coefficients and values. This chapter turns that careful reasoning onto the side lengths and angles of triangles, introducing the Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT), the AA / SSS / SAS criteria of similarity and the beautiful fact that similar triangles keep their perimeters in one ratio while pushing their areas in the square of that ratio. By the end you will be able to decide when two triangles are similar, prove they are, and use the results to size real-world objects from a single proportion.

1. Introduction to Triangles and Similarity

A triangle is a three-sided polygon. Every triangle has three sides, three angles and an important fixed property: the sum of its three interior angles is always \(180^{\circ}\). If you shrink or stretch a triangle uniformly, its angles stay exactly the same while its sides grow or shorten in one common proportion — that is the birth of similarity.

Similar figures surround us: a photo and its enlargement, the map of a city and the city itself, a toy car and the real car, a shadow and the object that casts it. In each case the shape is identical but the size differs by a fixed scale factor. This chapter formalises that idea for triangles and uses it to measure heights, widths and scaled drawings without ever walking to the object you are measuring.

Similar figures

Same shape, different (or equal) sizes.

Scale factor

The fixed multiplier \(k\) applied to every side.

Similar triangles

Corresponding angles equal, corresponding sides proportional.

BPT

A line parallel to a side divides the other two proportionally.

What you will learn in this chapter:

  • Tell similar figures apart from congruent ones.
  • Write and interpret the similarity symbol \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) and always use the correct vertex order.
  • Name corresponding sides and angles confidently.
  • State, prove and apply the Basic Proportionality Theorem and its converse.
  • Decide similarity using the AA, SSS and SAS criteria.
  • Connect perimeters (same ratio) and areas (squared ratio) of similar triangles.
  • Solve shadow, map, model and surveying problems with a single proportion.
  • Answer competency and case-based questions with reasoning, not just calculation.

2. Similar Figures

Two figures are similar when they have exactly the same shape. Size does not matter: a small equilateral triangle and a large equilateral triangle are similar because they differ only by a uniform scale. The circles below and the pair of triangles show the idea — each pair has the same shape but a different size.

Similar figures

Two figures having the same shape (not necessarily the same size) are called similar figures. For polygons this means two things together: their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are in the same ratio.

Which figures are always similar?

FigureAlways similar?Why
Any two line segmentsYesSame shape; which is "larger" is just a scale
Any two circlesYesEvery circle has the same shape
Any two squaresYesAll angles \(90^{\circ}\) and all sides equal ratio
Any two equilateral trianglesYesAll three angles \(60^{\circ}\) in each
Any two rectanglesNoOnly if length : breadth ratios match
Any two isosceles trianglesNoOnly if the vertex angles, hence the ratios, match
Careful

Same shape is not the same as same size. Two figures that are identical in both shape and size are called congruent; congruent figures are a special case of similarity where the scale factor is exactly \(1\).

3. Similar Figures vs Congruent Figures

Congruence compares size as well as shape; similarity compares only shape. A scaled-up photograph is similar to the original but not congruent to it, while a photocopy at 100% is both.

Memory hook

Congruent = same size and shape; similar = same shape. Every pair of congruent figures is similar (with scale factor 1), but most similar figures are not congruent.

4. Similar Triangles — Notation and Meaning

We write \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\), read as "triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF". The order of the letters is not optional: it tells you exactly which parts correspond.

Correspondence rule

In \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\):

  • Vertex \(A\) corresponds to vertex \(D\), so \(\angle A = \angle D\).
  • Vertex \(B\) corresponds to vertex \(E\), so \(\angle B = \angle E\).
  • Vertex \(C\) corresponds to vertex \(F\), so \(\angle C = \angle F\).
  • Side \(AB\) corresponds to side \(DE\) and \(\dfrac{AB}{DE} = \dfrac{BC}{EF} = \dfrac{CA}{FD}\).
\(\triangle ABC\)Corresponds to\(\triangle DEF\)
Vertex \(A\)↔Vertex \(D\)
Vertex \(B\)↔Vertex \(E\)
Vertex \(C\)↔Vertex \(F\)
Side \(AB\)↔Side \(DE\)
Side \(BC\)↔Side \(EF\)
Side \(CA\)↔Side \(FD\)
\(\angle A\)↔\(\angle D\)
Common error

Writing \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) while using the side pairing \(AB\leftrightarrow EF\) is wrong. The written order decides the correspondence: \(A\leftrightarrow D\), \(B\leftrightarrow E\), \(C\leftrightarrow F\), and the ratios must be written in that order.

5. Corresponding Sides and Angles

Corresponding parts of similar triangles behave predictably. The two triangles below are similar: \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\). Angles that sit at corresponding positions are equal, and the three pairs of corresponding sides share one common ratio.

Worked example (corresponding sides)

\(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with \(AB = 6\text{ cm}\), \(BC = 9\text{ cm}\), \(CA = 12\text{ cm}\) and \(DE = 4\text{ cm}\). Find \(EF\) and \(FD\).

The common ratio is \(\dfrac{AB}{DE} = \dfrac{6}{4} = \dfrac{3}{2}\). Hence

\[EF = BC \times \frac{2}{3} = 9 \times \frac{2}{3} = 6\text{ cm},\qquad FD = CA \times \frac{2}{3} = 12 \times \frac{2}{3} = 8\text{ cm}\]

So \(EF = 6\) cm and \(FD = 8\) cm ✓ (check: \(6,9,12\) is every corresponding side of \(4,6,8\) multiplied by \(\frac{3}{2}\)).

Tip

To find the common ratio, use the pair of sides where both lengths are given, then apply that single ratio to every other corresponding pair.

6. Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT)

The Basic Proportionality Theorem, also called Thales' Theorem, is the backbone of this chapter. It says that a line drawn inside a triangle, parallel to one side, cuts the other two sides in the same ratio.

Basic Proportionality Theorem

If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides at distinct points, then the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.

In \(\triangle ABC\), if \(DE \parallel BC\) with \(D\) on \(AB\) and \(E\) on \(AC\), then:

\[ \boxed{\frac{AD}{DB} = \frac{AE}{EC}} \]

Proof of the BPT

Theorem — Basic Proportionality Theorem
Given: \(\triangle ABC\), a line \(DE \parallel BC\), where \(D \in AB\) and \(E \in AC\).
To prove: \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\).
Construction: Join \(BE\) and \(CD\). Draw perpendiculars \(BG \perp AD\) (equivalently, use the altitude from \(E\) to \(AB\)).
Proof: The triangles \(\triangle ADE\) and \(\triangle DBE\) stand on the bases \(AD\) and \(DB\) with the same altitude from \(E\), so
\[ \frac{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ADE)}{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DBE)} = \frac{AD}{DB} \qquad (1)\]
Similarly \(\triangle ADE\) and \(\triangle DEC\) share the altitude from \(D\) on the bases \(AE\) and \(EC\):
\[ \frac{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ADE)}{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEC)} = \frac{AE}{EC} \qquad (2)\]
Now \(\triangle DBE\) and \(\triangle DEC\) have the same base \(DE\) and lie between the same parallels \(DE\) and \(BC\), so their areas are equal.
Dividing (1) by (2) and using the equal areas:
\[ \frac{AD}{DB} = \frac{AE}{EC} \qquad \blacksquare\]
Worked example (apply BPT)

In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE \parallel BC\), \(AD = 3\text{ cm}\), \(DB = 5\text{ cm}\) and \(AE = 4.5\text{ cm}\). Find \(EC\).

By BPT, \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\), so \(\dfrac{3}{5} = \dfrac{4.5}{EC}\).

\[EC = \frac{5 \times 4.5}{3} = 7.5\text{ cm}\]

So \(EC = 7.5\) cm.

Worked example (another segment)

In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE \parallel BC\), \(AD = 2\text{ cm}\), \(DB = 3\text{ cm}\), \(AC = 10\text{ cm}\). Find \(AC\)'s split into \(AE\) and \(EC\) — that is, find \(AE\) and \(EC\).

Using \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC} = \dfrac{2}{3}\), the whole of \(AC\) is divided in the ratio \(2 : 3\):

\[AE = \frac{2}{2+3} \times 10 = 4\text{ cm},\qquad EC = \frac{3}{2+3} \times 10 = 6\text{ cm}\]

Check: \(4 + 6 = 10\) cm ✓, and \(\frac{AD}{DB} = \frac{2}{3} = \frac{AE}{EC} = \frac{4}{6}\) ✓.

Careful

BPT needs the line to be parallel to a side. A line that merely cuts two sides without being parallel to the third side does not give the equal-ratio property.

7. Converse of BPT

The reverse statement is equally powerful: if a line splits two sides in the same ratio, the line must be parallel to the third side.

Converse of BPT

If a line divides two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side.

In \(\triangle ABC\), if \(D\) lies on \(AB\), \(E\) lies on \(AC\), and \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\), then \(DE \parallel BC\).

Worked example (converse)

In \(\triangle ABC\), \(AD = 2.4\text{ cm}\), \(DB = 3.6\text{ cm}\), \(AE = 2.8\text{ cm}\), \(EC = 4.2\text{ cm}\). Is \(DE \parallel BC\)?

Compute both ratios:

\[\frac{AD}{DB} = \frac{2.4}{3.6} = \frac{2}{3},\qquad \frac{AE}{EC} = \frac{2.8}{4.2} = \frac{2}{3}\]

The ratios are equal, so by the converse of BPT, \(DE \parallel BC\).

Tip

Simplify each ratio before comparing. Writing \(2.4 : 3.6 = 2 : 3\) and \(2.8 : 4.2 = 2 : 3\) makes the equality obvious and carries marks in board solutions.

8. Criteria for Similarity: AA, SSS and SAS

Checking all six conditions (three angles and three sides) is tedious. The three criteria below let you prove similarity from much less information. They are the similarity counterparts of the congruence rules, with "equal sides" relaxed to "proportional sides".

Criterion 1 — AA similarity

If two angles of one triangle are equal to two corresponding angles of another triangle, the triangles are similar. (The third angles are then automatically equal, since each sum is \(180^{\circ}\).)

Criterion 2 — SSS similarity

If the three sides of one triangle are proportional to the three sides of another triangle, the triangles are similar.

Criterion 3 — SAS similarity

If one angle of a triangle equals one angle of another triangle and the two sides including that angle are proportional, the triangles are similar. The equal angle must be the included angle — the angle between the two sides being compared.

The included angle matters

Two sides and a non-included angle (an "SSA" match) does not guarantee similarity. In SAS, the given angle must sit between the two sides, exactly as \(\angle A\) lies between \(AB\) and \(AC\).

Worked example (AA)

In \(\triangle ABC\), \(\angle A = 40^{\circ}\) and \(\angle B = 65^{\circ}\). In \(\triangle DEF\), \(\angle D = 40^{\circ}\) and \(\angle E = 65^{\circ}\). Are the triangles similar?

\(\angle A = \angle D = 40^{\circ}\) and \(\angle B = \angle E = 65^{\circ}\), so by the AA criterion, \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\). (The third angles agree too: both are \(180^{\circ} - 105^{\circ} = 75^{\circ}\).)

Worked example (SSS)

\(\triangle ABC\) has sides \(3, 4, 5.5\) and \(\triangle DEF\) has sides \(6, 8, 11\). Are they similar?

Check the ratios in matching order:

\[\frac{6}{3} = 2,\qquad \frac{8}{4} = 2,\qquad \frac{11}{5.5} = 2\]

All three ratios are equal, so by the SSS criterion, \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with scale factor \(\frac{1}{2}\).

Worked example (SAS)

In \(\triangle ABC\), \(AB = 5\), \(AC = 7\) and \(\angle A = 60^{\circ}\). In \(\triangle DEF\), \(DE = 10\), \(DF = 14\) and \(\angle D = 60^{\circ}\). Are they similar?

The equal angle \(\angle A = \angle D = 60^{\circ}\) is the included angle in both pairs, and

\[\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{1}{2},\qquad \frac{AC}{DF} = \frac{7}{14} = \frac{1}{2}\]

So by the SAS criterion, \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with scale factor \(\frac{1}{2}\).

Tip

Under AA, only two angles are needed, because the third follows for free. Under SSS, all three side-ratios must match. Under SAS, two sides and the included angle match.

9. Important Results

From the definition and the criteria, a cluster of results follows almost immediately. Learn them as a set; board questions reuse them constantly.

ResultStatement
1. TransitivityIf \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle PQR\) and \(\triangle PQR \sim \triangle XYZ\), then \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle XYZ\).
2. Similarity is not equality of sidesSimilar triangles can always be laid one over the other after scaling; equal sides make them congruent, a sub-case.
3. Perimeters in the same ratioIf \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with scale factor \(k\), then their perimeters are also in the ratio \(k : 1\).
4. Areas in the squared ratioThe areas of similar triangles are in the square of the ratio of corresponding sides.
5. Equal angles, proportional sidesCorresponding angles of similar triangles are equal and corresponding sides are proportional — the two defining properties always occur together.
6. Scale factor below 1If the scale factor \(k < 1\), the image triangle is smaller; if \(k > 1\) it is larger; if \(k = 1\) the triangles are congruent.
Worked example (perimeters)

\(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with \(AB = 8\), \(BC = 10\), \(CA = 6\) and \(DE = 12\). Find the perimeter of \(\triangle DEF\).

Scale factor = \(\dfrac{DE}{AB} = \dfrac{12}{8} = \dfrac{3}{2}\). Perimeter of \(\triangle ABC = 8 + 10 + 6 = 24\).

\[P(\triangle DEF) = 24 \times \frac{3}{2} = 36\]

So the perimeter of \(\triangle DEF\) is 36. Direct check: \(12 + 15 + 9 = 36\) ✓.

10. Ratio of Areas of Similar Triangles

This is one of the most frequently examined results of the chapter. Whenever triangles are similar, their sides stay in one ratio \(k\), but their areas grow by \(k^{2}\) because area depends on two linear dimensions (base and height).

Ratio of areas

If \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\), with corresponding sides in the ratio \(k\), then

\[ \boxed{\frac{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ABC)}{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF)} = k^{2} = \left(\frac{AB}{DE}\right)^{2} = \left(\frac{BC}{EF}\right)^{2} = \left(\frac{CA}{FD}\right)^{2}} \]

Derivation

Ratio of areas of similar triangles
Given: \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with \(\dfrac{BC}{EF} = k\).
Construction: Draw the altitudes \(AP\) and \(DQ\) on the bases \(BC\) and \(EF\).
Proof: Because all corresponding angles are equal (in particular \(\angle B = \angle E\)), the smaller right triangles formed by the altitudes are similar by AA, so the altitudes are in the same ratio as the bases:
\[ \frac{AP}{DQ} = \frac{BC}{EF} = k\]
Now compare the areas using \(\text{area} = \frac{1}{2}\times \text{base} \times \text{height}\):
\[ \frac{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ABC)}{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF)} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} \cdot BC \cdot AP}{\frac{1}{2} \cdot EF \cdot DQ} = \frac{BC}{EF} \times \frac{AP}{DQ} = k \times k = k^{2}\]
Hence the areas are in the ratio \(k^{2}\), the square of the ratio of corresponding sides. \(\blacksquare\)
Worked example (areas from sides)

\(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) and the ratio of corresponding sides is \(2 : 5\) (i.e. \(k = \frac{2}{5}\), \(\triangle ABC\) smaller). If \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ABC) = 20\text{ cm}^{2}\), find \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF)\).

Areas are in the ratio \(k^{2} = 4 : 25\). With \(\triangle ABC = 20\text{ cm}^{2}\),

\[\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF) = 20 \times \frac{25}{4} = 125\text{ cm}^{2}\]

So \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF) = \mathbf{125\text{ cm}^{2}}\).

Worked example (sides from areas)

Two similar triangles have areas \(49\text{ cm}^{2}\) and \(81\text{ cm}^{2}\). If a side of the smaller triangle is \(7\text{ cm}\), find the corresponding side of the larger triangle.

\[\frac{\text{smaller area}}{\text{larger area}} = \frac{49}{81} \implies k^{2} = \frac{49}{81} \implies k = \frac{7}{9}\]

Corresponding side of larger \(= 7 \times \frac{9}{7} = 9\text{ cm}\).

So the corresponding side is 9 cm.

Warning

For similar figures the perimeters use the ratio \(k\), but the areas use \(k^{2}\). A scale factor \(3\) multiplies sides by \(3\) and areas by \(9\). Mixing these two is the single most common error in this chapter.

11. Important Proofs

Board papers repeatedly ask for whole proofs. Practise boiling each one down to Given, To prove, Construction and Proof steps.

Proof A — Converse of BPT
Given: \(\triangle ABC\), with \(D \in AB\), \(E \in AC\) such that \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\).
To prove: \(DE \parallel BC\).
Construction: Suppose \(DE\) is not parallel to \(BC\). Through \(D\), draw \(DF \parallel BC\), meeting \(AC\) at \(F\).
Proof: Since \(DF \parallel BC\), BPT gives \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AF}{FC}\).
But we are given \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\), so \(\dfrac{AF}{FC} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\).
Adding 1 to both sides, \(\dfrac{AF + FC}{FC} = \dfrac{AE + EC}{EC} \implies \dfrac{AC}{FC} = \dfrac{AC}{EC}\), so \(FC = EC\).
Point \(F\) on \(AC\) is therefore exactly point \(E\), meaning the line through \(D\) parallel to \(BC\) is none other than \(DE\). Hence \(DE \parallel BC\). \(\blacksquare\)
Proof B — Ratio of side lengths in a repeated bisector configuration
Given: \(\triangle ABC\) and a line \(DE \parallel BC\) meeting \(AB\) at \(D\) and \(AC\) at \(E\), with \(AD = 2\cdot DB\).
To prove: \(AE = 2\cdot EC\).
Proof: By BPT, \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\).
Since \(AD = 2 DB\), the left ratio is \(\dfrac{2DB}{DB} = 2\). Therefore \(\dfrac{AE}{EC} = 2\), that is \(AE = 2 EC\). \(\blacksquare\)
Proof C — Line through the midpoint is parallel when it halves the other side
Given: \(\triangle ABC\) with \(D\) the midpoint of \(AB\), and a line through \(D\) meeting \(AC\) at \(E\) such that \(AE = EC\).
To prove: \(DE \parallel BC\).
Proof: \(D\) is the midpoint of \(AB\), so \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = 1\). Also \(E\) is the midpoint of \(AC\), so \(\dfrac{AE}{EC} = 1\).
Thus \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\), and by the converse of BPT, \(DE \parallel BC\). \(\blacksquare\)
Board presentation tip

Write "By BPT" / "By converse of BPT" at the exact line you apply them. Even a correct answer loses marks if the theorem name and the ratio it produces are not written explicitly.

12. Solved Examples

Twelve fully worked problems covering every idea in the chapter. Try each one on paper before reading the solution.

Solved Example 1 — Decide similarity (SSS)
Are two triangles with sides \(6, 9, 12\) and \(4, 6, 8\) similar?
Solution. Arrange correspondingly and compare:
\[\frac{6}{4} = \frac{3}{2},\qquad \frac{9}{6} = \frac{3}{2},\qquad \frac{12}{8} = \frac{3}{2}\]
All three ratios are equal, so by the SSS criterion the triangles are similar, smaller triplet being the \(\frac{2}{3}\) scale of the larger.
Solved Example 2 — Find missing sides
\(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with \(AB = 6\), \(BC = 9\), \(CA = 12\), \(DE = 4\). Find \(EF\) and \(FD\).
Solution. Scale factor \(\dfrac{DE}{AB} = \dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3}\). Hence \(EF = 9 \times \frac{2}{3} = 6\) and \(FD = 12 \times \frac{2}{3} = 8\).
Answer: \(EF = 6\), \(FD = 8\).
Solved Example 3 — Decide similarity (AA)
In \(\triangle ABC\), \(\angle A = 55^{\circ}\), \(\angle B = 68^{\circ}\). In \(\triangle DEF\), \(\angle D = 55^{\circ}\), \(\angle E = 57^{\circ}\). Are the triangles similar?
Solution. \(\angle C = 180^{\circ} - 55^{\circ} - 68^{\circ} = 57^{\circ}\) and \(\angle F = 180^{\circ} - 55^{\circ} - 57^{\circ} = 68^{\circ}\).
So \(\angle A = \angle D = 55^{\circ}\) and \(\angle C = \angle F = 57^{\circ}\). By AA, \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\).
Solved Example 4 — Apply BPT
In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE \parallel BC\), \(AD = 3\), \(DB = 7\), \(AE = 4.8\). Find \(EC\).
Solution. \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC} \implies \dfrac{3}{7} = \dfrac{4.8}{EC} \implies EC = \dfrac{7 \times 4.8}{3} = 11.2\).
Answer: \(EC = 11.2\).
Solved Example 5 — Converse of BPT
In \(\triangle ABC\), \(AD = 2.5\), \(DB = 5\), \(AE = 3\), \(EC = 6\). Is \(DE \parallel BC\)?
Solution. \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{2.5}{5} = \frac{1}{2}\) and \(\dfrac{AE}{EC} = \dfrac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}\). They are equal, so by the converse of BPT, \(DE \parallel BC\).
Solved Example 6 — Areas from the side ratio
\(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with sides in the ratio \(2 : 3\). If \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ABC) = 40\text{ cm}^{2}\), find \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF)\).
Solution. \(\dfrac{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ABC)}{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF)} = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{2} = \frac{4}{9}\), so \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF) = 40 \times \frac{9}{4} = 90\text{ cm}^{2}\).
Answer: \(90\text{ cm}^{2}\).
Solved Example 7 — Areas from the side ratio, reverse
Similar triangles have side ratio \(3 : 7\); the larger has area \(98\text{ cm}^{2}\). Find the smaller area.
Solution. Ratio of areas \(= (3 : 7)^{2} = 9 : 49\). Smaller area \(= 98 \times \frac{9}{49} = 18\text{ cm}^{2}\).
Answer: \(18\text{ cm}^{2}\).
Solved Example 8 — Shadow and height
A \(1.5\text{ m}\) tall boy casts a shadow \(2\text{ m}\) long. At the same moment a building casts a shadow \(40\text{ m}\) long. Find the building's height.
Solution. At the same time of day the sun makes similar triangles, so heights and shadows are proportional:
\[\frac{\text{height}}{\text{shadow}} = \frac{1.5}{2} = \frac{h}{40} \implies h = \frac{1.5 \times 40}{2} = 30\text{ m}\]
Answer: the building is 30 m tall.
Solved Example 9 — Corresponding sides and third side
\(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) with \(AB = 8\), \(BC = 10\), \(DE = 12\). Find \(EF\).
Solution. Ratio \(\dfrac{AB}{DE} = \dfrac{8}{12} = \frac{2}{3}\). So \(\dfrac{BC}{EF} = \frac{2}{3}\), giving \(EF = 10 \times \frac{3}{2} = 15\).
Answer: \(EF = 15\).
Solved Example 10 — BPT with a ratio split
In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE \parallel BC\), \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{2}{3}\), and \(AE = 6\). Find \(EC\).
Solution. \(\dfrac{AE}{EC} = \dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{2}{3} \implies \dfrac{6}{EC} = \dfrac{2}{3} \implies EC = 9\).
Answer: \(EC = 9\).
Solved Example 11 — Perimeters and areas combined
\(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\), perimeters \(24\) and \(36\). If \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ABC) = 20\text{ cm}^{2}\), find \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF)\).
Solution. \(k = \dfrac{24}{36} = \frac{2}{3}\) so \(\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF) = 20 \times \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2} = 20 \times \frac{9}{4} = 45\text{ cm}^{2}\).
Answer: \(45\text{ cm}^{2}\).
Solved Example 12 — SSS similarity check
Are triangles with sides \(4, 6, 8\) and \(6, 9, 12\) similar?
Solution. \(\dfrac{6}{4} = \frac{3}{2}\), \(\dfrac{9}{6} = \frac{3}{2}\), \(\dfrac{12}{8} = \frac{3}{2}\).
All ratios equal, so yes, by the SSS criterion with scale factor \(\frac{3}{2}\).

13. Application-Based Problems

Similarity's power is measurement at a distance. The pattern is always the same: identify two similar triangles, write the proportion, and solve for the unknown length. The diagrams below show the three classic setups: a shadow, a survey across a river, and a scaled drawing.

Shadows: heights from shadows

Application 1 — Tree by its shadow

A \(1.8\text{ m}\) girl casts a shadow \(2.4\text{ m}\) long. The shadow of a tree at the same moment is \(12\text{ m}\). Height of tree \(= \dfrac{1.8}{2.4} \times 12 = 9\text{ m}\). So the tree is 9 m tall.

Surveying: measuring a distance you cannot walk

Application 2 — River width by similar triangles

To find the width of a river, a surveyor forms two similar triangles sharing vertex \(A\). A small triangle with base \(2\text{ m}\) gives a scale of \(1 : 12\) against the full triangle, whose base is the river width \(W\). So \(W = 2 \times 12 = 24\text{ m}\). The river is 24 m wide.

Scale drawings and maps

Application 3 — Map scale

On a tourist map, \(1\text{ cm}\) represents \(5\text{ km}\). Two towns are \(4.6\text{ cm}\) apart on the map. Actual distance \(= 4.6 \times 5 = 23\text{ km}\). The towns are 23 km apart.

Application 4 — Model car

A model car is \(12\text{ cm}\) long and represents a real car of length \(4.8\text{ m}\). Scale = \(12\text{ cm} : 480\text{ cm} = 1 : 40\). The model is built at one fortieth scale.

Application 5 — Photograph enlargement

A photo is \(8\text{ cm}\) tall and \(12\text{ cm}\) wide. It is enlarged so the height becomes \(20\text{ cm}\). Scale factor \(= \dfrac{20}{8} = 2.5\), so the new width \(= 12 \times 2.5 = 30\text{ cm}\).

Application 6 — BPT on a sided ladder rest

A triangular bracket \(ABC\) has a strut \(DE \parallel BC\) with \(AD = 6\), \(DB = 12\), \(AE = 8\). By BPT, \(\dfrac{AE}{EC} = \dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{1}{2}\), so \(EC = 16\). The whole bracket arm \(AC = 8 + 16 = 24\).

Application 7 — Logo scaling

A small triangular logo has area \(12\text{ cm}^{2}\). The same logo is printed at 3 times the scale. Area scales by \(3^{2} = 9\), so the enlarged logo occupies \(12 \times 9 = 108\text{ cm}^{2}\).

Application 8 — Flagpole on the field

A vertical pole \(2\text{ m}\) tall casts a shadow \(2.5\text{ m}\). The flagpole's shadow at the same time is \(30\text{ m}\). Height of flagpole \(= \dfrac{2}{2.5} \times 30 = 24\text{ m}\). So the flagpole is 24 m tall.

14. Competency and Reasoning Questions

Competency items test why the tools work and whether you can justify a statement, not just compute. These questions each carry the necessary reasoning.

Why AA is enough

Two triangles have two equal pairs of angles. Why does that force similarity, without checking the third angle or any side?
Reasoning. Each angle sum is \(180^{\circ}\), so the third angles are automatically equal (\(180^{\circ} -\) the two known ones). Two equal angles fix the whole shape; sides then must be proportional, giving similarity.

Congruent vs similar

Every pair of congruent triangles is also similar. Explain the scale factor involved.
Reasoning. Congruent triangles share all angles (equal) and all sides (equal). For similar triangles, equal sides mean a common ratio of \(1:1\), i.e. scale factor \(k = 1\).

Perimeters follow the side ratio

If the ratio of corresponding sides of two similar triangles is \(5 : 6\), what is the ratio of their perimeters? Justify.
Reasoning. A perimeter is the sum of the three sides, and each side is scaled by the same \(k\). Summing the three scaled sides gives \(k\) times the original perimeter, so the perimeter ratio is also \(5 : 6\).

Rectangles: when are they similar?

Two rectangles have the same area. Must they be similar?
Reasoning. No. Similarity needs proportional sides (same length : breadth ratio), not equal areas. A \(2 \times 6\) and a \(3 \times 4\) rectangle both have area 12 but their side ratios \(1:3\) and \(3:4\) differ, so they are not similar.

Why vertex order matters

Why must the correspondence be written in order in \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\)?
Reasoning. The symbol lists the matching vertices: \(A\leftrightarrow D\), \(B\leftrightarrow E\), \(C\leftrightarrow F\). A scrambled order like \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle EFD\) would assert \(\angle A = \angle E\), which is generally false and would give the wrong side ratios.

Trap: equal shapes, different facts

A student claims, "If all angles of two triangles are equal, the triangles are congruent." Is that right?
Reasoning. No. Equal angles give similarity, not congruence. Congruence additionally requires equal (not merely proportional) sides; a large and a small equilateral triangle have equal angles but different sides.

Scaling images smaller or bigger

A triangle is scaled by \(k = 0.5\). Does its area shrink by 0.5?
Reasoning. No. Area multiplies by \(k^{2} = 0.25\), so the image has a quarter of the area. A scale of a half shrinks sides to half but area to one quarter.

BPT needs parallelism

In \(\triangle ABC\), a line cuts \(AB\) at \(P\) and \(AC\) at \(Q\) with \(AP = 2\), \(PB = 3\), \(AQ = 2.5\), \(QC = 4\). Is \(PQ \parallel BC\)?
Reasoning. Check \(\dfrac{AP}{PB} = \dfrac{2}{3}\) but \(\dfrac{AQ}{QC} = \dfrac{2.5}{4} = \dfrac{5}{8}\). The ratios are not equal, so by the converse of BPT, \(PQ\) is not parallel to \(BC\).

Areas rise faster than sides

Explain why a poster scaled up by factor 3 needs 9 times the paint.
Reasoning. Paint covers area, and for a fixed shape the area scales with the square of the linear scale factor. Factor 3 in lengths means factor \(3^{2} = 9\) in area, hence 9 times the paint.

Choosing the criterion

You know all three sides of a triangle and only two sides of another. Which similarity criterion can you possibly use?
Reasoning. With one side and two sides given, SSS is unusable (needs three pairs). If the included angle between the known sides is also known, SAS may apply; otherwise you cannot decide from the given data.

15. Case-Based Examples

Case studies present a situation and 2-3 related sub-questions. Read the whole case first, then answer using only its data, showing all working.

Case Study 1 — Shadows on a Playground

At 4:00 pm, a student of height \(1.5\text{ m}\) casts a shadow \(2\text{ m}\) long. Two playground structures, a slide and a swing frame, cast shadows of \(8\text{ m}\) and \(10\text{ m}\) respectively at the same moment.

Question 1

State the similarity criterion that lets you compare the student and the slide.

Question 2

Find the height of the slide.

Question 3

Find the height of the swing frame.

Solution.

(1)  The sun rays are parallel, so the two shadow triangles share equal angles; by the AA criterion the triangles are similar.

(2)  \(\dfrac{\text{height}}{\text{shadow}} = \dfrac{1.5}{2} = \dfrac{h_s}{8} \implies h_s = \dfrac{1.5 \times 8}{2} = 6\text{ m}\). The slide is 6 m tall.

(3)  \(h_f = \dfrac{1.5 \times 10}{2} = 7.5\text{ m}\). The swing frame is 7.5 m tall.

Case Study 2 — The Town Map

A town map is drawn to scale \(1 : 5000\). On the map, the park is a rectangle \(6\text{ cm}\) by \(4\text{ cm}\), and the distance between the park and the museum is \(8\text{ cm}\).

Question 1

Interpret the scale \(1 : 5000\) in words.

Question 2

Find the actual dimensions of the park.

Question 3

Find the actual distance between the park and the museum.

Solution.

(1)  Every \(1\text{ cm}\) on the map equals \(5000\text{ cm} = 50\text{ m}\) on the ground.

(2)  Length \(= 6 \times 50 = 300\text{ m}\), breadth \(= 4 \times 50 = 200\text{ m}\). The park is 300 m by 200 m.

(3)  Distance \(= 8 \times 50 = 400\text{ m}\). The museum is 400 m from the park.

Case Study 3 — Surveying the River

A surveying team wants the width of a river. They mark point \(A\) on one bank and create the similar pair of triangles shown in Section 13, with parallel bases \(BC \parallel DE\). On the small triangle \(ABC\), the side \(BC = 2\text{ m}\). The full triangle \(ADE\) has base \(DE\) crossing the river at a measured value of \(9\text{ m}\) in the drawing scale where the small base was measured.

Question 1

State the similarity criterion used between \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle ADE\).

Question 2

Find the scale factor from the small triangle to the full triangle.

Question 3

If the river width is the base \(DE\) and the survey recorded \(DE = 24\text{ m}\), verify that \(BC\) stays consistent with the two given measurements.

Solution.

(1)  Since \(BC \parallel DE\), \(\angle ABC = \angle ADE\) and \(\angle ACB = \angle AED\) (corresponding angles). By the AA criterion, \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle ADE\).

(2)  \(\dfrac{DE}{BC} = \dfrac{24}{2} = 12\), so the scale factor is 12.

(3)  Using the recorded \(DE = 24\text{ m}\) and \(BC = 2\text{ m}\), the ratio \(\dfrac{DE}{BC} = 12\) matches the scale factor found in part (2), so the measurements are consistent.

Case Study 4 — School Banner

A triangular school banner is \(4\text{ m}\) wide at the base and \(1.5\text{ m}\) high. A scaled-down version for a desk flag has a base of \(16\text{ cm}\), using the same shape.

Question 1

Find the scale factor from the banner to the desk flag.

Question 2

Find the height of the desk flag.

Question 3

What is the ratio of the areas of the banner and the desk flag?

Solution.

(1)  Banner base \(4\text{ m} = 400\text{ cm}\); desk flag base \(16\text{ cm}\). Scale factor \(= \dfrac{16}{400} = \dfrac{1}{25}\).

(2)  Desk flag height \(= 150\text{ cm} \times \dfrac{1}{25} = 6\text{ cm}\).

(3)  Areas are in the square of the scale factor: \(\left(\dfrac{1}{25}\right)^{2} = \dfrac{1}{625}\). The banner has 625 times the desk flag's area.

17. Common Examination Mistakes

Common mistakeCorrect method
Writing \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\) in the wrong orderKeep the order so that \(A\leftrightarrow D\), \(B\leftrightarrow E\), \(C\leftrightarrow F\); it fixes every other correspondence
Applying the area ratio \(k\) instead of \(k^{2}\)Areas of similar triangles use the square of the side ratio
Using SAS with a non-included angleThe given angle must lie between the two given sides
Using BPT when the line is not parallel to a sideBPT and its converse both require the parallelism/split to be stated correctly
Saying equal angles alone give congruenceEqual angles give similarity; equal sides give congruence
Forgetting to square when going from areas back to sidesSide ratio is the square root of the area ratio
Mixing units in shadow or map problemsConvert to one unit, e.g. metres, before writing the proportion
Quoting a criterion without its nameWrite "by AA criterion", "by SSS criterion" or "by SAS criterion" in the solution
Not simplifying ratios before comparingReduce \(2.4 : 3.6\) to \(2 : 3\); the equality then shows instantly
Ignoring a given "line is parallel" twice overReuse the same parallel in several sub-parts; it gives one proportion that answers them all

18. CBSE Exam Tips

  • Start every similarity answer by declaring the criterion: "By AA criterion, \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\)". The name gains a mark even before you compute.
  • For BPT questions, write the proportion \(\dfrac{AD}{DB} = \dfrac{AE}{EC}\) first, then substitute; signs and units then check themselves.
  • When the area ratio is given and you need the side ratio, take the square root; when the side ratio is given and you need the area ratio, square it.
  • In shadow problems state that the sun's rays are parallel, so the shadow triangles are similar by AA — a reason instead of a guess.
  • Cross-check proportions numerically: e.g. a scale \(1:12\) must inflate every measured length by 12, and areas by 144.
  • For "is \(PQ \parallel BC\)?" questions, write both ratios, simplify, and compare before concluding.
  • Present proofs in the four blocks (Given / To prove / Construction / Proof); keep each block short and label the theorem used.
  • Convert all lengths to one unit before forming a ratio, and cancel units completely before writing the final answer.

20. Formula and Theorem Box

Basic Proportionality Theorem

\[DE \parallel BC \implies \frac{AD}{DB} = \frac{AE}{EC}\]

Converse of BPT

\[\frac{AD}{DB} = \frac{AE}{EC} \implies DE \parallel BC\]

SSS similarity criterion

\[\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{BC}{EF} = \frac{CA}{FD} \implies \triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\]

SAS similarity criterion

\[\angle A = \angle D \text{ and } \frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{AC}{DF} \implies \triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\]

AA similarity criterion

\[\angle A = \angle D \text{ and } \angle B = \angle E \implies \triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF\]

Perimeters of similar triangles

\[P(\triangle ABC) : P(\triangle DEF) = AB : DE \qquad (k : 1)\]

Areas of similar triangles

\[ \boxed{\frac{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle ABC)}{\operatorname{ar}(\triangle DEF)} = k^{2}} \]

Warning

Keep the correspondence order straight in every formula: ratio of corresponding sides only, included angle only for SAS, and parallel-base only for BPT.
