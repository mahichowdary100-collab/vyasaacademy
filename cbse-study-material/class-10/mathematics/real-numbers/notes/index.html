Class 10 Mathematics – Chapter 1: Real Numbers

This chapter introduces the real number system, prime factorisation, HCF and LCM, and irrational numbers. You will learn to classify numbers, find HCF and LCM using prime factorisation, and prove that certain numbers are irrational.

Notes Practice Paper Quiz Chapter Test

1. Real Numbers – The Number System

In our daily life, we use different types of numbers. Classifying them properly helps us understand their properties. Here are the main types of numbers you must know.

Natural Numbers

The counting numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, … are called Natural Numbers. They are used for counting objects. The set of Natural Numbers is denoted by \(\mathbb{N}\).

Whole Numbers

Natural Numbers together with 0 form the set of Whole Numbers: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, …. The set is denoted by \(\mathbb{W}\).

Integers

Whole Numbers and their negatives form the set of Integers: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …. The set is denoted by \(\mathbb{Z}\).

Rational Numbers

A number that can be expressed in the form \(\dfrac{p}{q}\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are integers and \(q \neq 0\), is called a Rational Number.

Examples: \(\dfrac{2}{3},\; \dfrac{-5}{7},\; 0.5 = \dfrac{1}{2},\; 7 = \dfrac{7}{1}\)

Every integer is a rational number (take \(q = 1\)).

Irrational Numbers

A number that cannot be expressed in the form \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) (where \(p, q\) are integers and \(q \neq 0\)) is called an Irrational Number.

Examples: \(\sqrt{2},\; \sqrt{3},\; \sqrt{5},\; \pi\)

Real Numbers

All Rational Numbers and Irrational Numbers together form the set of Real Numbers, denoted by \(\mathbb{R}\). Every number on the number line is a Real Number.

Number System Hierarchy

The following diagram shows how the number systems are related:

  • Real Numbers
    • Rational Numbers
      • Integers
        • Whole Numbers
          • Natural Numbers (1, 2, 3, 4, …)
      • Non-integer Rational Numbers (e.g. \(\frac{2}{3}, -\frac{5}{7}\))
    • Irrational Numbers (e.g. \(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \pi\))
Remember

Every Natural Number is a Whole Number. Every Whole Number is an Integer. Every Integer is a Rational Number. Every Rational Number is a Real Number. But the reverse is not always true.

2. Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Definition

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.

Before we use this theorem, let us recall what prime and composite numbers are.

Prime Number

A number greater than 1 that has exactly two factors — 1 and itself — is called a Prime Number.

Examples: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, …

Composite Number

A number greater than 1 that has more than two factors is called a Composite Number.

Examples: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, …

The number 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Prime Factorisation

Prime Factorisation means writing a composite number as a product of its prime factors. We find the prime factorisation by repeatedly dividing by the smallest prime that divides the number.

Example – Prime Factorisation of 60
160 is divisible by 2: \(60 \div 2 = 30\)
230 is divisible by 2: \(30 \div 2 = 15\)
315 is divisible by 3: \(15 \div 3 = 5\)
45 is a prime number.

\(60 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5\)

Example – Prime Factorisation of 84
1\(84 \div 2 = 42\)
2\(42 \div 2 = 21\)
3\(21 \div 3 = 7\)
47 is a prime number.

\(84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7\)

Example – Prime Factorisation of 72
1\(72 \div 2 = 36\)
2\(36 \div 2 = 18\)
3\(18 \div 2 = 9\)
4\(9 \div 3 = 3\)
53 is a prime number.

\(72 = 2^3 \times 3^2\)

Why is this important?

Prime factorisation is the foundation for finding HCF and LCM. The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic guarantees that every number has a unique prime factorisation, so the HCF and LCM we calculate are definite and reliable.

3. HCF Using Prime Factorisation

Definition

The Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two or more numbers is the largest number that divides all of them exactly.

Key Rule

HCF = Product of common prime factors with the smallest powers.

Method

  1. Find the prime factorisation of each number.
  2. Identify the prime factors that are common to all the numbers.
  3. For each common prime factor, take the smallest power appearing in the factorisations.
  4. Multiply these to get the HCF.
Example 1 – HCF of 12 and 18

Prime factorisations:

\(12 = 2^2 \times 3\)

\(18 = 2 \times 3^2\)

Common prime factors: 2 and 3

Smallest power of 2: \(2^1\); Smallest power of 3: \(3^1\)

HCF = \(2 \times 3 = 6\)

Example 2 – HCF of 60, 84, and 72

Prime factorisations:

\(60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5\)

\(84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7\)

\(72 = 2^3 \times 3^2\)

Common prime factors: 2 and 3

Smallest power of 2: \(2^2\); Smallest power of 3: \(3^1\)

HCF = \(2^2 \times 3 = 4 \times 3 = 12\)

Example 3 – HCF of 90 and 126

Prime factorisations:

\(90 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 5\)

\(126 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 7\)

Common prime factors: 2 and 3

Smallest power of 2: \(2^1\); Smallest power of 3: \(3^2\)

HCF = \(2 \times 9 = 18\)

Common Error

Students often take the largest power instead of the smallest power for HCF. Remember: for HCF, always choose the smallest power of each common factor.

4. LCM Using Prime Factorisation

Definition

The Least Common Multiple (LCM) of two or more numbers is the smallest number that is exactly divisible by all of them.

Key Rule

LCM = Product of all prime factors with the greatest powers.

Method

  1. Find the prime factorisation of each number.
  2. List all prime factors that appear in any of the factorisations.
  3. For each prime factor, take the greatest power appearing in the factorisations.
  4. Multiply these to get the LCM.
Example 1 – LCM of 12 and 18

\(12 = 2^2 \times 3\)

\(18 = 2 \times 3^2\)

All prime factors: 2 and 3

Greatest power of 2: \(2^2\); Greatest power of 3: \(3^2\)

LCM = \(2^2 \times 3^2 = 4 \times 9 = 36\)

Example 2 – LCM of 60, 84, and 72

\(60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5\)

\(84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7\)

\(72 = 2^3 \times 3^2\)

All prime factors: 2, 3, 5, 7

Greatest powers: \(2^3\), \(3^2\), \(5^1\), \(7^1\)

LCM = \(8 \times 9 \times 5 \times 7 = 2520\)

Example 3 – LCM of 90 and 126

\(90 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 5\)

\(126 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 7\)

All prime factors: 2, 3, 5, 7

Greatest powers: \(2^1\), \(3^2\), \(5^1\), \(7^1\)

LCM = \(2 \times 9 \times 5 \times 7 = 630\)

Common Error

For LCM, students sometimes omit prime factors that appear in only one of the numbers. Remember: for LCM, take all prime factors from all numbers.

5. HCF and LCM Relationship

Important Formula

For any two positive integers \(a\) and \(b\):

\[\text{HCF}(a, b) \times \text{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b\]

This is one of the most useful relationships in Real Numbers. If you know the HCF and can find one of the numbers, you can find the other.

Verification with 12 and 18

HCF(12, 18) = 6, LCM(12, 18) = 36

HCF × LCM = \(6 \times 36 = 216\)

12 × 18 = 216

Both are equal. ✓

Example – Find the other number

The HCF of two numbers is 12 and their LCM is 360. If one number is 72, find the other.

1Let the other number be \(x\).
2HCF × LCM = 72 × \(x\)
3\(12 \times 360 = 72 \times x\)
4\(4320 = 72x\)
5\(x = 4320 \div 72 = 60\)

The other number is 60.

When to Use This Relationship

This formula works only for two positive integers. It does not extend directly to three or more numbers. For three numbers, you must find HCF and LCM using prime factorisation.

6. Applications of HCF and LCM

Many real-life problems can be solved by identifying whether we need HCF or LCM. Knowing which concept to apply is often the key step.

How to Decide: HCF or LCM?
Type of Problem Concept
Largest possible equal groups HCF
Greatest possible size that divides evenly HCF
Smallest number divisible by given numbers LCM
Events occurring together again LCM
Finding the biggest piece / container / group HCF
Finding when things line up / repeat / ring together LCM
Example 1 – Equal Grouping (HCF)

There are 60 chocolates and 84 biscuits. They are to be packed in boxes such that each box has the same number of chocolates and the same number of biscuits. What is the greatest number of boxes possible?

1Each box must divide both 60 and 84 exactly. We need the greatest number of boxes = HCF.
2\(60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5\), \(84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7\)
3HCF = \(2^2 \times 3 = 12\)

Greatest number of boxes = 12 (5 chocolates and 7 biscuits per box).

Example 2 – Smallest Divisible Number (LCM)

Find the smallest number that is exactly divisible by 8, 12, and 15.

1We need the smallest number = LCM.
2\(8 = 2^3\), \(12 = 2^2 \times 3\), \(15 = 3 \times 5\)
3LCM = \(2^3 \times 3 \times 5 = 120\)

The smallest number = 120.

Example 3 – Repeated Events (LCM)

Three bells ring at intervals of 12 minutes, 18 minutes, and 24 minutes. If they ring together at 8:00 AM, when will they ring together again?

1They ring together again after the LCM of their intervals.
2\(12 = 2^2 \times 3\), \(18 = 2 \times 3^2\), \(24 = 2^3 \times 3\)
3LCM = \(2^3 \times 3^2 = 8 \times 9 = 72\) minutes
472 minutes = 1 hour 12 minutes after 8:00 AM

They will ring together again at 9:12 AM.

Example 4 – Greatest Possible Size (HCF)

A rectangular garden is 96 m long and 72 m wide. It is to be divided into square plots of equal size. What is the greatest possible side length of each square plot?

1The side of each square must divide both 96 and 72 exactly. We need the greatest size = HCF.
2\(96 = 2^5 \times 3\), \(72 = 2^3 \times 3^2\)
3HCF = \(2^3 \times 3 = 24\)

Greatest possible side length = 24 m.

Example 5 – Lights Flashing Together (LCM)

Two traffic lights change colours every 48 seconds and 72 seconds. If they change together now, after how many seconds will they change together again?

1Find the LCM of 48 and 72.
2\(48 = 2^4 \times 3\), \(72 = 2^3 \times 3^2\)
3LCM = \(2^4 \times 3^2 = 16 \times 9 = 144\) seconds

They will change together again after 144 seconds (2 minutes 24 seconds).

7. Irrational Numbers

Definition

A number is called an Irrational Number if it cannot be expressed in the form \(\dfrac{p}{q}\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are integers and \(q \neq 0\).

Irrational numbers cannot be written as fractions and have no exact fractional representation. Some important irrational numbers:

  • \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41421356...\)
  • \(\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73205080...\)
  • \(\sqrt{5} \approx 2.23606797...\)
  • \(\pi \approx 3.14159265...\)

Rational vs Irrational Numbers

Feature Rational Numbers Irrational Numbers
Form \(\dfrac{p}{q},\; q \neq 0\) Cannot be written as \(\dfrac{p}{q}\)
Examples \(\frac{1}{3},\; 0.5,\; -7\) \(\sqrt{2},\; \sqrt{3},\; \pi\)
Key Point

The sum, difference, product, or quotient of a rational number and an irrational number is always irrational (provided \(q \neq 0\)). For example, \(2 + \sqrt{3}\) is irrational.

8. Proofs of Irrationality

Proving that a number is irrational is an important exam skill. The method used is called proof by contradiction.

General Strategy
  1. Assume the number is rational. So write it as \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) where \(p, q\) have no common factor (lowest terms) and \(q \neq 0\).
  2. Simplify to get an equation.
  3. Derive a contradiction — show that a prime divides both \(p\) and \(q\), contradicting the assumption that \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) is in lowest terms.
  4. Conclude: the assumption was wrong. Therefore the number is irrational.

Proof 1: \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational

Proof
1Assume \(\sqrt{2}\) is rational. Then \(\sqrt{2} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) where \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime (no common factor other than 1) and \(q \neq 0\).
2Squaring both sides: \(2 = \dfrac{p^2}{q^2}\), so \(p^2 = 2q^2\).
3Since \(p^2 = 2q^2\), \(p^2\) is even. Therefore \(p\) must be even (the square of an odd number is always odd).
4Let \(p = 2k\) for some integer \(k\). Then \(p^2 = 4k^2\). Substituting: \(4k^2 = 2q^2\), so \(q^2 = 2k^2\).
5Since \(q^2 = 2k^2\), \(q^2\) is even. Therefore \(q\) is also even.
6But if both \(p\) and \(q\) are even, they have a common factor of 2. This contradicts our assumption that \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime.
7Therefore our assumption is wrong. \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

Proof 2: \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational

Proof
1Assume \(\sqrt{3} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) where \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime and \(q \neq 0\).
2Squaring: \(3 = \dfrac{p^2}{q^2}\), so \(p^2 = 3q^2\).
3So \(3 \mid p^2\), which means \(3 \mid p\). Let \(p = 3k\).
4Then \(9k^2 = 3q^2\), so \(q^2 = 3k^2\). This means \(3 \mid q^2\), so \(3 \mid q\).
5Both \(p\) and \(q\) are divisible by 3. This contradicts the assumption that \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime.
6Therefore \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational.

Proof 3: \(\sqrt{5}\) is irrational

Proof
1Assume \(\sqrt{5} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) where \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime and \(q \neq 0\).
2Squaring: \(5 = \dfrac{p^2}{q^2}\), so \(p^2 = 5q^2\).
3So \(5 \mid p^2\), which means \(5 \mid p\). Let \(p = 5k\).
4Then \(25k^2 = 5q^2\), so \(q^2 = 5k^2\). This means \(5 \mid q^2\), so \(5 \mid q\).
5Both \(p\) and \(q\) are divisible by 5. This contradicts the assumption that \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime.
6Therefore \(\sqrt{5}\) is irrational.
Writing the Proof in Exams

Always state the assumption clearly, show each step, and explicitly point out the contradiction. End with a concluding statement: "Therefore, our assumption is wrong, and the number is irrational."

9. Important Concepts to Remember

Rational vs Irrational Numbers

A rational number can be written as \(\dfrac{p}{q}\). An irrational number cannot. Together they form the Real Numbers.

Prime vs Composite Numbers

A prime number has exactly two factors (1 and itself). A composite number has more than two factors. 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Prime Factorisation

Writing a composite number as a product of prime numbers. By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, this factorisation is unique (apart from the order).

HCF

The product of common prime factors taken with the smallest powers. It gives the greatest number that divides all the given numbers.

LCM

The product of all prime factors taken with the greatest powers. It gives the smallest number that is divisible by all the given numbers.

HCF–LCM Relationship

For two positive integers \(a\) and \(b\): HCF(\(a, b\)) × LCM(\(a, b\)) = \(a \times b\). This does not work for three or more numbers.

Proving Irrationality

Use proof by contradiction: assume the number is rational, express as \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms, derive that a prime divides both \(p\) and \(q\), and conclude the assumption is false.

10. Important Results

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Every composite number can be expressed as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique apart from the order of the prime factors.

HCF

Product of common prime factors using the smallest powers.

LCM

Product of all prime factors using the greatest powers.

HCF–LCM Relationship

For two positive integers \(a\) and \(b\):

\[\text{HCF}(a, b) \times \text{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b\]

Irrational Numbers

A number that cannot be expressed as \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) (where \(p, q \in \mathbb{Z},\; q \neq 0\)) is irrational. Examples: \(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}\).

11. Solved Examples

Example 1 – Identifying Rational and Irrational Numbers

Question: Which of the following are irrational? (a) \(\sqrt{9}\)   (b) \(\sqrt{10}\)   (c) \(\dfrac{22}{7}\)   (d) \(1 + \sqrt{3}\)

Concept Used: Definition of irrational numbers.

Solution:

  • (a) \(\sqrt{9} = 3\) — this is a rational number.
  • (b) \(\sqrt{10}\) — 10 is not a perfect square, so \(\sqrt{10}\) is irrational.
  • (c) \(\dfrac{22}{7}\) — this is in the form \(\dfrac{p}{q}\), so it is rational.
  • (d) \(1 + \sqrt{3}\) — the sum of a rational number and an irrational number is irrational. So this is irrational.

Final Answer: \(\sqrt{10}\) and \(1 + \sqrt{3}\) are irrational.

Example 2 – Prime Factorisation

Question: Find the prime factorisation of 240.

Concept Used: Prime factorisation.

Solution:

1\(240 \div 2 = 120\)
2\(120 \div 2 = 60\)
3\(60 \div 2 = 30\)
4\(30 \div 2 = 15\)
5\(15 \div 3 = 5\)
65 is prime.

Final Answer: \(240 = 2^4 \times 3 \times 5\)

Example 3 – Finding HCF

Question: Find the HCF of 180 and 252.

Concept Used: HCF using prime factorisation.

Solution:

\(180 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5\)

\(252 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 7\)

Common factors with smallest powers: \(2^2 \times 3^2\)

Final Answer: HCF = \(4 \times 9 = 36\)

Example 4 – Finding LCM

Question: Find the LCM of 24 and 36.

Concept Used: LCM using prime factorisation.

Solution:

\(24 = 2^3 \times 3\)

\(36 = 2^2 \times 3^2\)

All factors with greatest powers: \(2^3 \times 3^2\)

Final Answer: LCM = \(8 \times 9 = 72\)

Example 5 – Using HCF × LCM Relationship

Question: The HCF of two numbers is 14 and their LCM is 1512. If one number is 56, find the other.

Concept Used: HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers.

Solution:

1HCF × LCM = \(56 \times x\), where \(x\) is the unknown number.
2\(14 \times 1512 = 56x\)
3\(21168 = 56x\)
4\(x = 378\)

Final Answer: The other number is 378.

Example 6 – HCF Application

Question: Three tankers contain 403 litres, 465 litres, and 496 litres of water respectively. These are to be filled into identical containers. What is the capacity of the largest container that can measure the water an exact number of times?

Concept Used: HCF gives the largest capacity that divides all quantities exactly.

Solution:

\(403 = 13 \times 31\)

\(465 = 3 \times 5 \times 31\)

\(496 = 2^4 \times 31\)

Common factor: 31

Final Answer: The largest container capacity is 31 litres.

Example 7 – LCM Application

Question: The length, breadth, and height of a room are 825 cm, 675 cm, and 450 cm respectively. Find the longest tape that can measure the dimensions of the room exactly.

Concept Used: HCF of three numbers.

Solution:

\(825 = 3 \times 5^2 \times 11\)

\(675 = 3^3 \times 5^2\)

\(450 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 5^2\)

HCF = \(3 \times 5^2 = 75\)

Final Answer: The longest tape = 75 cm.

Example 8 – Proving Irrationality

Question: Prove that \(3 + 2\sqrt{5}\) is irrational.

Concept Used: Proof by contradiction + the fact that the sum of a rational and irrational number is irrational.

Solution:

1Assume \(3 + 2\sqrt{5}\) is rational. Then \(3 + 2\sqrt{5} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) for coprime integers \(p, q\) with \(q \neq 0\).
2\(2\sqrt{5} = \dfrac{p}{q} - 3 = \dfrac{p - 3q}{q}\)
3\(\sqrt{5} = \dfrac{p - 3q}{2q}\)
4Since \(p, q\) are integers, \(\dfrac{p - 3q}{2q}\) is rational. But \(\sqrt{5}\) is irrational.
5This is a contradiction. Therefore, our assumption is wrong.

Final Answer: \(3 + 2\sqrt{5}\) is irrational.

Example 9 – Mixed Conceptual Problem

Question: Can two numbers have 18 as their HCF and 380 as their LCM? Give a reason.

Concept Used: The relationship HCF × LCM = product of two numbers.

Solution:

If two numbers have HCF 18 and LCM 380, then:

HCF × LCM = \(18 \times 380 = 6840\)

So the product of the two numbers must be 6840.

But the LCM must be a multiple of the HCF. Here, \(380 \div 18 = 21.11...\), which is not a whole number. So 18 does not divide 380.

Final Answer: No. The LCM must always be a multiple of the HCF. Since 18 does not divide 380, no such pair of numbers exists.

Example 10 – Proving \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) is Irrational

Question: Prove that \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) is irrational.

Concept Used: Proof by contradiction.

Solution:

1Assume \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) where \(p, q\) are coprime integers and \(q \neq 0\).
2Cross-multiplying: \(q = p\sqrt{2}\), so \(\sqrt{2} = \dfrac{q}{p}\).
3But \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational (already proved), so it cannot equal \(\dfrac{q}{p}\) (which is rational since \(p, q\) are integers).
4This is a contradiction. Therefore, our assumption is wrong.

Final Answer: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) is irrational.

12. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1 – Confusing HCF and LCM

HCF finds the largest number that divides all given numbers. LCM finds the smallest number divisible by all given numbers. Using the wrong one gives incorrect answers. Ask yourself: "Am I dividing or being divisible?"

Mistake 2 – Wrong Powers in HCF

For HCF, you must take the smallest power of each common factor, not the greatest. For example, HCF of \(2^3 \times 3^2\) and \(2^2 \times 3^3\) is \(2^2 \times 3^2\), not \(2^3 \times 3^3\).

Mistake 3 – Omitting Factors in LCM

For LCM, include all prime factors from all numbers. Students sometimes forget factors that appear in only one number.

Mistake 4 – Incorrect Prime Factorisation

Double-check your factorisation. A common error is stopping before reaching prime factors, e.g. writing \(60 = 4 \times 15\) instead of \(60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5\).

Mistake 5 – Misusing HCF × LCM for Three Numbers

The relationship \(\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = a \times b\) works only for two numbers. It does not work for three or more numbers.

Mistake 6 – Incomplete Irrationality Proofs

Always: (1) State the assumption clearly, (2) show the steps, (3) identify the contradiction explicitly, (4) conclude with a clear statement. Missing any step loses marks.

Mistake 7 – Not Mentioning Coprimality

In irrationality proofs, you must state that \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime (have no common factor). This is what the contradiction refutes.

Mistake 8 – Confusing Rational and Irrational

\(\dfrac{22}{7}\) is rational (it is in the form \(\dfrac{p}{q}\)), even though it is used as an approximation of \(\pi\). Don't assume a fraction-looking number involving 7 is irrational.

13. CBSE Exam Tips – Real Numbers

Exam Tips
  1. Write prime factorisation clearly. Show the division steps; do not just write the answer.
  2. Show all important steps. CBSE awards marks for steps, not just the final answer.
  3. State whether you are using HCF or LCM. Write it explicitly: "We use HCF because…"
  4. In application problems, identify the concept first. Write one line explaining why you are finding HCF or LCM before starting calculations.
  5. For irrationality proofs: State the assumption, show the steps, point out the contradiction, and conclude clearly.
  6. Do not skip reasoning. Even if the answer is obvious to you, write the reasoning. CBSE marks depend on the steps shown.
  7. Check your final answer. Verify that HCF divides all given numbers. Verify that LCM is divisible by all given numbers. Verify that HCF × LCM = product (for two numbers).
  8. Use proper notation. Write \(2^2 \times 3 \times 5\), not \(2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5\). Examiners appreciate concise mathematical notation.
