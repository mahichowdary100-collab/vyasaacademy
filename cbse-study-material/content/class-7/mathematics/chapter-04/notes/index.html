1. Letters can stand for numbers

Arithmetic answers a particular question, such as the cost of five pencils at ₹8 each. Algebra describes the relationship for any price. Let \(x\) be the price in rupees of one pencil. Then five pencils cost \(5x\) rupees. If the price changes, the same expression still works.

Unknown

A letter can stand for a value you have yet to find, such as an unlabelled side length.

Variable

A letter can stand for a value that can change. When \(n\) is a pattern step, use positive whole numbers for \(n\).

Constant

A fixed value does not depend on the variable. In \(a+3\), a person’s age \(a\) may change, but the three-year gap stays fixed.

Learning objectives

  • Define letter-numbers and turn situations into expressions and formulas.
  • Identify signed terms, substitute numerical values and simplify expressions.
  • Explain equivalent expressions and use algebra to discover and justify patterns.

Define each letter and its unit before calculating. Distinct letters can represent distinct quantities without requiring their values to differ. In one calculation, use the same value each time a particular letter occurs.

Placing factors together means multiplication: \(3x=3\times x\), \(ab=a\times b\), and \(2(x+4)=2\times(x+4)\). Put numerical coefficients first. Use the multiplication sign between two plain numerals so \(2\times3\) does not look like 23. Use brackets to show that a whole sum is repeated or divided.

For a classroom example, \(4n+3\) describes \(n\) rows of four seats and three extra seats. For travel, a vehicle moving at \(v\) kilometres per hour for \(t\) hours covers \(vt\) kilometres at a constant speed. In a situation involving equal sharing, \(\frac{m+6}{3}\) divides the entire total, whereas \(m+6/3\) divides only the 6.

Read the order carefully

“Seven less than \(x\)” is \(x-7\). “Seven minus \(x\)” is \(7-x\). Test with \(x=10\) to see why the order matters.

3. Terms, factors, coefficients and constants

As in arithmetic, first read a subtraction as adding an opposite. For \(3x+5y-7\), the three signed terms are \(3x\), \(5y\) and \(-7\). A product’s factors are the parts multiplied together. In \(5y\), 5 is the numerical coefficient and \(y\) is the letter factor. The term \(-7\) is a constant because it has no variable.

3x+5y−7

The signed coefficient of \(-x\) is \(-1\). Top-level addition and subtraction separate terms; a bracketed group stays together until you expand it. For example, \(4(x+1)\) is a single product before expansion. Its expanded form \(4x+4\) has two terms.

Optional vocabulary: After simplifying, an expression with one, two or three terms is often called a monomial, binomial or trinomial, respectively. Here the practical skill is recognising like terms and keeping the signs, rather than classifying advanced polynomials.

4. Substitute and evaluate

  1. Replace every letter with its given number. Put a negative replacement in brackets.
  2. Work inside brackets, then do multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction. Follow the usual order for equal priorities.
  3. Check the result in context and give units where needed.

Two letter-numbers

Evaluate \(4x-2y+3\) at \(x=-2,\ y=5\).

\(4(-2)-2(5)+3=-8-10+3\). The result is \(-15\).

An expression is a calculation such as \(4m+2\); an equation makes an equality statement such as \(4m+2=18\). Evaluating finds the result for a supplied \(m\). Solving would find which \(m\) makes the equality true. This chapter uses formulas and equivalent expressions, without requiring a separate equation-solving procedure.

5. Simplify, expand and check equivalence

Like terms share the same letter part, so their signed coefficients can be added. For example, \(3x+5x=(3+5)x=8x\), and \(7a-2a=5a\). Constants also combine with constants. Unlike terms such as \(2x\) and \(3y\) stay separate: two copies of one quantity and three of another need not be five copies of either. For the same reason, \(x\) and \(x^2\) are unlike terms.

Rules for equivalent forms
RuleUseExample
CommutativeSwap addends or factors2x + 3y = 3y + 2x
AssociativeRegroup addends or factors(a + b) + 5 = a + (b + 5)
DistributiveMultiply every term or factor out a shared multiplier4(x − 2) = 4x − 8
Subtracting a groupReverse the sign of each term inside6 − (x + 3) = 3 − x
Collect like termsKeep the letter part and combine the coefficients5a − 2a = 3a
Additive oppositesCancel equal positive and negative terms3y + (−3y) = 0

Open brackets with the correct signs

\(3(x+2)=3x+6\) because 3 multiplies both terms. In \(8-(x-2)\), subtract every signed term: \(8-x+2=10-x\). A plus in front of a group keeps its signs. Reorder only after treating subtraction as addition of signed terms.

Two expressions are equivalent when they give the same value for every allowed substitution. A property proves a valid rewrite: \(4(x+2)=4x+8\) by distribution. Trying several values can check your work, but a match at a few values is not a proof. One differing value disproves equivalence: \(2x\) and \(x+2\) agree at \(x=2\) but give 6 and 5 at \(x=3\).

To add expressions, collect the signed terms from both. To subtract, first reverse every sign of the expression taken away. A quiz score of \(5p-2q\) plus \(4p-q\) gives \(9p-3q\). Comparing \(5p-2q\) with \(4p-q\) instead gives the difference \(p-q\), so who leads depends on the scoring rules.

For a rectangle of height \(h\) and widths 4 and 6 joined along a full side, add the areas \(4h+6h=10h\). Or use the combined width in \(h(4+6)\). The outer perimeter excludes the shared edge. This area model shows why distributing and collecting like terms describe the same quantity.

In a square, \(s^2\) means \(s\times s\) and gives area in square units. \(2s=s+s\) gives a length. For example, at \(s=5\) the two values are 25 and 10. A regular pentagon or hexagon with side \(s\) has perimeter \(5s\) or \(6s\), respectively.

Quick check

7. Find and explain pattern rules

Growing shapes

Describe what stays and what each step adds. A joined square row uses four sticks first and three more per added square. At step \(n\ge1\), the rule is \(4+3(n-1)=3n+1\).

Number machine

A machine that triples its first input and subtracts its second has rule \(3a-b\). Try inputs (5, 2) for 13 and (4, 7) for 5. Describe a rule in words before writing it.

Repeating cycle

For star, circle, triangle repeated, positions are \(3k+1\), \(3k+2\) and \(3k+3\) after \(k\) full cycles. Divide a position by 3 and use the remainder to locate its place within the cycle.

Grow from term one: If the first term is 8 and each step adds 5, then \(8+5(n-1)=5n+3\). The first position is 1, so only \(n-1\) additions follow the first term. Joined triangles that share one full side use \(3+2(n-1)=2n+1\). For \(n\) separated squares, count \(4n\) sticks or \(4n\) vertices. In joined squares, shared vertices and sides must be counted only once: a row has \(2(n+1)\) distinct vertices.

Calendar proof

A full two-by-two calendar block starting at \(a\) has top row \(a,a+1\), bottom row \(a+7,a+8\). Both diagonal sums simplify to \(2a+8\). In a cross centred at \(a\), its neighbours are \(a-7,a+7,a-1,a+1\). Opposite offsets cancel, so the five entries sum to \(5a\). This proves the rules for every full block or cross.

Numbered grids: For a grid of width 4, row \(r\), column \(c\) is \(4(r-1)+c\). In a positive numbered grid, locate a number by counting complete rows before it. In a rope-cutting investigation, first count how many distinct points the cut reaches. If each stage deliberately puts one additional strand under the blade, stage \(n\) from one strand gives \(n+1\) cut points and \(n+2\) pieces. An end-to-end fold that doubles the strands gives a different pattern. The construction, rather than a short list of totals, justifies the rule.

    9. Step-by-step solved examples

    Try each question before revealing its solution. The set includes 9 basic, 12 intermediate and 9 advanced examples.

    Basic · Examples 1–9

    Example 1 · A changing age

    Question: Mira is five years older than Arun. Arun is a years old. Write Mira’s age.

    1. Rule: Add the fixed age difference to the changing age.
    2. Work: For a = 12, the expression gives 12 + 5. If a changes, the five-year difference stays the same.

    Final answer: a + 5 years; 17 years when a = 12.

    Example 2 · A fixed purchase count

    Question: A pencil costs ₹p. Find the cost of six pencils.

    1. Rule: The price is repeated six times.
    2. Work: Add p six times or multiply 6 × p, written 6p.

    Final answer: ₹6p

    Example 3 · Less than a number

    Question: Write “seven less than a number t.”

    1. Rule: Start with the quantity from which you subtract.
    2. Work: Start at t, then remove 7. Check t = 10 gives 3.

    Final answer: t − 7

    Example 4 · Equal shares

    Question: Share n cards equally among four pupils.

    1. Rule: Divide the total by the number of pupils.
    2. Work: Each share is n ÷ 4. If cards stay whole, n must be a multiple of 4 for equal shares with none left.

    Final answer: n/4 cards

    Example 5 · From multiplication to algebra

    Question: Write the nth positive multiple of 7.

    1. Rule: Multiply the step number by the common increment starting at step 1.
    2. Work: The first terms are 7 × 1, 7 × 2, 7 × 3. Replacing the position with n gives 7 × n.

    Final answer: 7n

    Example 6 · Interpret juxtaposition

    Question: What does 3(x + 4) mean?

    1. Rule: A number beside a bracket multiplies the entire group.
    2. Work: Add x and 4, then multiply by 3. At x = 2 this gives 3 × 6 = 18.

    Final answer: Three times the sum of x and 4.

    Example 7 · Read signed terms

    Question: Identify the terms in 3x + 5y − 7.

    1. Rule: Read subtraction as addition of an opposite.
    2. Work: Rewrite as 3x + 5y + (−7). The numeric factors of the letter terms are 3 and 5.

    Final answer: 3x, 5y and −7. The constant term is −7.

    Example 8 · Find an implicit coefficient

    Question: Give the coefficients in −a + b.

    1. Rule: A lone variable has coefficient 1 and its opposite has coefficient −1.
    2. Work: Write (−1)a + 1b. The signs are part of the terms.

    Final answer: −1 for a and 1 for b.

    Example 9 · Clarify the equality sign

    Question: Classify 4m + 2 and 4m + 2 = 18.

    1. Rule: An equation states that two expressions have equal value.
    2. Work: The first is a calculation rule. The second states equality for values of m that make the left side 18.

    Final answer: An expression, followed by an equation.

    Intermediate · Examples 10–21

    Example 10 · Substitute a positive value

    Question: Evaluate 6n − 4 at n = 5.

    1. Rule: Replace the letter first and multiply before subtracting.
    2. Work: 6 × 5 − 4 = 30 − 4.

    Final answer: 26

    Example 11 · Substitute a negative value

    Question: Evaluate 15 − 3t when t = −2.

    1. Rule: Put negative replacements in brackets.
    2. Work: 15 − 3(−2) = 15 − (−6). Taking away −6 adds 6.

    Final answer: 21

    Example 12 · Substitute two values

    Question: Evaluate 2a − 5b + 8 at a = −3 and b = 2.

    1. Rule: Use the stated value for every occurrence of each letter.
    2. Work: 2(−3) − 5(2) + 8 = −6 − 10 + 8 = −16 + 8.

    Final answer: −8

    Example 13 · Keep the bracket intact

    Question: Evaluate 4(p − 3) at p = 8.

    1. Rule: Work inside brackets first.
    2. Work: 4(8 − 3) = 4 × 5. Alternatively 4p − 12 gives 32 − 12.

    Final answer: 20

    Example 14 · Collect like terms

    Question: Simplify 8x − 3x + 2x.

    1. Rule: Add the signed coefficients of terms with the same letter part.
    2. Work: Factor out x: (8 − 3 + 2)x = (5 + 2)x.

    Final answer: 7x

    Example 15 · Collect two families

    Question: Simplify 7p + 4q − 2p − q + 6.

    1. Rule: Reorder terms with their signs and group the letter parts.
    2. Work: (7 − 2)p + (4 − 1)q + 6 = 5p + 3q + 6.

    Final answer: 5p + 3q + 6

    Example 16 · Cancel opposite terms

    Question: Simplify 5a − 3b − 5a + 3b.

    1. Rule: Each pair of opposite terms adds to zero.
    2. Work: (5a − 5a) + (−3b + 3b) = 0 + 0.

    Final answer: 0

    Example 17 · Expand a product

    Question: Expand 5(2r − 3).

    1. Rule: Distribute the multiplier to both terms.
    2. Work: 5 × 2r + 5 × (−3) gives 10r − 15.

    Final answer: 10r − 15

    Example 18 · Subtract a bracket

    Question: Simplify 7m − (3m − 4).

    1. Rule: Subtracting a group reverses every sign in it.
    2. Work: 7m − 3m + 4 = (7 − 3)m + 4.

    Final answer: 4m + 4

    Example 19 · Compare two forms

    Question: Are 2(x + 3) and 2x + 3 equivalent?

    1. Rule: Expand the bracket and compare the resulting terms.
    2. Work: The first is 2x + 6. For x = 0 the values are 6 and 3, so the difference is real.

    Final answer: No. The first is greater by 3.

    Example 20 · Perimeter of a rectangle

    Question: A rectangle has length l and width w. Find its perimeter when l = 8 and w = 5.

    1. Rule: Opposite sides have equal lengths.
    2. Work: l + w + l + w = 2l + 2w. With the given lengths, 16 + 10 = 26.

    Final answer: 2l + 2w; 26 units.

    Example 21 · Area of joined rectangles

    Question: Two rectangles of height h have widths 5 and 7. They share a full edge of height h. Find the combined area.

    1. Rule: Area adds or use the combined width.
    2. Work: Areas are 5h and 7h. The total equals (5 + 7)h.

    Final answer: 12h square units

    Advanced and application · Examples 22–30

    Example 22 · Model recurring expenses

    Question: A club has ₹s initially. It earns ₹18 each week and pays ₹7 each week. Find the balance after w weeks.

    1. Rule: Separate the starting value from the weekly change.
    2. Work: s + 18w − 7w = s + 11w. When s = 20 and w = 6, this is 20 + 66.

    Final answer: ₹(s + 11w); ₹86 in the example.

    Example 23 · Count joined squares

    Question: A row of n squares shares edges. Find the sticks for 12 squares.

    1. Rule: Count 4 for the first square and 3 for each new one.
    2. Work: There are n − 1 later squares: 4 + 3(n − 1) = 3n + 1. With n = 12, 36 + 1 = 37.

    Final answer: 3n + 1 for n ≥ 1; 37 sticks.

    Example 24 · Use a calendar grid

    Question: In a full two-row, three-column block on a weekly calendar, the lower middle date is d. Name the top row.

    1. Rule: Moving up subtracts 7 and moving across changes dates by 1.
    2. Work: The upper middle is d − 7. The top left is one less and the top right is one more.

    Final answer: d − 8, d − 7, d − 6

    Example 25 · Model a rotating cycle

    Question: A display repeats star, circle, triangle. Find the 71st symbol and describe circle positions.

    1. Rule: Full cycles contain three positions.
    2. Work: 71 = 3 × 23 + 2, so it is second in a cycle. After k full cycles, the second position is 3k + 2 for k ≥ 0.

    Final answer: Circle; positions 3k + 2.

    Example 26 · Find a numbered-grid cell

    Question: A grid fills rows of four. Find row 9 column 3, and locate 71.

    1. Rule: Count completed rows first.
    2. Work: Formula 4(r − 1) + c gives 4 × 8 + 3 = 35. Since 71 = 4 × 17 + 3, there are 17 complete rows before it.

    Final answer: 35; 71 is in row 18, column 3.

    Example 27 · Compare quiz scores

    Question: Nina has 8 correct and 2 wrong. Dev has 7 correct and 1 wrong. Award p per correct answer and subtract q per wrong answer. Compare the scores.

    1. Rule: Subtract the second score from the first and simplify.
    2. Work: (8p − 2q) − (7p − q) = p − q. A positive result favours Nina; a negative result favours Dev.

    Final answer: Nina leads if p > q, Dev if p < q; a tie if p = q.

    Example 28 · Show a coincidence is not a rule

    Question: For x = 2, 3x and x + 4 agree. Are they always equal?

    1. Rule: One equal substitution is a check, not a proof.
    2. Work: At x = 2 both give 6. At x = 0 they give 0 and 4. One counterexample disproves equivalence.

    Final answer: No; x = 0 is a counterexample.

    Example 29 · Count with a fixed start

    Question: A machine takes 9 seconds to start and then sorts 6 parcels each second. How many seconds to sort 48 parcels? Give a rule for n parcels at the same rate.

    1. Rule: Add the delay once, after dividing parcels by the rate.
    2. Work: Sorting time is n/6 seconds. Total is 9 + n/6. With n = 48 this becomes 9 + 8.

    Final answer: 9 + n/6 seconds; 17 seconds for 48 parcels.

    Example 30 · Compare two floor borders

    Question: One rectangle has sides x + 4 and 3; another has sides x + 1 and 6. Compare perimeters for positive x.

    1. Rule: Write both perimeters and use distribution.
    2. Work: First: 2(x + 4 + 3) = 2x + 14. Second: 2(x + 1 + 6) also gives 2x + 14. Both simplifications preserve every term.

    Final answer: Equal perimeters for every allowed x.

    11. Quick revision and exam preparation

    Define → Translate

    State what each letter measures. Use brackets for a whole group and respect the order of subtraction.

    Identify → Combine

    Mark signed terms, coefficients and constants. Collect only like terms and distribute to every term.

    Substitute → Check

    Use the same value for every occurrence of the letter. Bracket negative replacements and follow arithmetic priorities.

    Observe → Prove

    Explain a pattern from its structure. Verify your proposed rule at the starting step. Use properties for an identity and a counterexample to disprove one.

    Last-minute checklist

    1. Can I explain what the variable and constant mean in the situation?
    2. Did I keep every sign when removing a bracket or rearranging terms?
    3. Is the result a length, square units, a score, or rupees?
    4. For a pattern, does the step start at 0 or 1? Which sides or vertices are shared?
    5. For assertion–reason items, check both statements before deciding whether the reason explains the assertion.

    Review the translation table, the properties reference, and worked examples for a final recap.

    3. A student substitutes x=3 into 4(x−2) and reads 4x−2. Correct both the evaluation and expansion.
    4. The cost of an item is ₹v. A shop displays “three less than twice the price.” A learner writes 3−2v. Explain the repair.
    5. A n-segment decoration uses 2 sticks per segment plus a cap of 3 sticks. A revised model adds one stick per segment but removes one cap stick. Compare rules.
    Assertion–reason · Questions 56–60

    Choose A if both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion, B if both are true without that explanation, C if only the assertion is true, or D if only the reason is true.

    1. Assertion: 4x+3x=7x. Reason: Like terms may be collected by adding their coefficients.
    2. Assertion: 3(a+2)=3a+6. Reason: Addition is commutative.
    3. Assertion: The constant term of 5y−4 is −4. Reason: A constant term changes when y changes.
    4. Assertion: x+x=x² for every x. Reason: x² denotes the product x×x.
    5. Assertion: 2r+1 generates odd positive numbers beginning at 1 as r begins at 0. Reason: 2r is even for whole r, and adding one gives the next odd number.
    Case study · Questions 61–62
    1. The craft stall. Packs of cards cost ₹p and tubes of glue cost ₹q. A class buys 5 packs and 2 tubes, later buys 3 packs and returns 1 tube for a full refund. (a) Form the first cost. (b) Give the net cost. (c) Use p=20 and q=12.
    2. Garden paths. A rectangular path border uses a row of n joined unit squares along its length. Each new square shares one side. (a) Count the sticks for four squares. (b) Write and explain a rule. (c) How many sticks do 25 squares require?
    Higher-order thinking · Questions 63–67
    1. Find two expressions of different appearance equivalent to 12x+18 and justify each.
    2. Find the mistake in (3x+4)−(x−5)=2x−1 and check the correction at x=2.
    3. Write an expression for the sum of the three entries in row r of a 3-column grid numbered across from 1. Simplify it.
    4. A pupil finds that 5x−4 and 3x+2 agree when x=3. Show why this cannot prove a general identity and give a counterexample.
    5. Two repeating cycles have lengths four and six and start together at position 1. Give a rule for positions where both start a new cycle and the fourth such position including position 1.

    Multiple choice · Answers 1–15
    1. B. p−7. Start at p and subtract seven.
    2. C. 5(a+b). The multiplier applies to the entire sum.
    3. D. −1. −z means (−1)×z.
    4. C. −11. The signed term −11 has no letter.
    5. A. 3x and −8x. Both terms have the same letter part x.
    6. B. 4p. Collect the coefficients: 5−2+1=4.
    7. D. 4a−12. Multiplying both terms gives 4a−4×3.
    8. C. −10. 3(−4)+2=−12+2=−10.
    9. C. P=4s. The perimeter is s+s+s+s.
    10. B. 10−x. Reverse both inside signs: 8−x+2=10−x.
    11. A. 4n+1. The rule starts at 5 and rises by 4 each step.
    12. B. 2×x×y. Adjacent factors denote multiplication.
    13. C. They agree at that tested value. One matching value cannot establish an identity.
    14. B. 2a−5=9. It makes an equality statement with two sides.
    15. B. 7. Count four for the first and three new ones for the second.

    Long answer · Answers 46–50
    1. Start at s, add 4w, subtract 7. Then 18+20−7=31 chairs.
    2. Distribute to get 8a−4b+12, subtract the second group as −5a−2b+4, and collect 3a−6b+16.
    3. New dimensions are x and 10. Total area is 4x+6x=10x. Outer perimeter is 2x+20; the shared edge is interior.
    4. Add (6p−2q)+(9p−5q)=15p−7q. Substitute to get 60−7=53.
    5. Separate count is 4n. Joined is 4+3(n−1)=3n+1. Difference is n−1 shared edges. At n=15 it saves 14 sticks.
    Competency based · Answers 51–55
    1. Correct rule is 35+12d; 47d charges the starting fee for every km. Both give ₹47 at d=1, which is only one trip length.
    2. 5n+2 models the two extra markers. 5(n+2) describes ten extra markers in two full rows.
    3. Evaluate 4(3−2)=4. Expanding gives 4x−8, which also gives 4 at x=3.
    4. Start from twice the price, then remove three: 2v−3. The order of subtraction controls the sign.
    5. Original 2n+3. Revised 3n+2. Revised minus original is n−1, so they agree at n=1 and the revised uses more for n>1.

    1. 6(2x+3) expands to 12x+18. Also 8x+4x+20−2 collects to 12x+18.
    2. The −5 becomes +5. Simplify to 2x+9. At x=2, 10−(−3)=13 and 2×2+9=13.
