Getting started · Three lines and a triangle

Three distinct lines can enclose a triangle when each pair meets and the three intersections are different points. Those points are the vertices. The parts of the lines between the vertices are the sides. Think of the rails of a bridge truss, the edges of a triangular traffic sign or the roads bounding a small triangular island.

In △ABC, the sides are AB, BC and CA. The interior angles are ∠BAC, ∠ABC and ∠BCA. The middle letter names the vertex. BC is opposite ∠A. AB and AC are adjacent to ∠A, and ∠B and ∠C are at the ends of BC. Extending a side gives a ray you can use to name an exterior angle.

Learning goals. Construct triangles from given measurements, decide when a construction is possible, give reasons for angle calculations, draw altitudes and classify triangles in two ways.

7.1 · Equilateral triangles

An equilateral triangle has three equal sides. A ruler fixes the base. A compass produces all the points at a fixed distance from its centre, so the same compass opening gives the remaining two sides.

Construct △ABC with all sides 5 cm

Sketch a triangle and write 5 cm on every edge. Mark AB = 5 cm with a ruler. Draw an arc centred at A with opening 5 cm. Repeat from B with the same opening. Mark an intersection C and join it to both A and B.

AC and BC are radii of their respective arcs, so each is 5 cm. The intersection below AB produces a mirror image. A ruler-only trial could give the correct AC without fixing BC, which is why both arcs matter.

Investigate equal radii

Goal. Find isosceles and equilateral triangles in circles.

Materials. A compass, ruler and paper.

  1. Draw a circle with centre O. Choose two distinct points P and Q whose chord misses O. Join OP, OQ and PQ.
  2. With the same opening, draw a second circle centred at P. Join O and P to an intersection of the circles.
Reveal the observation and explanation

The first triangle has OP = OQ. The second has three equal sides. Every point on a circle is one radius from its centre. Here OP is also equal to that radius.

7.2 · Constructing a triangle from its sides

For three given side lengths, begin with any side as the base. This is often called an SSS construction. A rough labelled sketch helps you assign the other two lengths to the correct centres.

Construct AB = 7 cm, AC = 5 cm and BC = 6 cm

Draw AB with the ruler. Set the compass to 5 cm at A and 6 cm at B. Mark one intersection C and join CA and CB. Measure all three sides to check. With AB fixed, the possible positions of C are reflections across AB.

When do the arcs meet?

The direct route from A to B is shorter than the route through C whenever C is off the line AB. Apply that reasoning to each side. For positive lengths sorted as a ≤ b ≤ c, checking a + b > c is enough. The other two sums exceed their opposite side automatically.

\(a+b>c,\quad b+c>a,\quad c+a>b\)

If a + b = c the circles just touch on the base, producing a flat arrangement. If a + b < c they leave a gap. If a + b > c they cross at two points away from the base. When the two fixed lengths are 6 cm and 9 cm, a third length x must satisfy 3 < x < 15 because 6 + x > 9 and 6 + 9 > x.

Investigate with sticks and circles

Goal. Compare a gap, a straight shape and a closed triangle.

Materials. A ruler, compass and paper strips.

  1. Cut strips of lengths 3, 5, 7, 8 and 9 cm.
  2. Take 3 and 5 cm as movable sides and try the bases 7, 8 and 9 cm.
  3. Repeat using the two short sides as compass radii from opposite ends of each base.
Reveal the observation and explanation

A 7 cm base closes, an 8 cm base flattens, and a 9 cm base leaves a gap. Compare 3 + 5 with the longest side in each case.

7.3 · Constructing from sides and angles

Two sides and their included angle

The included angle is at the common endpoint of the two specified sides. For AB = 6 cm, AC = 4 cm and ∠A = 65°, make a rough sketch, draw AB and place the protractor at A with zero along AB. Draw the 65° ray, mark AC = 4 cm on it, and join BC. A positive length on each ray and an angle strictly between 0° and 180° give one shape, with a reflected option on the other side of AB.

Two angles and their included side

The included side joins the two specified angle vertices. For AB = 7 cm, ∠A = 45° and ∠B = 70°, make a rough sketch and draw AB. Draw the rays above AB, measuring from AB at A and from BA at B. Their common point C completes the triangle. The angles are fixed by the rays, so a fixed base gives one shape up to reflection.

With positive base angles, a sum below 180° lets the rays meet. At 180° the rays are parallel by the same-side interior angle relationship. Above 180° the two rays do not meet on the chosen side. If the given side is elsewhere and two angles are available, calculate the missing angle first, then use the two angles at the ends of the given side. Three angles alone fix shape while leaving scale free.

Why the interior angles add to 180°

  1. Draw a line through C parallel to AB.
  2. AC is a transversal, so the angle between the left parallel ray and CA equals ∠A by alternate interior angles.
  3. BC is a transversal, so the angle between CB and the right parallel ray equals ∠B for the same reason.
  4. Those two angles and ∠ACB fill the straight angle at C, giving the angle sum.

\(\angle A+\angle B+\angle C=180^\circ\)

Investigate with a protractor and paper corners

Goal. See why the sum persists for different triangles.

Materials. Paper, pencil, protractor and scissors.

  1. Draw a thin triangle and a wide triangle. Measure each set of three angles and record the sums.
  2. Cut or tear off three corners of one triangle. Place their tips together with consecutive edges touching.
  3. For the folding variation, start with an acute paper triangle. Fold the top vertex to the base along a crease parallel to the base. Fold the two base corners inward until their tips meet the folded top tip.
Reveal the observation and explanation

The three angles pack into a straight angle. Measurements may differ by a degree or two. The parallel-line argument establishes the exact value. The folds copy angles without changing their measures.

Try next. Try the cut-corner method with one obtuse angle. What stays the same?

Investigate angle limits

Goal. Compare angle-based constructions as one angle changes.

Materials. Ruler, protractor and paper.

  1. Draw two bases of different lengths and mark the same pair of angles, 35° and 75°, at their ends.
  2. Estimate the third angles. Then fix 35° at A and vary ∠B through 125°, 145° and 155°.
Reveal the observation and explanation

Both initial triangles have third angle 70°. The rays meet for 125°, run parallel at 145° and diverge at 155°. The base changes scale. The 180° threshold controls whether the rays close.

Exterior angle at a vertex

Extend BC beyond C to D. ∠ACD and ∠ACB share CA and form a linear pair, so their sum is 180°. ∠A + ∠B + ∠ACB also equals 180°. Subtract ∠ACB from both totals to obtain the exterior-angle property.

\(\angle ACD=\angle A+\angle B\)

For example, 44° + 62° gives the exterior angle 106°. Alternatively, the interior angle at C is 74°, so subtract 74° from 180°. If one remote angle is unknown, subtract the known remote angle from the exterior angle.

7.4 · Constructions related to altitudes

An altitude is a perpendicular segment from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side. Its endpoint on that line is the foot. When BC is the base, the height is the length of the altitude drawn from A. Changing the base can change the height.

In an acute triangle all feet lie on the sides. In an obtuse triangle the two altitudes from acute vertices land on extensions. In a right triangle the two legs each serve as an altitude, and the altitude from the right-angle vertex meets the hypotenuse.

Fold to find an altitude

Goal. Make a perpendicular crease through a chosen vertex.

Materials. A paper triangle, straightedge and pencil.

  1. Choose BC as base. For an outside foot, draw the triangle on a larger sheet so BC can be extended.
  2. Fold so the crease passes through A and the line BC lies over itself with opposite directions matched.
  3. Unfold and mark the foot D. Check the angle with a set square.
Reveal the observation and explanation

The crease passes through A and makes a right angle with BC. The two angles at the crease mirror each other and form a straight angle, so each is 90°.

7.5 · Types of triangles

Classify the same triangle once by sides and once by angles. An equilateral triangle is the special case of three equal sides. In a three-column classification use “isosceles” for exactly two equal sides. A property about two equal sides also applies when all three are equal.

ClassificationTestExampleA counterexample
EquilateralThree equal sides5, 5, 5 cm5, 5, 6 cm
IsoscelesAn equal pair of sides5, 5, 6 cm4, 5, 6 cm
ScaleneThree distinct side lengths4, 5, 6 cm5, 5, 6 cm
AcuteEvery angle < 90°50°, 60°, 70°40°, 50°, 90°
RightOne angle = 90°45°, 45°, 90°45°, 55°, 80°
ObtuseOne angle > 90°25°, 35°, 120°45°, 45°, 90°

Two equal sides face two equal angles. In an isosceles triangle, fold the two equal sides onto one another through their common vertex. The base endpoints and their angles match. If the apex is 40°, the base angles are each (180° − 40°) ÷ 2 = 70°. In an equilateral triangle, all three angles are equal, so each is 60°. Conversely, two equal angles face equal sides.

Explore opposite sides and angles

Draw AB = 7 cm and construct ∠A = 40° and ∠B = 60°. The third angle is 80°. Measure BC, AC and AB. Compare the order of these lengths with the order of the angles at their opposite vertices. Try a second triangle and compare your observations.

Investigate which classifications go together

Goal. Explore combinations of side type and angle type.

Materials. Protractor, ruler and paper.

  1. Make triangles with base angles 45° and 45°, then 30° and 30°, using the same base.
  2. Try to make an equilateral right or obtuse triangle.
  3. Fix hypotenuse AC and use several positive ∠A and ∠C pairs that add to 90°.
Reveal the observation and explanation

The first isosceles triangle is right, the second obtuse. The equilateral triangle is always acute. Many right triangles share any fixed positive hypotenuse length. The angle sum gives 90°, 120° and 60° respectively. A fixed hypotenuse leaves the acute-angle pair free to vary.

Applications and investigations

Bridges and transmission towers. Once all three bar lengths are fixed, a triangle has a fixed shape. Diagonal bracing divides a frame into triangles. Compare a pinned rectangle and a pinned triangle made with card strips. The rectangle can skew while the triangle holds its angles.

Roof trusses and architecture. Equal rafters create an isosceles cross-section. Measuring the altitude gives the vertical rise above the tie beam. Diagonal supports divide repeated designs into smaller triangular frames.

Traffic signs, road islands and surveying. An equilateral warning-sign outline has three equal sides and angles. Three road centre lines may bound a triangular island. Surveyors can mark a known baseline and measure two angles to locate a visible third point. In patterns, rotating and reflecting a triangular tile can create repeating designs.

Shortest path on a box

Goal. Compare routes across a box’s faces.

Materials. A small carton, paper, thread and ruler.

  1. Mark opposite corners of a 3 cm by 4 cm by 6 cm box or model.
  2. Make paper versions of the possible two-face unfoldings. Draw the straight segment between the marked corners on each.
  3. Measure the segments and fold the paper back to identify each surface route.
Reveal the observation and explanation

The unfolded rectangles are 7 × 6, 9 × 4 and 10 × 3. The 7 × 6 rectangle gives the shortest of these three diagonals. The line within an unfolding is the shortest route across those faces. Compare its measured length with the other choices.

Properties at a glance

Interior sum

A + B + C = 180°

Use when. Any non-flat triangle.

A parallel through one vertex places the other two angles along its straight angle.

Example. 52° + 68° leaves 60°.

Exterior angle

Exterior at C = A + B

Use when. Extend BC beyond C.

Both the linear pair and the triangle sum equal 180°. Eliminate the shared angle.

Example. 37° + 59° gives 96°.

Linear pair

Neighbouring angles sum to 180°

Use when. The non-shared rays are opposite.

Their sectors fill a straight angle.

Example. An interior 74° has adjacent exterior 106°.

Vertically opposite angles

Opposite angles are equal

Use when. Two intersecting lines.

Each is supplementary to the same adjacent angle.

Example. If an angle is 62°, the angle opposite it is 62°.

Triangle inequality

Shortest two together exceed longest

Use when. Positive lengths.

The broken path through the third vertex is longer than the direct path.

Example. 4 + 5 > 8, so these lengths work.

Isosceles angles

Equal sides face equal angles

Use when. Two equal sides.

The fold through the apex matches the base corners.

Example. An apex of 44° leaves two 68° base angles.

Equilateral angles

Each angle is 60°

Use when. Three equal sides.

All angles are equal by the equal-side property and sum to 180°.

Example. 180° ÷ 3 = 60°.

Altitude

AD ⟂ BC

Use when. D is on the line through BC.

A set-square edge perpendicular to BC fixes the direction.

Example. If AB ⟂ BC, AB is the altitude from A.

Construction conditions

Three lengths, two sides and included angle, or two angles and included side

Use when. Apply the appropriate existence test.

Two arcs, a measured ray, or two rays determine the last vertex.

Example. With ∠A = 45° and ∠B = 70°, ∠C = 65°.

30 step-by-step worked examples

Basic

Example 1 · Name the angle at B and its opposite side.

Given. Triangle ABC

Find. Use angle notation and name the opposite side.

Property. The middle letter names the angle vertex.

Solution. Write B between the other two letters. The side that joins the other vertices is opposite B.

Answer. ∠ABC; side AC.

Example 2 · Can three lines through one lamp-post bound a triangular island?

Given. Three distinct concurrent lines

Find. Decide whether the lines enclose a triangle.

Property. The three intersections must be distinct.

Solution. Each pair meets at the lamp-post. A triangle needs three different vertices.

Answer. No triangle forms.

Example 3 · Find ∠C if A = 48° and B = 67°.

Given. ∠A = 48°, ∠B = 67°

Find. Find ∠C.

Property. The triangle angles total 180°.

Solution. Add 48° + 67° = 115°. Subtract this from 180°.

Answer. ∠C = 65°.

Example 4 · Find the exterior angle at C.

Given. A = 38°, B = 72°; extend BC to D

Find. Find ∠ACD.

Property. An exterior angle equals the two remote interior angles added together.

Solution. Add 38° + 72°. The remote angles are at A and B.

Answer. ∠ACD = 110°.

Another method. Find C = 70°, then subtract 70° from 180° by the linear-pair rule.

Example 5 · Can 4, 7 and 9 cm form a triangle?

Given. Three positive lengths

Find. Check if the sides can close.

Property. Add the two shorter lengths.

Solution. 4 + 7 = 11 cm. Since 11 > 9, the arcs cross when 9 cm is the base.

Answer. Yes, a triangle is possible.

Example 6 · Can 3, 6 and 9 cm form a triangle?

Given. Three positive lengths

Find. Check whether there is an off-base intersection.

Property. The side inequality is strict.

Solution. 3 + 6 = 9. Circles from the ends of the 9 cm base touch on the base.

Answer. The arrangement is flat.

Example 7 · Classify a 45°–90°–45° triangle.

Given. A = C = 45°, B = 90°

Find. Give the side type and angle type.

Property. Equal angles face equal sides. A 90° angle makes a right triangle.

Solution. A and C face BC and AB, so those legs agree. B is right.

Answer. Isosceles and right-angled.

Example 8 · Find the angles of an equilateral triangle.

Given. AB = BC = CA

Find. Find all three angles.

Property. Equal sides face equal angles.

Solution. Write each as the same measure. Divide 180° by 3.

Answer. All three measure 60°.

Example 9 · Name the altitude from A if AB is perpendicular to BC.

Given. AB ⟂ BC

Find. Find the segment and its foot.

Property. The altitude passes through A and meets line BC at 90°.

Solution. AB already passes through A and forms a right angle with BC at B.

Answer. Altitude AB, foot B.

Intermediate

Example 10 · Construct an equilateral triangle with side 5 cm.

Given. AB = AC = BC = 5 cm

Find. Construct and check the triangle.

Property. A compass arc fixes the distance to its centre.

Solution. Draw AB. Draw a 5 cm arc from each endpoint. Join their common point C to A and B. Both remaining sides are 5 cm radii.

Answer. △ABC has side 5 cm throughout.

Example 11 · Construct a 7–5–6 cm triangle.

Given. AB = 7 cm, AC = 5 cm, BC = 6 cm

Find. Locate C precisely.

Property. C is the common point of two arcs.

Solution. Draw AB. Draw arcs of radius 5 from A and 6 from B. Mark an intersection and join it to A and B. 5 + 6 > 7 ensures a crossing.

Answer. The lengths are 7, 5 and 6 cm.

Example 12 · Construct from two sides and the angle between them.

Given. AB = 6 cm, AC = 4 cm, A = 65°

Find. Construct △ABC.

Property. The included angle is at the common endpoint.

Solution. Draw AB, then measure 65° from AB at A. Mark C 4 cm along the ray using a ruler or compass. Join BC.

Answer. △ABC satisfies all three measurements.

Example 13 · Construct from two angles and their included side.

Given. AB = 7 cm, A = 45°, B = 70°

Find. Construct △ABC and find C.

Property. Rays with these positive angles meet because their sum is under 180°.

Solution. Draw AB. Draw the two rays on the same side, measuring B from BA. Mark C at their intersection. Subtract 45° + 70° from 180°.

Answer. △ABC constructed; ∠C = 65°.

Example 14 · Find the remote angle from an exterior angle.

Given. Exterior at C = 132°, A = 58°

Find. Find ∠B.

Property. Exterior at C = A + B.

Solution. Use 132° = 58° + B. Subtract 58°.

Answer. ∠B = 74°.

Example 15 · Find equal base angles of a roof truss.

Given. CA = CB and apex C = 36°

Find. Find A and B.

Property. Equal sides face equal angles.

Solution. A = B and A + B = 180° − 36° = 144°. Divide by 2.

Answer. A = B = 72°.

Example 16 · Classify a triangle with an angle ratio of 2 : 3 : 4.

Given. Angle ratio A : B : C = 2 : 3 : 4

Find. Find the angles and both types.

Property. The sum of the angles is 180°.

Solution. There are 9 parts, so a part is 180° ÷ 9 = 20°. Multiply 20° by each ratio term.

Answer. 40°, 60°, 80°. Acute and scalene.

Example 17 · List integer values of the third side.

Given. Two sides of 5 cm and 8 cm; third side x cm

Find. Find the possible integers.

Property. Each side is strictly shorter than the other two together.

Solution. 5 + x > 8 gives x > 3. 5 + 8 > x gives x < 13. The third inequality holds for positive x.

Answer. 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 or 12 cm.

Example 18 · Find two base angles given an exterior angle.

Given. AC = BC; exterior at C is 108°

Find. Find A and B.

Property. A linear pair sums to 180°. Equal sides face equal angles.

Solution. The interior at C is 180° − 108° = 72°. The equal angles A and B together account for the exterior 108°. Divide by 2.

Answer. A = B = 54°.

Example 19 · Draw an altitude with its foot outside the triangle.

Given. A = 35°, B = 115°, C = 30°

Find. Draw the altitude from A to line BC.

Property. The altitude must meet the entire base line perpendicularly.

Solution. Extend CB beyond B. Slide the set square along that line until its perpendicular edge goes through A. Draw AD.

Answer. D lies beyond B and AD ⟂ BC.

Example 20 · Will the rays of an angle construction meet?

Given. Base AB = 6 cm; A = 42°, B = 138°

Find. Decide whether a triangle exists.

Property. Positive base angles must sum to less than 180°.

Solution. 42° + 138° = 180°. The rays are parallel on the chosen side.

Answer. No triangle forms.

Example 21 · Find the angle opposite a marked 62° angle.

Given. Two straight lines cross; one angle is 62°

Find. Find both the adjacent angle and the opposite angle.

Property. Linear pairs sum to 180° and vertically opposite angles are equal.

Solution. Subtract 62° from 180° to get 118°. The angle opposite 62° is also adjacent to 118°, so subtract again.

Answer. Adjacent 118°; opposite 62°.

Advanced and competency-based

Example 22 · Find angles expressed using x.

Given. A = x + 10°, B = 2x, C = x + 30°

Find. Solve for x and the angles.

Property. Triangle angle sum.

Solution. Add the expressions: 4x + 40° = 180°. Thus 4x = 140° and x = 35°. Substitute in all three expressions.

Answer. x = 35°; the angles are 45°, 70°, 65°.

Example 23 · Solve an exterior-angle equation.

Given. A = x + 12°, B = 2x, exterior at C = 129°

Find. Find all three interior angles.

Property. The exterior angle is the sum of the remote angles.

Solution. Set 3x + 12° = 129°. Then x = 39°, so A = 51° and B = 78°. Subtract 129° from 180° for C.

Answer. 51°, 78°, 51°.

Example 24 · Find the permissible angles of a survey triangle.

Given. A = 64°; B is a positive integer in degrees

Find. Find the largest possible B and the corresponding C.

Property. The sum of the two base angles must be less than 180°.

Solution. B < 116°, so the greatest integer is 115°. C = 180° − 64° − 115°.

Answer. B = 115° and C = 1°.

Example 25 · Two measurements do not fix the shape.

Given. Right angle at B; AC = 8 cm

Find. Give two different triangles satisfying the data.

Property. The two non-right angles must sum to 90°.

Solution. Use AC as base. Construct A = 30°, C = 60° once, then A = C = 45°. Both cases leave 90° at B.

Answer. Both triangles work and have different angle sets.

Example 26 · Find the angles of an isosceles right triangle.

Given. B = 90° and AB = BC

Find. Find A and C.

Property. Equal sides give equal opposite angles.

Solution. A = C, and the sum remaining after the right angle is 90°. Divide by 2.

Answer. A = C = 45°.

Example 27 · The altitude divides an obtuse triangle outside its base.

Given. A = 35°, B = 115°, C = 30°; AD ⟂ line BC

Find. Find ∠DAB, with D beyond B.

Property. Linear pair at B and angle sum in △ADB.

Solution. ∠ABD = 180° − 115° = 65°. In right △ADB, ∠DAB = 180° − 90° − 65°.

Answer. ∠DAB = 25°.

Example 28 · Find C using an attached triangle.

Given. Extend BC to D and choose E on ray CA beyond A. In △CDE, ∠D = 64° and ∠E = 34°.

Find. Find ∠ACB and the sum A + B in △ABC.

Property. The angle at C in △CDE is the exterior angle of △ABC.

Solution. Ray CE is ray CA. ∠DCE = 180° − 64° − 34° = 82°. Then ∠ACB = 180° − 82° and A + B = 82°.

Answer. ∠ACB = 98°; A + B = 82°.

Example 29 · Check whether the given angle is included.

Given. AB = 6 cm, AC = 4 cm and ∠C = 65°

Find. Decide whether the given sides meet at the marked angle.

Property. The included angle is at the endpoint shared by both known sides.

Solution. AB and AC share A, whereas C is on AC and BC. Mark A on a rough sketch to locate the included angle.

Answer. The included angle is ∠A.

Example 30 · Find an isosceles triangle with integer sides and perimeter 19 cm.

Given. The base is 7 cm. The equal sides are both y cm.

Find. Find y and check existence.

Property. Use perimeter and the strict side inequality.

Solution. 2y + 7 = 19 gives y = 6. The base is the longest side and 6 + 6 > 7.

Answer. The sides are 6, 6 and 7 cm.

Common mistakes

1. Any three lines enclose a triangle.

Correct approach. Check three distinct intersections and rule out any parallel pair.

Concurrent lines yield only one vertex. Sketch the three intersections first.

2. The adjacent interior angle equals the exterior angle.

Correct approach. Find it by subtracting the exterior angle from 180°.

Their outside rays point in opposite directions. Trace the full straight line.

3. The three interior angles total 360°.

Correct approach. Use one straight angle, 180°.

A parallel through a vertex brings the other two angle measures beside the vertex angle.

4. The exterior angle equals the adjacent angle plus another interior angle.

Correct approach. Add the two remote interior angles.

Cover the vertex of the exterior angle when choosing the two interior vertices.

5. The two angles nearest the extension are both remote.

Correct approach. Use A and B for the exterior at C, regardless of how the triangle is rotated.

The adjacent angle shares C and CA with the exterior. Identify the common vertex first.

6. Every triangle has equal sides.

Correct approach. Look for given lengths, matching marks or equal opposite angles.

A 7–5–6 triangle has three unequal sides.

7. Two equal sides prove that all three are equal.

Correct approach. Use isosceles for a pair of equal sides in the classification chart. Require three equal sides for equilateral.

Compare the unmarked third side with the marked pair.

8. Right-angled and isosceles are incompatible.

Correct approach. Classify separately by side equality and by angles.

A right angle can lie between two equal legs.

9. Equality passes the triangle inequality.

Correct approach. Require a strict greater-than sign.

Compass circles that just touch make a flat arrangement.

10. Any three positive lengths close.

Correct approach. Compare the sum of the two shortest to the longest side.

The two movable sides can leave a gap. Sort the lengths before adding.

11. Use BC as the compass radius at A.

Correct approach. Use AC from A and BC from B.

Read the distance from the centre named in each step.

12. Read either scale on the protractor.

Correct approach. Start at the zero on the baseline ray.

At B in an ASA construction, the baseline ray points from B toward A. Check whether your angle should look acute or obtuse.

13. A side looks perpendicular, so it is an altitude.

Correct approach. Require a right-angle mark, a stated 90° angle or a construction.

Rotate the page. Appearance changes while the perpendicular relationship does not.

14. Write only the final angle.

Correct approach. Show the equation and name the property beside it.

For C, write the 180° total and subtract the given angles. The equation identifies the reason.

15. Combine x + 10°, 2x and x + 30° as 3x + 40°.

Correct approach. Combine coefficients 1 + 2 + 1 to get 4x + 40°.

Solve the equation and substitute every angle to check the 180° sum.
