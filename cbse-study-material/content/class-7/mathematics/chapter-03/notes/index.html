1. Why we need smaller units

Two pencils can both be longer than 8 cm and shorter than 9 cm, yet have different lengths. Whole numbers tell us the interval; dividing the interval into equal parts tells us more. On a millimetre ruler, 8 cm and 4 mm is 8.4 cm. A ruler with finer marks can distinguish smaller differences.

Money

₹12.75 combines rupees and hundredths of a rupee.

Length and mass

A 2.65 m ribbon and a 0.075 kg parcel use fractional units.

Weather and sport

A temperature of 24.6 °C and a time of 12.08 s show readings between whole units.

Learning objectives

  • Split units into tenths, hundredths and thousandths, then read and write decimals.
  • Convert simple fractions and everyday units, locate decimals and justify their order.
  • Add and subtract decimals, extend patterns, estimate results and check that units make sense.

Try it: Measure an object from the zero mark. If an edge lies between ticks, give a range instead of inventing extra digits. If you start at a nonzero mark, subtract the starting reading from the ending reading.

Quick check

2. Tenths, hundredths and thousandths

One whole split into 10 equal parts gives tenths. Split each tenth into 10 equal parts and you get 100 hundredths per whole. Split a hundredth into 10 equal parts and you get 1,000 thousandths per whole.

Different descriptions can name the same quantity: \(1.36=1+\frac3{10}+\frac6{100}=1+\frac{36}{100}=\frac{136}{100}\). When combining fractions with place-value denominators, you can regroup place by place or convert them all to a common denominator.

Exchange rule

Ten parts in a column make one part in the column on its left. One part in any column can be exchanged for ten parts in the column on its right.

Quick check

3. Decimal place value, reading and expanded form

The decimal point separates the whole-number part from the fractional part. In 2,304.507, the whole-number part is 2,304. The fractional part is 0.507, read as five hundred seven thousandths. Read the full numeral aloud as “two thousand three hundred four point five zero seven.” The zero preserves the hundredths place.

Face value is the digit itself. Place value is the contribution of that digit in its column. Here the face value of 7 is 7 and its place value is 0.007. The expanded form is \(2{,}000+300+4+0.5+0.007\). To return to standard form, fill all columns, including empty ones.

The system continues to the right. A ten-thousandth is \(0.0001\). Moving from one column to its neighbour changes the value of that place by a factor of ten. For example, 36 tenths is 3 wholes and 6 tenths, or 3.6. In contrast, 36 hundredths is 0.36.

Quick check

4. Fractions and decimals

Fraction → decimal

When possible, scale both terms to make the denominator 10, 100 or 1,000. For example, \(\frac34=\frac{75}{100}=0.75\). Each hundredth represents one cell out of 100.

Decimal → simplest fraction

Use the last digit’s place as the denominator. For \(0.48=\frac{48}{100}\), divide both terms by 4 to obtain \(\frac{12}{25}\).

A fraction also represents division of the numerator by the denominator. Some divisions terminate; others, such as 1 ÷ 3, repeat. The main examples here focus on denominators you can rewrite as powers of ten. The converter identifies repeating digits rather than presenting a truncated representation as exact.

5. Decimal measurements and units

Everyday unit conversions

Worked conversion: 1 m = 100 cm, so 1 cm = 0.01 m. Therefore 248 cm = 248 hundredths of a metre = 2.48 m. Similarly, 65 g = 0.065 kg. When converting a measurement into a smaller unit, the number of units grows; when converting into a larger unit, the number of units shrinks.

Why factors of ten work: Multiplying a quantity by 10 makes each digit contribute ten times as much. In \(0.37\times10=3.7\), 3 tenths become 3 ones and 7 hundredths become 7 tenths. Dividing reverses that change: \(37\div100=0.37\). This same reasoning applies to 100 and 1,000 and supports metric conversions. General decimal multiplication and division are outside this chapter’s main arithmetic focus.

Compare or combine readings only after putting them in the same unit. For a temperature increase from 21.4 °C to 22.05 °C, the difference is 0.65 °C. For a race, a smaller elapsed time means finishing sooner.

Quick check

6. Locate decimals on a number line

Between 2 and 3, mark ten equal steps of 0.1. For 2.37, first locate the interval from 2.3 to 2.4. Zoom in and split that interval into ten equal steps of 0.01. The seventh tick after 2.30 is 2.37. To locate 2.374, zoom again between 2.37 and 2.38, where each step is 0.001.

To find a missing label, subtract the known endpoints and divide by the number of gaps. For instance, from 4.10 to 4.15 in five equal gaps, each gap represents 0.01 and the second tick after 4.10 is 4.12.

Quick check

7. Compare, order and find equal values

  1. Compare the whole-number parts first.
  2. If they match, compare tenths, hundredths and later places from left to right.
  3. Stop at the first difference. Add zeros on the right to match lengths only if it helps you see the columns.

More digits does not mean a greater value

\(0.5=0.50=0.500\). Since \(50/100>45/100\), \(0.5>0.45\). In contrast, 0.05 is five hundredths, so it is one tenth of 0.5.

For ascending order, list from least to greatest; for descending order, reverse it. For example, \(3.06<3.6<3.607\), because their thousandths-based values are 3.060, 3.600 and 3.607.

Quick check

8. Add, subtract, extend sequences and estimate

Align decimal points and write enough placeholder zeros to match columns. Then add or subtract from the right. Exchange ten in one place for one in the place to its left when adding. Exchange in the opposite direction when subtracting. The decimal point in the answer remains in the same column.

Alternative method: With three decimal places, work in thousandths. Subtract \(5000-2076=2924\) thousandths. Then write \(\frac{2924}{1000}=2.924\). An inverse check is \(2.924+2.076=5.000\).

Decimal sequences

Subtract consecutive terms to find the repeating step. In 2.95, 3.10, 3.25, … the step is +0.15, so the next two terms are 3.40 and 3.55. For 5.02, 4.96, 4.90, … the step is −0.06, so the next term is 4.84. Check more than one gap before assuming the same step continues.

Estimate before calculating

For two nonnegative decimals with whole parts \(a\) and \(b\), their sum is at least \(a+b\) and less than \(a+b+2\), because each fractional part is less than one. Their difference lies strictly between \(a-b-1\) and \(a-b+1\). For instance, \(17.85+5.42\) is at least 22 and less than 24; the exact sum 23.27 fits.

Optional rounding reminder

Rounding is one way to estimate. Locate the two nearest marks at the requested place and choose the closer mark, choosing the higher one for a positive midpoint. For 6.473, the nearest whole is 6, the nearest tenth is 6.5 and the nearest hundredth is 6.47. Chapter 3 emphasises estimating sums and differences rather than a separate rounding procedure.

Quick check

9. When a dot does not mean a decimal

Decimals use base ten, but mixed units may not. 2.25 hours is 2 hours and 15 minutes, because \(0.25\times60=15\). It is different from 2:25 on a clock. In cricket, a scoring display such as 7.4 overs means 7 overs plus 4 legal balls; an over has six legal balls. Two balls after 7.4 completes the eighth over. Similarly, 1.5 feet means 1 foot 6 inches, since a foot has 12 inches.

The chapter traces decimal fractions in earlier mathematical traditions and shows how writers used different marks before modern separator conventions. In the Indian notation used here, the dot separates whole and fractional places; some countries use a comma for that purpose. Always check the notation and units before comparing recorded values.

Measurement check

The same digit string can mislead if a unit or decimal mark is copied incorrectly. A 0.05 g reading is five hundredths of a gram, while 0.5 g is five tenths of a gram and ten times as large. Keep units next to numbers and estimate the expected size before writing a result.

    11. Step-by-step solved examples
    Try each example first. The 30 examples progress through 9 basic, 12 intermediate, and 9 advanced and application-based questions.
    Basic · Examples 1–9
    Example 1 · Measure beyond whole units
    Question: A ribbon ends at the seventh tenth after 2 m. Give its length.
    1. Rule: Divide each metre into ten equal tenths.
    2. Work: The starting mark is 2 m. Add 7/10 m = 0.7 m.
    Final answer: 2.7 m
    

    Example 2 · Read a zero in the middle
    Question: Read 6.045 digit by digit and as place values.
    1. Rule: Keep zero as a placeholder.
    2. Work: Say six point zero four five. The fractional part is 0 tenths, 4 hundredths and 5 thousandths.
    Final answer: Six and forty-five thousandths.
    

    Example 3 · Identify the place and value
    Question: Give the face value and place value of 8 in 42.081.
    1. Rule: Face value is the digit; place value is digit times column value.
    2. Work: The 8 is two places to the right of the point, so multiply 8 by 1/100.
    Final answer: Face value 8; place value 0.08.
    

    Example 4 · Expand a decimal
    Question: Write 306.709 in expanded form.
    1. Rule: Break the numeral into its nonzero place contributions.
    2. Work: There are 3 hundreds, 6 ones, 7 tenths and 9 thousandths. Tens and hundredths contribute zero.
    Final answer: 300 + 6 + 0.7 + 0.009
    

    Example 5 · Rebuild a number
    Question: Write 40 + 2 + 0.03 + 0.005 in standard form.
    1. Rule: Use a zero in the empty tenths column.
    2. Work: The whole part is 42. The decimal digits are 0, 3 and 5.
    Final answer: 42.035
    

    Example 6 · Read a decimal fraction
    Question: Write 67/100 as a decimal.
    1. Rule: One hundredth is 0.01.
    2. Work: Group the 67 hundredths into 6 tenths and 7 hundredths.
    Final answer: 0.67
    

    Example 7 · Read fractions of a unit
    Question: Write 304/1,000 as a decimal.
    1. Rule: Use the thousandths place for the final digit.
    2. Work: 300 thousandths = 3 tenths. Add 4 thousandths and keep zero hundredths.
    Final answer: 0.304
    

    Example 8 · Compare unequal lengths
    Question: Which is greater, 0.5 or 0.45?
    1. Rule: Put the numbers in the same place-value columns.
    2. Work: Write 0.50 and 0.45. Fifty hundredths exceed forty-five hundredths.
    Final answer: 0.5 > 0.45
    

    Example 9 · Recognise equivalent decimals
    Question: Are 2.5 and 2.500 equal?
    1. Rule: Appending zeros at the right end adds no value.
    2. Work: Both have 2 ones and 5 tenths; the two extra digits contribute zero.
    Final answer: 2.5 = 2.500
    

    Intermediate · Examples 10–21
    Example 10 · Convert a fraction with denominator four
    Question: Write 3/4 as a decimal.
    1. Rule: Make an equivalent fraction with a power-of-ten denominator.
    2. Work: Multiply both numerator and denominator by 25 to get 75/100.
    Final answer: 0.75
    

    Example 11 · Simplify a decimal fraction
    Question: Write 0.125 as a fraction in lowest terms.
    1. Rule: Use the final place to choose the denominator.
    2. Work: 0.125 = 125/1,000. Divide top and bottom by 125.
    Final answer: 1/8
    

    Example 12 · Regroup an unusually large count of tenths
    Question: Write 328 tenths as a decimal.
    1. Rule: Ten tenths make one whole.
    2. Work: 320 tenths give 32 units and 8 tenths are left.
    Final answer: 32.8
    

    Example 13 · Find a precise number-line point
    Question: Which hundredth lies seven ticks after 3.20 when each tick is one hundredth?
    1. Rule: Each step adds 0.01.
    2. Work: Start at 3.20 and add 7 × 0.01 = 0.07.
    Final answer: 3.27
    

    Example 14 · Order decimals
    Question: Arrange 4.09, 4.9, 4.099 and 4.19 in ascending order.
    1. Rule: Compare digits of equal place value.
    2. Work: Write 4.090, 4.900, 4.099 and 4.190. Tenths divide the groups; thousandths decide between 4.090 and 4.099.
    Final answer: 4.09 < 4.099 < 4.19 < 4.9
    

    Example 15 · Convert a reading
    Question: Express 73 mm in centimetres.
    1. Rule: Ten millimetres make one centimetre.
    2. Work: 70 mm = 7 cm and 3 mm = 0.3 cm. Add the two parts.
    Final answer: 7.3 cm
    

    Example 16 · Convert a mixed mass
    Question: Write 2 kg 75 g in kilograms.
    1. Rule: One gram is one thousandth of a kilogram.
    2. Work: 75 g = 75/1,000 kg = 0.075 kg. Add 2 kg.
    Final answer: 2.075 kg
    

    Example 17 · Convert rupees
    Question: Express ₹12.08 entirely in paise.
    1. Rule: A rupee equals 100 paise.
    2. Work: 12 rupees give 1,200 paise and 0.08 rupee gives 8 paise.
    Final answer: 1,208 paise
    

    Example 18 · Add with regrouping
    Question: Find 3.78 + 1.46.
    1. Rule: Add the same place values, exchanging ten for one in the next column.
    2. Work: 8 hundredths + 6 hundredths = 14 hundredths. Carry 1 tenth. The tenths total is 12 tenths; carry 1 whole. The whole part is 5.
    Final answer: 5.24
    

    Example 19 · Add a whole number and a decimal
    Question: Find 17 + 2.605.
    1. Rule: A whole number may be written with zero fractional digits in its columns.
    2. Work: Write 17.000. Adding 2.605 gives 19 wholes with 605 thousandths.
    Final answer: 19.605
    

    Example 20 · Subtract with unequal lengths
    Question: Find 8.4 − 3.275.
    1. Rule: Align points and write 8.400.
    2. Work: Regroup the 4 tenths as 3 tenths and 10 hundredths, then as 9 hundredths and 10 thousandths. Column differences from the right are 5, 2, 1 and 5.
    Final answer: 5.125
    

    Example 21 · Borrow across the point
    Question: Find 9 − 2.086.
    1. Rule: Work in equal-sized place units.
    2. Work: Write 9.000 and exchange a whole through the fractional places. Or subtract 2,086 thousandths from 9,000 thousandths to get 6,914 thousandths.
    Final answer: 6.914
    

    Advanced and applications · Examples 22–30
    Example 22 · Find change after two purchases
    Question: You pay ₹100 for a ₹48.75 item and a ₹26.80 item.
    1. Rule: Compute total cost before subtracting it from the amount paid.
    2. Work: 48.75 + 26.80 = 75.55. Then 100.00 − 75.55 = 24.45. Check 75.55 + 24.45 = 100.00.
    Final answer: ₹24.45
    

    Example 23 · Compare sports times
    Question: A timed sprint takes 12.076 s and another takes 12.08 s. Which is quicker, and by how much?
    1. Rule: The smaller elapsed time is quicker; pad zeros before subtracting.
    2. Work: Write 12.08 as 12.080. Compare 76 and 80 thousandths. Difference = 4 thousandths.
    Final answer: 12.076 s is quicker by 0.004 s.
    

    Example 24 · Use a common length unit
    Question: A board is 1.35 m long and a strip is 875 mm long. Find their combined length in metres.
    1. Rule: One millimetre is 0.001 m.
    2. Work: 875 mm = 0.875 m. Then 1.350 + 0.875 = 2.225.
    Final answer: 2.225 m
    

    Example 25 · Compare equivalent measurements
    Question: Which is greater, 1.205 kg or 1,250 g?
    1. Rule: Express both measurements in grams.
    2. Work: 1.205 kg = 1,205 g. Compare 1,205 and 1,250; the difference is 45 g.
    Final answer: 1,250 g is greater by 45 g.
    

    Example 26 · Find the missing decimal
    Question: Find the number halfway between 3.4 and 3.5.
    1. Rule: The interval is one tenth, or ten hundredths.
    2. Work: Half the interval is 0.05. Add that to 3.40, or subtract it from 3.50.
    Final answer: 3.45
    

    Example 27 · Use decimals beyond thousandths
    Question: Express 3.6048 as a sum of its nonzero places.
    1. Rule: Each new column to the right is one tenth of the previous column.
    2. Work: Use 3 ones, 6 tenths, 4 thousandths and 8 ten-thousandths. The hundredths digit is zero.
    Final answer: 3 + 0.6 + 0.004 + 0.0008
    

    Example 28 · Check a decimal difference
    Question: A student claims 5.02 − 0.8 = 4.94. Explain and correct the error.
    1. Rule: Align tenths with tenths, not with hundredths.
    2. Work: 0.8 = 0.80. Subtract 80 hundredths from 502 hundredths, giving 422 hundredths. The incorrect result comes from subtracting 0.08.
    Final answer: 4.22
    

    Example 29 · Solve a capacity problem
    Question: A jug holds 2 L. It already contains 650 mL. Then 0.875 L is added. How much room remains?
    1. Rule: Use litres for all quantities.
    2. Work: 650 mL = 0.650 L. The new amount is 0.650 + 0.875 = 1.525 L. Subtract from 2.000 L.
    Final answer: 0.475 L, or 475 mL.
    

    Example 30 · Work backwards from a total
    Question: Two lengths sum to 5.075 m. One is 2.96 m. Find the second and verify.
    1. Rule: Subtraction undoes addition.
    2. Work: Pad 2.96 to 2.960. Subtract 2,960 thousandths from 5,075 thousandths. Check 2.960 + 2.115 = 5.075.
    Final answer: 2.115 m
    

    12. Common mistakes and corrections
    An incorrect idea, its correction, and a way to check
    MistakeIncorrectCorrectWhy and how to check
    Counting decimal digits0.45 > 0.50.45 < 0.50Compare matching places, starting with tenths.
    Tenths and hundredths0.05 = 0.50.05 < 0.5Use a hundred-square: 5 cells versus 50 cells.
    Removing the wrong zero2.05 = 2.52.05 = 2.050Delete only zeros at the far right of a fractional part.
    Treating trailing zeros as extra value2.50 > 2.52.50 = 2.5The zero adds zero hundredths.
    Fraction denominator7/100 = 0.77/100 = 0.07Use the second decimal place for hundredths.
    Aligning from the right3.2 + 0.45 = 0.773.20 + 0.45 = 3.65Line up decimal points, then pad zeros.
    Padding the wrong place4.2 − 0.05 = 0.374.20 − 0.05 = 4.15Appending a trailing zero keeps the tenths in place.
    Counting ticks rather than gapsFrom 2.00 to 2.10 with 11 marks, each gap is 0.10/11There are 10 gaps, each 0.01A line with both endpoints marked has one fewer gap than marks.
    Converting metres to centimetres0.8 m = 8 cm0.8 m = 80 cmOne metre gives 100 centimetres; the smaller unit gives a larger count.

    13. Quick revision and exam preparation
    

    Split → Read
    One whole = 10 tenths = 100 hundredths = 1,000 thousandths. A zero inside the fractional part can hold a place.
    Convert → Compare
    Choose a common unit or denominator. Compare whole parts, then fractional digits from left to right.
    Align → Calculate
    Put the points in one column, pad with zeros, then regroup as needed.
    Estimate → Check
    Bound the answer with whole parts. Check a difference by adding the result and the quantity taken away.
    

    Last-minute checklist
    1. Can I justify 0.50 = 0.5 and explain why 0.05 is different?
    2. Have I labelled the scale on a number line and counted the gaps?
    3. Have I aligned places, kept trailing placeholders where helpful, and written the common unit?
    4. Does my answer fit an estimate, and can I check it with the inverse operation?
    5. For assertion–reason questions, decide whether both statements are true before deciding whether the reason explains the assertion.
    

    Review the place-value chart, conversion table, column calculations, and worked examples for a final recap.

    Assertion–reason · Questions 56–60
    Choose A if both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion; B if both are true without that explanation; C if only the assertion is true; or D if only the reason is true.
    1. Assertion: 0.45 < 0.5. Reason: 0.5 is 50 hundredths, while 0.45 is 45 hundredths.
    2. Assertion: 2.08 + 0.92 = 3. Reason: A zero at the end of a fractional part does not change the value.
    3. Assertion: 4.2 m = 420 cm. Reason: One metre contains 10 centimetres.
    4. Assertion: 0.009 > 0.09. Reason: One hundredth equals 10 thousandths.
