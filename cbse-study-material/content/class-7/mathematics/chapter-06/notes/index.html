1. What can you tell without doing the whole calculation?

A lamp is off. Flip its switch 63 times. What is its final state?

Reveal the observation

Each pair of flips restores the starting state. After 31 pairs, the lamp is still off. The last flip turns it on. Only the parity of 63 matters.

Learning objectives

  • Use relative sizes and repeating or growing rules to make predictions.
  • Reason about even and odd numbers, test grid constraints, and construct magic squares.
  • Explain the Virahāṅka–Fibonacci rule and solve digit puzzles using place value and carries.
  • Distinguish an observation from a proof, and use a counterexample to disprove an always-claim.

Number play turns everyday observations into reasoning. Think of seating people in pairs, adding prices from the units digits, checking a set of totals, or building a drum rhythm from one- and two-beat notes.

2. Numbers tell us things

Read a queue from the front. Each person calls the number of strictly taller people ahead. The calls describe relative order without giving actual heights. In the queue 146, 158, 142, 151 cm, the calls are 0, 0, 2, 1.

The call at position \(i\) lies from 0 to \(i-1\). A 0 means that nobody ahead is taller, so several people can call 0. Call 0 is guaranteed for the first person and any tallest person. Call \(i-1\) means that everyone ahead is taller.

Investigation · Rebuild a queue

Make cards of different heights. Record the calls, then hide the order. For distinct heights, reconstruct it from the back: when the last person calls \(k\), choose the \((k+1)\)th tallest remaining card. Remove it and repeat. For the calls 0, 1, 0, the last card is tallest, the second is shortest of the two remaining, and the first is the last card left. Using 1 for the shortest and 3 for the tallest, the ranks from front to back are 2, 1, 3.

Try calls 0, 1, 2, 3. Can any other relative order fit? Explain by checking how many taller people each position requires.

Test a claim: “A person calling 0 must be tallest” is sometimes true. It holds for a tallest person, but fails for the first person if someone later is taller. Finding one such counterexample disproves “always true.” To prove an always-claim, give a reason that works for every allowed input.

3. Picking parity

Parity describes whether an integer is even or odd. An even number of objects forms complete pairs. An odd number leaves one object unpaired. Zero is even because it is \(2\times0\); negative integers follow the same multiple-of-two test.

Parity quick-reference

Even × oddEven8 × 5 = 40
Odd × oddOdd7 × 5 = 35

Two odd sums pair their leftover counters, which makes an even total. An even factor supplies a factor 2. For two odd factors, multiply \((2a+1)(2b+1)\): the two-factor term and the two middle terms are all even, with one extra unit left. A subtraction has the same parity as the corresponding addition because a number and its opposite have the same parity.

Write any even integer as \(2n\), and any odd integer as \(2n+1\), for integer \(n\). When listing the positive even and odd numbers from position 1, use \(2n\) and \(2n-1\) respectively. Consecutive integers \(n,n+1,n+2\) alternate in parity. Consecutive evens are \(2n,2n+2,2n+4\); consecutive odds can be \(2n+1,2n+3,2n+5\).

Use the structure instead of calculating

A sum has the parity of its number of odd addends. Five odds have odd sum, even if the values are unknown. A product is even when at least one factor is even. To check a large numeral, check its units digit: every other place contributes a multiple of 10, which is even.

Investigation · Can this total happen?

Pick three odd cards and two even cards. Predict the parity before adding. Now replace one odd card with an even card. The new sum must change parity because the count of odd cards drops from three to two. Try to construct an even total with three odd and two even cards without changing the counts. Explain why no trial can work.

Other useful checks follow the same pattern. Consecutive page labels on a double-sided sheet are one odd and one even, so those two labels total an odd number. The total of an even number of such complete sheets is even. Check the page-number assumption before using this shortcut.

4. Number grids and magic squares

A row-and-column puzzle using 1–9 once each has total 45. The three row sums and the three column sums must each add to 45. Any individual row or column sum lies from \(1+2+3=6\) through \(7+8+9=24\). These are necessary checks; passing them does not guarantee a solution. If only the parities are known, the number of odd row sums and odd column sums must have the same parity.

A magic square has the same sum on all three rows, three columns and two diagonals. For the digits 1–9, each row totals \(45/3=15\). Its centre is 5. To see why, add the four lines through the centre \(m\). Their combined sum is \(4S\), where \(S\) is the magic sum. These lines count the full grid once and count the centre three extra times, so \(4S=3S+3m\). Therefore \(S=3m\).

Investigation · Construct and transform

Start with centre 5. Opposite cells on a line through 5 must total 10. The digit 1 cannot be in a corner: a corner belongs to three magic lines, but only the pairs 5 and 9, or 6 and 8, can join 1 to total 15. The same reasoning for 9 uses pairs 1 and 5, or 2 and 4. Put 1 and 9 opposite each other at edge middles. The other edge middles are 3 and 7; the even numbers take the corners. Complete the grid and check its eight lines. Rotating or reflecting gives eight layouts of the same 1–9 arrangement.

Add the same \(k\) to all entries and the sum becomes \(S+3k\). Multiply every entry by \(k\) and it becomes \(kS\). Try subtracting 5 for a square with magic sum 0, or doubling to get nonconsecutive entries. In the diagram’s orientation, use its offsets from the centre to build a square centred at any integer \(m\).

History and culture: The chapter connects magic squares with the Lo Shu and Indian yantras. The Chautīsā Yantra at the Pārśhvanath Jain temple in Khajuraho is a 4 × 4 example. The numbers 1–16 total 136; four equal row sums therefore give 34. Look for other sets of four with the same sum.

5. Virahāṅka–Fibonacci rhythms

The textbook starts the sequence at 1, 2. Each later term adds the previous two: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, … . State the starting values when numbering positions; other Fibonacci conventions start differently. To go backwards, subtract the earlier known term from the later one.

Investigation · Count rhythms without listing all of them

Use ordered short notes of one beat and long notes of two beats. One beat allows only 1. Two beats allow 1+1 and 2. Three beats allow 1+1+1, 1+2, and 2+1. For \(n\) beats, a rhythm begins with either 1 or 2. The remaining part then takes \(n-1\) or \(n-2\) beats. These two groups do not overlap, so their counts add.

Make a table through six beats: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 ways. Try listing the five rhythms of length four to check the count. The same reasoning counts stair climbs made with one- and two-step moves.

The parities repeat in the block odd, even, odd. After the first three terms, the next three follow the same recurrence with the same parity pattern. Even terms are at positions 2, 5, 8, …, or \(3k+2\), for whole \(k\ge 0\). For example, 20 is \(3\times6+2\), so term 20 is even without calculating its value.

NCERT traces the rule to Virahāṅka’s work on poetic metre and relates it to the later Fibonacci naming. It uses examples from art, music and nature to prompt observation. A pattern seen in some flowers is a clue to investigate, not a rule for every flower.

6. Digits in disguise

A cryptarithm replaces digits with letters. The same letter always means the same digit; different letters mean different digits. A leading digit of a multi-digit number cannot be zero. Use the ones column first, account for a carry, and then verify the whole sum.

Work through the digit restrictions

In A + A + A = BA, the right side means \(10B+A\), not a product. Rearrange \(3A=10B+A\) to \(2A=10B\). Since \(A\le 9\) and \(B\) is nonzero, \(B=1\) and \(A=5\). Check \(5+5+5=15\).

A puzzle can have more than one solution. For AB + AB = B4, \(2B\) ends in 4, so \(B\) may be 2 or 7. Carry 0 or 1 as appropriate. Both 12 + 12 = 24 and 37 + 37 = 74 pass all the stated rules.

For digits \(a,b,c\), a two-digit numeral AB means \(10a+b\), and a three-digit numeral ABC means \(100a+10b+c\). The reversed two-digit numeral has value \(10b+a\). If \(b=0\), its written reversal begins with zero, so it is not a two-digit number.

Place-value extension · Why the reversal trick works

Choose a two-digit number such as 72. Reverse it and subtract: \(72-27=45\). In letters the signed difference is \(9(a-b)\), which is always a multiple of 9. This also explains why the results can be negative or zero. For a digit-sum check, write a numeral as its digit sum plus multiples of 9: each of 10, 100 and 1,000 is one more than a multiple of 9. Rearranging digits preserves the digit sum. These checks support the puzzles without requiring a catalogue of divisibility rules.

Patterns and claims: A repeating block and a growing sequence use different rules. A finite list can fit many rules. For 2, 4, 6, the constant-step rule gives 8 next, while repeating the block gives 2. Ask which rule is intended. Check a proposed rule on several positions, then justify it from its construction.

8. Step-by-step solved examples

Try each question before opening its solution. The 30 examples include 9 basic, 12 intermediate, and 9 advanced questions.

Basic · Examples 1–9

Example 1 · How many taller people are ahead?

Question: Heights in order from front: 150, 160, 145, 155. What does the last person call?

  1. Rule: Count only taller people ahead.
  2. Work: The earlier heights are 150, 160 and 145. Only 160 exceeds 155.

Final answer: 1

Example 2 · Read the front position

Question: What number must the first person in any line call?

  1. Rule: There is nobody ahead of the first person.
  2. Work: The set of earlier people is empty, whatever the first person’s height.

Final answer: 0

Example 3 · Pair counters

Question: Decide whether 17 is odd or even.

  1. Rule: Group objects in twos.
  2. Work: Sixteen objects make 8 pairs. One object is left.

Final answer: Odd

Example 4 · Check zero

Question: Is 0 even?

  1. Rule: An even integer is a multiple of 2.
  2. Work: Write 0 = 2 × 0. Zero counters leave no unmatched counter.

Final answer: Even

Example 5 · Predict a sum

Question: Is 235 + 487 odd or even?

  1. Rule: Two odd addends have two leftovers that form one pair.
  2. Work: Both units digits are odd. Their sum has even parity without needing the full sum.

Final answer: Even

Example 6 · Predict a difference

Question: What is the parity of 974 − 231?

  1. Rule: Equal parities give an even difference; different parities give an odd one.
  2. Work: 974 is even and 231 is odd. Taking an odd number from an even number leaves an odd result.

Final answer: Odd

Example 7 · Predict a product

Question: What is the parity of 41 × 62?

  1. Rule: An even factor makes the product even.
  2. Work: 62 = 2 × 31, so the whole product contains the factor 2.

Final answer: Even

Example 8 · Use a position rule

Question: Find the 37th positive odd number.

  1. Rule: The nth positive odd number is 2n − 1.
  2. Work: Substitute n = 37: 74 − 1.

Final answer: 73

Example 9 · Write a number using digits

Question: Represent a two-digit number with tens digit a and ones digit b.

  1. Rule: The tens digit is worth ten times its face value.
  2. Work: Add the contributions 10 × a and 1 × b. For a = 6 and b = 2, this gives 62.

Final answer: 10a + b

Intermediate · Examples 10–21

Example 10 · Read a complete height code

Question: Find the calls for heights 158, 141, 166, 150.

  1. Rule: For each position, count strictly taller predecessors.
  2. Work: First says 0. At 141, only 158 is taller. At 166, none is taller. At 150, 158 and 166 are taller.

Final answer: 0, 1, 0, 2

Example 11 · Rebuild from height calls

Question: For four distinct height ranks, shortest = 1, find the order for calls 0, 0, 2, 1.

  1. Rule: From the back, choose the (call + 1)th tallest remaining rank.
  2. Work: Last calls 1, so choose 3. Third calls 2, so choose the shortest remaining, 1. Second calls 0, so choose 4. The remaining rank is 2.

Final answer: 2, 4, 1, 3

Example 12 · Count flips

Question: A switch starts off and is flipped 57 times. Where does it end?

  1. Rule: Two flips restore the original state.
  2. Work: There are 28 pairs and one extra flip. Only the extra one changes the state.

Final answer: On

Example 13 · Group three odds

Question: Is 9 + 15 + 23 + 100 odd or even?

  1. Rule: Count the odd addends.
  2. Work: There are three odd addends, so there is one leftover after two leftovers pair. Adding 100 changes no parity.

Final answer: Odd

Example 14 · Choose an algebraic form

Question: Is 6n + 7 always odd for integer n?

  1. Rule: An even coefficient makes the variable part even.
  2. Work: 6n is twice 3n. The constant 7 is odd; their sum is odd.

Final answer: Always odd

Example 15 · Use consecutive integers

Question: Find the sum of three consecutive integers starting at n. When is it even?

  1. Rule: Write them as n, n + 1, n + 2.
  2. Work: The sum is 3n + 3 = 3(n + 1). Three is odd, so the parity equals that of n + 1.

Final answer: Even exactly when the middle integer n + 1 is even.

Example 16 · Spot an impossible grid total

Question: A 1–9 grid has row sums 12, 15, 18 and claimed column sums 9, 15, 20. Is it possible?

  1. Rule: Adding all row sums or all column sums counts the same grid.
  2. Work: The row total is 45. The column total is 44. No arrangement can have both.

Final answer: Impossible

Example 17 · Restrict a row total

Question: Could a row in a 1–9 grid sum to 25?

  1. Rule: Three distinct entries have a maximum allowed sum.
  2. Work: The greatest possible three entries are 9, 8 and 7. They sum to 24, below 25.

Final answer: No

Example 18 · Complete one magic-square row

Question: A 1–9 magic square has top row 4, 9, □. Find the missing number.

  1. Rule: Total 45 split equally across three rows gives 15 per row.
  2. Work: Subtract 4 + 9 = 13 from 15. The missing entry is compatible with the rotated square shown in the notes.

Final answer: 2

Example 19 · Shift a magic square

Question: Add 4 to all cells of a magic square of sum 15. Find its new sum.

  1. Rule: Every line has three entries.
  2. Work: The line gains 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. The centre increases from 5 to 9.

Final answer: 27

Example 20 · Continue the chapter’s sequence

Question: Continue 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 with the Virahāṅka rule.

  1. Rule: Add the previous two terms.
  2. Work: 5 + 8 = 13. Then 8 + 13 = 21.

Final answer: 13, 21

Example 21 · Go backwards in the sequence

Question: Two successive terms are 34, 55. Find the previous two terms in increasing order.

  1. Rule: Subtract the smaller known term from the larger to reverse the rule.
  2. Work: 55 − 34 = 21. Then 34 − 21 = 13.

Final answer: 13, 21

Advanced and application · Examples 22–30

Example 22 · Prove a magic centre rule

Question: A 3 × 3 magic square has sum S and centre m. Show that S = 3m.

  1. Rule: Add the four lines passing through the centre.
  2. Work: The eight outside cells appear once, the centre four times. These lines total 3S + 3m because the whole square is 3S. They also total 4S, so S = 3m.

Final answer: S = 3m

Example 23 · Build a magic square with zero sum

Question: Construct a nonzero integer magic square with sum 0.

  1. Rule: Translate a 1–9 magic square by subtracting 5 from every entry.
  2. Work: Start with rows 4,9,2; 3,5,7; 8,1,6. Every line decreases by 15, so its sum is zero.

Final answer: Rows −1,4,−3; −2,0,2; 3,−4,1.

Example 24 · Solve a parity-grid constraint

Question: Can a 2 × 3 grid have two odd row sums and exactly one odd column sum?

  1. Rule: Parity of the whole total is independent of grouping.
  2. Work: Odd + odd gives an even total. One odd column with two even columns gives an odd total. The two conclusions conflict.

Final answer: Impossible

Example 25 · Count rhythms

Question: How many ordered rhythms of seven beats use only notes of length 1 or 2?

  1. Rule: Split by the first note.
  2. Work: Lengths 5 and 6 have 8 and 13 rhythms. Starting with 1 leaves 6 beats and starting with 2 leaves 5. Add 13 and 8.

Final answer: 21

Example 26 · Find a distant term’s parity

Question: Find the parity of the 29th term in 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, … without finding its value.

  1. Rule: The parity cycle is odd, even, odd, and repeats.
  2. Work: Each next parity is the sum of the preceding two. Even terms occupy positions 2, 5, 8, … . As 29 = 3 × 9 + 2, position 29 is even.

Final answer: Even

Example 27 · Solve a cryptarithm

Question: In A + A + A = BA, different letters are different digits and B is nonzero. Find them.

  1. Rule: Use 3A = 10B + A.
  2. Work: Then 2A = 10B. Since A is at most 9, B must be 1. This gives A = 5 and 5 + 5 + 5 = 15.

Final answer: A = 5; B = 1.

Example 28 · Use a carry

Question: In AB + AB = B4, find the digits using the usual distinct-letter and leading rules.

  1. Rule: The ones column forces 2B to end in 4.
  2. Work: B is 2 or 7. For B = 2, carry 0 gives 2A = 2, so A = 1. For B = 7, carry 1 gives 2A + 1 = 7, so A = 3.

Final answer: 12 + 12 = 24 and 37 + 37 = 74.

Example 29 · Explain a reversal trick

Question: Why is the difference between a two-digit number and its reverse divisible by 9?

  1. Rule: Use place-value expressions and subtract.
  2. Work: (10a + b) − (10b + a) = 9a − 9b = 9(a − b). An initial 0 in the reverse does not affect the value.

Final answer: The signed difference is always a multiple of 9.

Example 30 · Refute a universal claim

Question: A learner says an odd total always comes from exactly one odd addend. Is that true?

  1. Rule: One counterexample disproves an always-claim.
  2. Work: 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 gives an odd sum from three odd addends. The condition is an odd number of odd addends.

Final answer: False; three odd addends can give an odd total.

10. Quick revision and exam preparation

Compare → Encode

Only count taller people ahead. With distinct heights, decode from the back using the remaining ranks.

Pair → Predict

Even means no leftover; odd means one. Count odd addends, or look for an even factor.

Count → Check

Grid totals must agree. A magic square adds diagonal constraints. For rhythms, split by the first note.

Reason → Verify

Use place value in digit puzzles, check carries and distinct digits, and test a conjecture with counterexamples.

Last-minute checklist

  1. Did I state the rule and where counting starts?
  2. Did I explain why a parity shortcut is valid?
  3. Did I test both diagonal sums as well as rows and columns?
  4. Does a proposed cryptarithm solution satisfy every column and the original sum?
  5. For assertion–reason questions, decide whether both parts are true before checking the explanation.

Assertion–reason · Questions 56–60

Choose A if both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion, B if both are true without that explanation, C if only the assertion is true, or D if only the reason is true.

  1. Assertion: The sum of two odd integers is even. Reason: Their two unmatched counters form one extra pair.
  2. Assertion: Every 1–9 magic square has magic sum 15. Reason: Two odd integers add to an even integer.
  3. Assertion: 2n − 1 gives the nth positive odd number. Reason: n must start at zero to obtain 1.
  4. Assertion: A three-digit numeral ABC has value A+B+C. Reason: A in the hundreds column contributes 100A.
  5. Assertion: Two tallies for a grid, one by rows and one by columns, have the same sum. Reason: Both tally every cell exactly once.
