Before you start

Which batter is more consistent? How tall is a typical student in your class? Can one unusually high price change the average? What makes a graph persuasive or misleading? Statistics helps you answer these questions by checking what was measured and looking for patterns.

What you’ll learn

  • Ask questions that need data.
  • Find and interpret the mean, median and range.
  • Read and build dot plots and clustered bar graphs.
  • Explain what a dataset can support.

Plan your study

Allow about 100–120 minutes for the core notes, then practise in short sessions. It helps to know how to order decimals, add several values and divide a total into equal parts.

5.1 · Of Questions and Statements

A statistical question expects variation and needs a collection of observations. ‘How long do Class 7 students spend travelling to school?’ expects a range of answers. ‘What is the school’s official opening time today?’ asks for a single fact. A claim such as ‘Most surveyed students take less than 25 minutes’ needs a stated group and evidence.

Collect and organise data

Define the group, question, unit and observation period first. Use the same unit throughout. Record each observation once, including repeats. A value of zero can be an observation, while a blank means missing unless the data say otherwise. Use a frequency table when values repeat.

Materials. Paper and a pencil.

  1. Ask everyone one measurable question, such as how many minutes they travelled to class today.
  2. Use anonymous responses. Make a frequency table or ordered list.
  3. Check that recorded frequencies add to the number of responses.

A question about a group can have many different answers, and the same value may occur several times.

5.2 · Representative Values and the Mean

A representative value summarises a collection. The arithmetic mean uses every observation. \(\text{Mean}=\frac{\text{sum of observations}}{\text{number of observations}}\). When the mean and count are known, total = mean × count. Count every repeated value and keep the unit of the original observations.

Fair share

Four groups start with 2, 6, 9 and 3 litres. Their total is 20 L. Redistribute the water equally and each group gets 5 L. The total stays at 20 L. An average can be fractional: sharing 7 litres among two containers gives 3.5 L each.

Balance point

Place equal-weight counters at 2, 4, 6 and 8 on an evenly spaced number line. At 5, the left distances add to 3 + 1 and the right distances add to 1 + 3. In general, signed distances from the mean add to zero because total − count × mean = 0.

Averages around us

Use a mean to describe runs per innings, water use per day, or cost per purchase under like conditions. A mean of 2.5 books is possible although no student read half a book in a survey of completed books. In these notes, cricket activities calculate mean runs per selected innings. Official batting average uses dismissals as its denominator.

5.2 · Outliers, Medians and the Ends of Data

Sort first. For an odd count, take the one middle observation. For an even count, average the two middle values. With 4, 1, 9, 3, 6, order to 1, 3, 4, 6, 9, so the median is 4. With 4, 1, 9, 6, order to 1, 4, 6, 9 and average 4 and 6 to get 5.

An outlier is an observation unusually far from the rest in its context. It may be real, or it may signal a recording error. Changing only the largest observation of 4, 6, 7, 9, 10 to 40 raises the mean from 7.2 to 13.2. The median stays at 7 because the middle position is unchanged. A different change can move the median too, especially if the ordering changes.

What you want to knowUseful measure and why
Even sharing of a totalMean divides the total by the count.
A typical value when a few prices are far higherMedian can be less sensitive to extremes.
How far apart the ends areRange is maximum minus minimum.

Of Ends and the Essence

Minimum and maximum show the ends of the data. The range describes how far apart they are, in the same unit. For 128, 134, 134, 142 and 151 cm, the range is 151 − 128 = 23 cm. Equal ranges can hide very different clusters, and equal means can hide very different ranges.

Compare two classes

Materials. Use original or anonymous sample heights in centimetres.

  1. Use the same intervals and axis limits for both groups.
  2. Compare group sizes, medians, minimums and maximums.
  3. Point to an overlapping interval.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

An average for a group does not determine an individual’s height. Dot plots show overlap and variability that one number hides.

5.3 · Visualising Data

Dot plots

Place one dot for each observation on a labelled numerical axis. Equal changes in value need equal horizontal distances. Stack repeats directly above the same tick. Count all the dots to recover the sample size. Look for stacks, clusters, gaps and possible unusual observations.

Clubbing the Columns

A clustered bar graph puts two or more series side by side for each category. Match the legend before comparing. Use one axis and one numerical scale for all series. Draw equal bar widths and consistent gaps. Ordinary bar lengths start at zero so that the height comparison represents the values. With an increment of 5, three grid intervals represent 15 units.

Summer and Winter at the Same Time

The textbook asks what monthly data measured in hours might represent. It explores daylight and opposite seasons in the hemispheres. Check the measurement and the units before interpreting. For a separate sample weather comparison, the temperature in North City peaks near July and the temperature in South City peaks near January. These sample temperatures are fictional.

Compare daylight totals fairly when months have different lengths. In a sample comparison, 270 hours over 30 days averages 9 hours per day, while 434 hours over 31 days averages 14 hours per day. Compare the daily means before discussing seasonal changes. A daylight duration in hours and a temperature in degrees describe different measurements.

All It Takes Is a Minute

A sports graph can show runs in each over at a glance. Read ‘runs per over’ as the number scored in that over. For a match total, add the over values. For a timing challenge, make a dot plot of your stopwatch results and compare them with the 60-second target. Repeated attempts measure consistency as well as closeness to the target.

5.4 · Data Detective and Telling Tall Tales

Separate observation, inference and a new question. ‘Two groups overlap between 140 and 150 cm’ is a visible observation. ‘The difference might depend on the age mix’ is a possible explanation to investigate. ‘Everyone in Group A is taller’ requires checking every comparison.

Compare like measurements and named groups. Check if values are missing. Different group sizes require comparing a mean with a mean or a proportion with a proportion. Correct labels, equal intervals and a visible zero for ordinary bars help keep a graph fair.

A labelled zoomed view can help inspect small changes, such as changes in average height between surveys. Keep the scale visible and avoid treating the drawn heights of truncated bars as ratios of the underlying values. For example, a change from 158.2 cm to 159.1 cm is 0.9 cm on either scale.

ClaimWhat the data can support
‘East onions cost 4 rupees/kg more than West onions.’Supported by the shown sample: 32 − 28 = 4 rupees/kg.
‘East is always more expensive.’Unjustified. Some listed products cost less in East. Other dates were not measured.
‘The mean height is 145 cm, so most learners are exactly 145 cm.’The mean alone gives neither the mode nor the shape. Inspect the plot.

  1. Make separate dot plots with a common axis.
  2. Identify a typical value, a gap and any overlap.
  3. Write one supported claim and one question the data leave open.

Investigations and exam skills

Use the sequence: ask a measurable question, collect observations consistently, choose a display, calculate a suitable summary, and state the result for the group you actually measured.

Compare book pages

Materials. Two books and a tally sheet.

  1. Pick several full lines from each book.
  2. Count words in each line and plot both groups on the same scale.
  3. Compare median and range, and describe the overlap.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

A higher median word count suggests a longer typical line in the selected sample. The range shows how much line counts vary.

Try next. Could the type size or page width explain some differences?

Compare sentence and name lengths

Materials. Two books and a set of invented or anonymised first names.

  1. Count words per complete sentence from two pages. Then count letters per name separately.
  2. Make a dot plot of each numerical dataset and find the mean, median and range.
  3. Sort initials alphabetically. Count first letters and compare the frequency of vowel and consonant starts with paired bars.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

Sentence and name lengths are numerical. Initials are categories: frequencies or alphabetical positions are meaningful, while an arithmetic mean of the letters has no everyday interpretation.

Try next. What changes if you take sentences from a different type of book?

Estimate and measure

Materials. Ruler, paper and three classroom objects.

  1. Estimate each length in centimetres before measuring.
  2. Draw clustered bars for each estimate and measurement.
  3. Calculate the absolute error for each object, then find the mean error.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

Estimates of 12, 6 and 20 cm and measurements of 13, 5 and 18 cm give errors of 1, 1 and 2 cm. The mean absolute error is 4/3 cm, about 1.33 cm.

Try next. How does averaging absolute differences avoid cancellation? Track a different daily count for two weeks and compare the weeks with dot plots.

Compare one-minute and three-minute estimates

Materials. Stopwatch. Ask only willing volunteers and skip recording names.

  1. Use the one-minute tool and repeat with the three-minute target.
  2. Make a dot plot for each target. Compare errors from 60 and 180 seconds.
  3. Compare median error and range of errors instead of treating raw durations as like values.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

A group can be close to the target on average and still make widely varying estimates. Signed errors may cancel.

Materials. Original code puzzle with three distinct digits.

30 step-by-step worked examples

Basic

Example 1 · Classify a question

Question. Which needs data: students’ travel times or the posted starting time?

Method. Variability

  1. Ask whether responses can differ across learners.
  2. Travel times vary, so collect several observations.
  3. The posted starting time is a single stated fact.

Answer. Travel times is the statistical question.

Example 2 · Count frequencies

Question. The observations are 2, 3, 3, 5, 5, 5.

Method. Counting

  1. List each distinct number.
  2. Count 2 once, 3 twice and 5 three times.
  3. Check 1 + 2 + 3 = 6.

Answer. Frequencies 1, 2 and 3.

Example 3 · Share water fairly

Question. Pails contain 2, 6, 9 and 3 L.

Method. Fair share

  1. Add to 20 L.
  2. There are four pails.
  3. Divide 20 L by 4.

Answer. 5 L per pail.

Example 4 · Find a mean with repeats

Question. Data: 3, 3, 9.

Method. Mean

  1. Include both 3s.
  2. Total = 15.
  3. Divide by 3.

Answer. 5.

Example 5 · Find an odd median

Question. Data: 8, 2, 5, 3, 9.

Method. Median

  1. Sort to 2, 3, 5, 8, 9.
  2. The third entry has two neighbours on each side.

Answer. 5.

Example 6 · Find an even median

Question. Data: 8, 2, 5, 3.

Method. Median

  1. Sort to 2, 3, 5, 8.
  2. Use the second and third entries 3 and 5.
  3. Divide their sum 8 by 2.

Answer. 4.

Example 7 · Read the ends

Question. Heights: 132, 138, 141, 150 cm.

Method. Range

  1. Minimum = 132 cm.
  2. Maximum = 150 cm.
  3. 150 − 132 = 18.

Answer. 18 cm.

Example 8 · Find a total from a mean

Question. Five days average 7 L.

Method. Reverse mean

  1. The mean is 7 L per day.
  2. The count is five.
  3. Multiply 7 by 5.

Answer. 35 L.

Example 9 · Read stacked dots

Question. One dot at 2, two at 3, three at 5.

Method. Dot plot

  1. Count all dots: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6.
  2. The tallest stack is at 5.
  3. The end values are 2 and 5.

Answer. Six observations, most common value 5, range 3.

Intermediate

Example 10 · Balance a beam

Question. Equal weights at 2, 4, 6 and 8.

Method. Mean as balance

  1. Total 20 ÷ 4 = 5.
  2. Left distances from 5 are 3 and 1.
  3. Right distances are 1 and 3.

Answer. Balance point 5.

Example 11 · Mean as a fraction

Question. Readings are 4.5, 5 and 5.5 L.

Method. Exact division

  1. Combine 4.5 and 5.5 to make 10.
  2. Add 5 to get total 15 L.
  3. Divide by three.

Answer. 5 L.

Example 12 · Recover a missing observation

Question. The mean of 3, 8, x and 12 is 7.

Method. Missing value

  1. Total required is 4 × 7 = 28.
  2. Known total is 3 + 8 + 12 = 23.
  3. Subtract 23 from 28.

Answer. x = 5.

Example 13 · Compare centre and ends

Question. A: 4, 6, 8. B: 1, 6, 11.

Method. Centre and variability

  1. Both total 18 across three observations.
  2. Both sorted groups have a middle 6.
  3. Ranges are 8 − 4 and 11 − 1.

Answer. Both centres are 6. B has the larger range, 10 versus 4.

Example 14 · Replace a high value

Question. Change 10 to 40 in 4, 6, 7, 9, 10.

Method. Outlier effect

  1. The total rises from 36 to 66.
  2. Divide each by 5 to get 7.2 and 13.2.
  3. The ordered middle remains 7.

Answer. The mean rises by 6. Median stays 7.

Example 15 · Compare shop bars

Question. East onions 32 and West onions 28 rupees/kg.

Method. Matched categories

  1. Select onion in both series.
  2. Keep the unit the same.
  3. Subtract 28 from 32.

Answer. East is 4 rupees/kg dearer in the sample.

Example 16 · Read a vertical scale

Question. A launch bar reaches four intervals of 5 launches each.

Method. Axis intervals

  1. Start at zero.
  2. Multiply 4 intervals by 5 launches per interval.

Answer. 20 launches.

Example 17 · Compare seasons

Question. North City has 8°C in Jan, 28°C in Jul. South City has 26°C and 11°C.

Method. Temperature comparison

  1. North increases by 20°C.
  2. South falls by 15°C.
  3. The warmer city differs by month.

Answer. North warms from Jan to Jul. South cools.

Example 18 · Combine per-over counts

Question. A fictional team scores 4, 8, 3, 9, 6 runs per over.

Method. Sum and mean

  1. Add the five bar heights to get 30 runs.
  2. Divide by five for runs per over.

Answer. 30 total, 6 runs per over on average.

Example 19 · Check a supported claim

Question. Group A: 142, 147, 151 cm. Group B: 138, 145, 154 cm.

Method. Overlap

  1. A median is 147 and B median is 145.
  2. B’s maximum 154 exceeds A’s maximum 151.
  3. Compare the claim ‘everyone in A is taller’.

Answer. The median comparison is supported but the individual claim fails.

Example 20 · Interpret missing values

Question. A table lists rainfall 0 mm, blank and 6 mm.

Method. Missingness

  1. Zero is a recorded dry day.
  2. The blank is not measured.
  3. Average the two recorded readings only.

Answer. 3 mm for the observed days.

Example 21 · Decide on a typical price

Question. Fictional quotes: 28, 31, 31, 34 and 96 rupees/kg.

Method. Mean and median

  1. Total is 220, so mean is 44.
  2. Sorted middle is 31.
  3. The unusually large quote pulls the mean upward.

Answer. Median 31 rupees/kg describes a typical quote.

Advanced and competency-based

Example 22 · Combine unequal groups

Question. Four students average 12 and six average 17.

Method. Weight by group count

  1. Totals are 4 × 12 = 48 and 6 × 17 = 102.
  2. Combined total 150 across 10 observations.
  3. Divide 150 by 10.

Answer. Combined mean 15.

Example 23 · Estimate timing accuracy

Question. Attempts: 52, 57, 62, 65 and 69 s.

Method. Closeness and consistency

  1. The median estimate is 62 s.
  2. The range is 69 − 52 = 17 s.
  3. Absolute errors from 60 are 8, 3, 2, 5 and 9 s.

Answer. Closest attempt 62 s, with error 2 s.

Example 24 · Find an unknown from two totals

Question. Six scores averaged 14. A seventh score makes the average 16.

Method. Total from mean

  1. Six scores total 84.
  2. Seven scores total 112.
  3. The added score is the difference 112 − 84.

Answer. 28.

Example 25 · Spot an axis exaggeration

Question. Two counts are 48 and 50. A graph starts at 47.

Method. Graph integrity

  1. The actual ratio is 50/48, just above 1.
  2. The drawn lengths are 3 and 1 from baseline 47.
  3. Use a zero baseline to show magnitude fairly.

Answer. The truncated graph exaggerates the difference.

Example 26 · A balance with a missing point

Question. Four weights at 1, 2, 5 and x balance at 4.

Method. Sum from balance

  1. Required sum = 4 × 4 = 16.
  2. Known sum = 8.
  3. Put the missing weight at 8.

Answer. x = 8.

Example 27 · Read a multi-year pattern

Question. Orbit counts 5, 8, 12. Zenith counts 3, 5, 7.

Method. Compare growth

  1. Orbit increases by 12 − 5 = 7.
  2. Zenith increases by 7 − 3 = 4.
  3. Compare the increases.

Answer. Orbit’s increase is 3 launches greater.

Example 28 · Compare stability

Question. Batter A: 18, 20, 22. B: 5, 20, 35.

Method. Mean and range

  1. Each total = 60, so each mean = 20.
  2. Ranges: 22 − 18 = 4 and 35 − 5 = 30.
  3. On these innings A has less spread.

Answer. A is more consistent by range.

Example 29 · Predict effect of adding 3

Question. Start with 2, 4, 6. Add 3 to every observation.

Method. Translation

  1. New values are 5, 7, 9.
  2. Both mean and median rise from 4 to 7.
  3. The ends move equally, so range stays 4.

Answer. Mean and median +3, range unchanged.

Example 30 · Construct two data stories

Question. Find two five-value datasets with median 6 and different ranges.

Method. Median positions

  1. Choose the middle 6 in each sorted list.
  2. Use 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, which has range 4.
  3. Widen the ends to form 1, 5, 6, 7, 12, range 11.

Answer. Both medians are 6. The ranges are 4 and 11.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Count repeats
Incorrect. Divide 2+2+8 by 2.
Correction. There are three observations, so 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
Sort first
Incorrect. Read 2 as the median of 8, 2, 4.
Correction. Sort to 2, 4, 8. The middle is 4.
Use both middles
Incorrect. Choose 3 alone from 1, 3, 7, 9.
Correction. Average 3 and 7 to get 5.
Keep zero and blank distinct
Incorrect. Count a blank as 0 automatically.
Correction. Treat it as missing unless the table explicitly says zero.
Subtract endpoints
Incorrect. Report range as 17 for data 9, 12, 17.
Correction. Range = 17 − 9 = 8.
Recognise an outlier’s impact
Incorrect. Assume a very high value changes median by the same amount as mean.
Correction. Check ordered positions. Only the total directly controls the mean.
Place dots evenly
Incorrect. Use equal spacing for 1, 2 and 10.
Correction. Give equal numeric intervals equal width. Separate 2 and 10 by eight units.
Use one common baseline
Incorrect. Start bars at 47 so counts of 48 and 50 look three times apart.
Correction. Start at 0 for an ordinary chart of magnitudes.
Match the legend
Incorrect. Compare the wrong town’s bar.
Correction. Identify each colour or pattern from the legend before reading values.
Check inference
Incorrect. Claim everyone in the higher-mean class is taller.
Correction. Compare the ranges and overlap, and state only what was measured.
Weight group means
Incorrect. Average means of groups of 4 and 10 directly.
Correction. Multiply each mean by its group size, then divide the combined total by 14.
Include units and group
Incorrect. Write ‘the mean is 19’ without saying what was measured.
Correction. For example, ‘mean was 19 runs per over for the selected overs’.

How do you find the mean?

Add every observation and divide by the count.

What do you do before finding the median?

Sort the observations in order.

How does the even median work?

Find the two centre positions and average their values.

How do you find the range?

Subtract the minimum from the maximum.

What does the tallest stack show?

It shows the value that occurs most in the displayed data.

What should a graph conclusion include?

Name the group, measurement unit and the evidence behind the claim.
