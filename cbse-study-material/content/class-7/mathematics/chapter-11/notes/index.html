Before you start

Two bells ring every 6 and 8 minutes. If both ring now, when will they next ring together? If you have ribbons 18 and 24 centimetres long, what is the longest equal-length piece you can cut from both without waste? One question asks for the first shared multiple. The other asks for the greatest shared factor.

Finding Common Ground is Chapter 3 of Ganita Prakash Part II and Chapter 11 in the website’s continuous sequence. Review divisibility, multiplication and simple expressions before you begin. See Arithmetic Expressions and Number Play.

Learn to list factors and multiples, factorise numbers into primes, calculate HCF and LCM in two ways, and choose the right operation for grouping or synchronising. Allow about 2 hours over several sessions for the notes and selected activities.

3.1 · The Greatest of All

A positive factor divides a positive integer exactly. The smallest positive factor of n is 1 and the greatest is n. One has just one positive factor. Rectangles of 24 objects have side lengths 1 and 24, 2 and 12, 3 and 8, or 4 and 6. Each pair multiplies to 24. Try increasing possible divisors until the partner would be smaller than the divisor. A square pair such as 6 × 6 contributes 6 only once.

A common factor belongs to every factor list. The common factors of 12 and 18 are 1, 2, 3 and 6. Their highest common factor (HCF), also called their greatest common factor, is 6. A 12 m by 18 m rectangular floor can fit 6 m square models across both sides. A 9 m square fails because it cannot cover the 12 m dimension exactly.

Tile a floor with the fewest whole-unit squares

Materials. Squared paper and pencil.

  1. Draw a rectangle 12 units wide and 18 units long.
  2. Try 2-, 3- and 6-unit square tiles. Check where a 4-unit tile leaves a gap.
  3. Connect the successful lengths to the factor lists.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

Successful side lengths divide both dimensions. The HCF gives the largest tile, and the number of tiles is (12 ÷ 6) × (18 ÷ 6) = 6.

Prime factorisation and factors inside it

A prime has exactly two positive factors, 1 and itself. A composite number has more than two. One is neither. Two is the only even prime because larger even numbers have 2 as a proper factor. To factorise, split composite branches until all leaves are prime, or divide repeatedly by prime factors until the quotient is 1. A prime’s factorisation is itself. Different valid trees for the same number have the same prime leaves, including repeats.

\(36=2^2\times 3^2\) · \(60=2^2\times 3\times 5\) · \(72=2^3\times 3^2\).

Every factor comes from a subpart of the prime factorisation. For \(36=2^2\times 3^2\), combine 0, 1 or 2 copies of 2 with 0, 1 or 2 copies of 3. Selecting neither prime gives 1. The nine factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 36. For \(p^{a}q^{b}\), the count is \((a+1)(b+1)\), since each prime’s exponent can range from 0 through the given exponent.

Compare two factor trees

Materials. Paper, or the interactive tree builder below.

  1. Split 72 as 8 × 9. Break both factors down until prime.
  2. Start again with 72 = 6 × 12. Break down all composite parts.
  3. Sort and compare the two lists of leaves.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

Both lists give three 2s and two 3s. Changing the first split changes the shape of the tree while the prime factorisation stays fixed.

HCF by shared prime copies

Find the greatest subpart that appears in every prime factorisation. For 24 and 36, both have at least two copies of 2 and one copy of 3, while 36 has no third copy of 2 and 24 has no second copy of 3. Multiply the minima: \(2^2\times 3=12\). A prime missing from any input has minimum exponent zero and contributes no factor. The same rule works for three or more numbers.

3.2 · Least, but not Last!

Positive multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16 and so on. They come from multiplying 4 by positive integers. If the multiplier may be zero, 0 is a multiple too, but LCM always asks for the least positive common multiple. A factor of a positive number fits into it; a multiple contains a whole number of copies of it.

Positive multiples of 6 include 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30. The common positive multiples of 4 and 6 are 12, 24, 36 and so on. They are all multiples of their LCM, 12. Compare ribbon strips joined end to end: a shared length is a multiple of each strip length, and the shortest shared length is the LCM.

LCM by including every required prime copy

Every multiple contains the original number’s prime factors, possibly with extra copies or extra primes. For 12 and 18, their factored forms are \(2^2\times 3\) and \(2\times 3^2\). A common multiple needs two copies of 2 and two copies of 3. The minimum number that contains all these copies is \(2^2\times 3^2=36\). Take the greatest exponent needed for each prime across the inputs, including primes occurring in only one. This works for two or more inputs.

Find repeated meetings

Materials. Squared paper and two coloured pencils.

  1. On one line mark jumps of 4, and on a second line mark jumps of 6 from the same zero.
  2. Compare every position at which both lines land.
  3. Divide later shared positions by the first positive shared position.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

The first positive meeting is 12. Each later meeting is a multiple of 12. A position must contain every factor copy needed for each jump size.

3.3 · Patterns, Properties, and a Pretty Procedure!

What the pairs have in common

A conjecture is a pattern proposed for testing. A generalisation explains why the pattern holds for all inputs in the claim. If one positive integer divides the other, the smaller is the HCF and the larger is the LCM. For co-prime numbers the HCF is 1 and the LCM is their product. Co-prime describes a pair. For example, 8 and 15 are co-prime though both are composite.

PairHCFReason
Consecutive positive integers1A common divisor divides their difference, 1.
Consecutive even integers2A common divisor divides their difference, 2, and both are even.
Consecutive odd integers1A common divisor divides 2, and both inputs are odd.
Any two even integersAt least 2Both contain the prime factor 2.
a and a multiple kaaa divides both, and no larger number divides a.

Two multiples of 3 have an LCM divisible by 3. For consecutive even integers a and b the LCM is \(ab/2\). Consecutive integers have LCM equal to their product because they are co-prime. Doubling both inputs doubles HCF and LCM. More generally, multiplying both by k adds the prime factors of k to both lists, so both answers are multiplied by k. In particular, the HCF of kr and ks is exactly k when r and s are co-prime.

Shared division ladder

Divide both numbers by a common factor and repeat until the last pair is co-prime. The product of the extracted factors is the HCF. The LCM is that common product times both last entries. For 84 and 132, common divisions by 2, 2 and 3 leave 7 and 11. Thus HCF = 12 and LCM = 12 × 7 × 11 = 924. One division by 12 works too. For a ladder with three inputs, take shared divisors for the HCF. For the LCM, continue by primes dividing at least one remaining entry, carrying any unchanged entry down, until all entries are 1.

HCF × LCM for two positive integers

For each prime with exponents a and b, the HCF uses the smaller and the LCM uses the larger. The sum of those two exponents is a + b, the exponent in the product of the inputs. Therefore HCF × LCM equals the product of the two positive inputs. The LCM cannot exceed that product because the product is already a common multiple.

For 24 and 36, \(12\times 72=864=24\times 36\). If HCF = 6 and LCM = 90 and one input is 18, the other is \((6\times 90)/18=30\). Always verify the proposed pair. With three inputs, the middle exponent can be lost: 4, 6 and 12 have HCF 2 and LCM 12, and their product 288 differs from 24.

Scale and test a conjecture

Materials. Paper or the HCF and LCM visualiser.

  1. Find HCF and LCM of 8 and 12. Repeat for 16 and 24.
  2. Compare 3 × 8 and 3 × 12. Explain what factor 3 adds to both exponent lists.
  3. Test HCF × LCM with two numbers, then find a three-number counterexample.
Reveal the observation and reasoning

Both common measures scale by the common multiplier. The two-input product identity works because minimum plus maximum exhaust the pair of exponents.

Formula and method cards

Factors and multiples

d divides n exactly when n = d × k for a whole-number k.

4 is a factor of 24; 24 is a multiple of 4.

Picture rows going into an array.

Primes and composites

A prime has exactly two positive factors. A composite has more than two.

2 is prime, 9 is composite, and 1 is neither.

Check whether there is a factor beyond 1 and itself.

Prime factorisation

Keep splitting composites until the leaves are all prime.

72 = 2³ × 3².

Verify that every leaf is prime and all leaves multiply back to the original.

Divisibility

Use the digit test for the divisor you are testing.

4158 passes the tests for 9 and 11.

Use the digit sum for 9 and alternate sums for 11.

HCF

Include the minimum copies of each prime across all input factorisations.

HCF(24, 36) = 2² × 3 = 12.

A shared divisor fits inside every input.

LCM

Include the maximum copies of each prime across all inputs.

LCM(24, 36) = 2³ × 3² = 72.

A shared multiple contains every input.

Co-prime pairs

Two positive integers are co-prime if their HCF is 1.

8 and 15 have no shared prime.

Co-prime refers to the pair. Its members can be composite.

HCF and LCM product

For two positive integers only, product = HCF × LCM.

24 × 36 = 12 × 72.

Smallest and largest exponents together recover both copies.

Basic

Example 1 · Find the factor pairs of 24

Given. 24 objects

Find. List all positive factors

Rule. Try divisors from the smallest upward.

  1. 1 × 24 and 2 × 12 give the first two pairs.
  2. 3 × 8 and 4 × 6 give the next two. At 5 there is no exact division.
  3. After 4, any larger divisor giving a pair has a partner already checked.

Answer. 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.

Example 2 · Identify the special case 1

Given. The number 1

Find. Classify 1

Rule. A prime has two distinct positive factors.

  1. Only 1 divides 1 exactly.
  2. One factor is fewer than the two required for a prime and the more than two required for a composite.

Answer. 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Example 3 · List the positive multiples of 7

Given. 7 and the first five positive multipliers

Find. List the first five positive multiples

Rule. Multiply by 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5.

  1. Start at 7 × 1 = 7.
  2. Add 7 repeatedly: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35.

Answer. 7, 14, 21, 28 and 35.

Example 4 · Test divisibility by 9

Given. 4,158

Find. Decide whether 9 divides it

Rule. Use the digit sum.

  1. 4 + 1 + 5 + 8 = 18.
  2. 18 is divisible by 9. As a direct check, 4,158 = 9 × 462.

Answer. 4,158 is divisible by 9.

Example 5 · Recognise a composite odd number

Given. 49

Find. Classify 49

Rule. An exact factor between 1 and the number proves it composite.

  1. 49 is odd, so 2 is not a factor.
  2. 49 = 7 × 7, giving the proper factor 7.

Answer. 49 is composite.

Example 6 · Find common factors and HCF

Given. 12 and 18

Find. Find the intersection and its greatest member

Rule. Compare the two complete factor lists.

  1. Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.
  2. Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18.
  3. Keep the shared entries and choose the greatest.

Answer. Common factors 1, 2, 3, 6. HCF 6.

Example 7 · Find the first meeting by listing

Given. 4 and 6

Find. Find their LCM

Rule. Find the first positive common entry.

  1. Multiples of 4 begin 4, 8, 12.
  2. Multiples of 6 begin 6, 12.
  3. Both first meet at 12.

Answer. LCM = 12.

Example 8 · Recognise a co-prime pair

Given. 8 and 15

Find. Find the HCF

Rule. Co-prime numbers have no common prime factor.

  1. 8 = 2³.
  2. 15 = 3 × 5.
  3. No prime appears in both lists.

Answer. HCF = 1. The pair is co-prime.

Example 9 · Calculate from an exact divisibility relationship

Given. 9 and 63

Find. Find HCF and LCM

Rule. When the smaller divides the larger, the pair gives both results.

  1. 63 = 9 × 7, so 9 divides both inputs.
  2. 63 contains both inputs as divisors and is the first positive multiple of 63.

Answer. HCF 9; LCM 63.

Intermediate

Example 10 · Build two trees for 72

Given. 72 = 8 × 9 = 6 × 12

Find. Find and compare the prime leaves

Rule. Every valid prime factorisation agrees up to order.

  1. 8 supplies three 2s; 9 supplies two 3s.
  2. 6 supplies 2 and 3; 12 supplies two 2s and one 3.
  3. Each route gives the same counts.

Answer. 72 = 2³ × 3².

Example 11 · Use repeated prime division

Given. 180

Find. Find the prime factorisation

Rule. Divide by prime factors until reaching 1.

  1. 180 ÷ 2 = 90; 90 ÷ 2 = 45.
  2. 45 ÷ 3 = 15; 15 ÷ 3 = 5; 5 ÷ 5 = 1.
  3. Collect the divisors and verify their product.

Answer. 180 = 2² × 3² × 5.

Example 12 · List factors from prime subparts

Given. 36 = 2² × 3²

Find. Generate every factor

Rule. For each prime choose any allowed exponent.

  1. With no factor 3: 1, 2, 4.
  2. With one factor 3: 3, 6, 12.
  3. With two factors 3: 9, 18, 36. Sort the distinct results.

Answer. 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.

Example 13 · Use minimum and maximum exponents

Given. 24 and 36

Find. Find HCF and LCM

Rule. Take minima for HCF and maxima for LCM.

  1. 24 = 2³ × 3; 36 = 2² × 3².
  2. HCF needs two 2s and one 3. LCM needs three 2s and two 3s.
  3. Check 24 and 36 both divide 72 and are both divisible by 12.

Answer. HCF 12; LCM 72.

Example 14 · Find a three-number HCF

Given. 36, 60 and 84

Find. Find the greatest shared divisor

Rule. Only primes common to all three contribute.

  1. 36 = 2² × 3²; 60 = 2² × 3 × 5; 84 = 2² × 3 × 7.
  2. All have two 2s; only one 3 is shared.
  3. Multiply 4 × 3.

Answer. 12.

Example 15 · Find a three-number LCM

Given. 8, 12 and 18

Find. Find the least positive common multiple

Rule. Include the largest exponent for every prime.

  1. 8 = 2³; 12 = 2² × 3; 18 = 2 × 3².
  2. Keep three 2s and two 3s.
  3. 2³ × 3² = 8 × 9.

Answer. 72.

Example 16 · Use the shared division ladder

Given. 84 and 132

Find. Find both common measures

Rule. Divide out a common factor until the remainders are co-prime.

  1. Divide both by 2 twice, then by 3. The last values are 7 and 11.
  2. HCF = 2 × 2 × 3 = 12.
  3. LCM = 12 × 7 × 11 = 924.

Answer. HCF 12; LCM 924.

Example 17 · Check more than one divisibility test

Given. 5,544

Find. Test 8 and 11 separately

Rule. Use the last three digits for 8 and alternate sums for 11.

  1. 544 = 8 × 68, so 8 divides 5,544.
  2. (5 + 4) − (5 + 4) = 0. A difference of zero satisfies the 11 test.

Answer. Divisible by both 8 and 11.

Example 18 · Cut equal ribbons

Given. 18 cm and 24 cm

Find. Find the longest cut length and number of pieces

Rule. The cut length must divide each ribbon.

  1. The HCF is 6 because 18 = 6 × 3 and 24 = 6 × 4 with 3 and 4 co-prime.
  2. Cut the first ribbon into three pieces and the second into four.

Answer. Seven pieces of 6 cm each.

Example 19 · Synchronise buses

Given. One bus leaves every 9 minutes; the other every 12 minutes. Both leave now.

Find. Find the next simultaneous departure

Rule. Elapsed time must be a positive multiple of both intervals.

  1. 9 = 3² and 12 = 2² × 3.
  2. The maximum powers give 2² × 3² = 36.
  3. The buses complete four and three intervals respectively.

Answer. 36 minutes.

Example 20 · Find two consecutive even inputs’ HCF

Given. 46 and 48

Find. Find HCF and LCM

Rule. A shared divisor divides the difference.

  1. Both are even and their difference is 2, so HCF = 2.
  2. LCM = 46 × 48 ÷ 2 = 23 × 48.

Answer. HCF 2; LCM 1,104.

Example 21 · Find a missing input

Given. One number is 18, HCF is 6 and LCM is 90

Find. Find the second number

Rule. Use the two-input product identity.

  1. The product must be 6 × 90 = 540.
  2. Divide 540 by 18 to find 30.
  3. 18 and 30 have HCF 6 and LCM 90, so the result is consistent.

Answer. 30.

Example 22 · Pack a cuboid with identical cubes

Given. A box measures 18 cm by 24 cm by 30 cm

Find. Find the largest whole-centimetre cube edge and cube count

Rule. The edge divides all three dimensions.

  1. The common prime product is 2 × 3 = 6.
  2. The box fits 3, 4 and 5 cubes along its respective dimensions.
  3. Multiply the three counts.

Example 23 · Find the next meeting of three events

Given. Periods 6, 8 and 10 seconds, with a shared start

Find. Find the first positive meeting

Rule. Use the LCM of all three intervals.

  1. 6 = 2 × 3; 8 = 2³; 10 = 2 × 5.
  2. Choose 2³ × 3 × 5 = 120.
  3. 120 ÷ 6 = 20, 120 ÷ 8 = 15 and 120 ÷ 10 = 12.

Answer. 120 seconds.

Example 24 · Predict the effect of scaling

Given. HCF(8,12) = 4 and LCM(8,12) = 24

Find. Find both measures after tripling both inputs

Rule. Both measures scale by the common multiplier.

  1. The new inputs are 24 and 36.
  2. Multiplying both by 3 adds one copy of 3 to both prime lists.
  3. Thus the minimum and maximum powers of 3 both rise by one.

Answer. HCF 12; LCM 72.

Example 25 · Disprove the three-number product claim

Given. 4, 6 and 12

Find. Compare the two products

Rule. The product identity is specific to two inputs.

  1. HCF is 2 and LCM is 12, so their product is 24.
  2. The product of the inputs is 4 × 6 × 12 = 288.
  3. The two totals differ.

Answer. 24 ≠ 288.

Example 26 · Satisfy divisibility and a remainder

Given. The number is divisible by 4 and 6, and leaves remainder 1 on division by 5

Find. Find the least positive possibility

Rule. Use the required LCM before checking the remainder.

  1. The LCM of 4 and 6 is 12, so check 12, 24 and 36.
  2. Their remainders on division by 5 are 2, 4 and 1.
  3. 36 is the first that works.

Answer. 36.

Example 27 · Build a pair from HCF and LCM

Given. HCF 8 and LCM 120

Find. Give two different positive inputs

Rule. Divide the LCM by the HCF and split the quotient into co-prime factors.

  1. 120 ÷ 8 = 15. Choose co-prime factors 3 and 5.
  2. Scale them by 8 to get 24 and 40.
  3. They share factor 8, and 8 × 3 × 5 = 120.

Answer. One pair is 24 and 40.

Example 28 · Use an efficient common denominator

Given. 1/12 + 5/18

Find. Find the sum

Rule. Use the LCM of the denominators.

  1. The LCM is 36.
  2. Convert to 3/36 + 10/36.
  3. The numerator sum is 13, which shares no prime factor with 36.

Answer. 13/36.

Example 29 · Decode prime multiplicities

Given. A code shows three 2-marks, one 3-mark and one 5-mark

Find. Find the encoded number and test divisibility by 12

Rule. Each mark is one prime factor.

  1. Multiply 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 120.
  2. 12 needs two copies of 2 and one 3.
  3. That subpart is present, so 12 divides 120.

Answer. 120, divisible by 12.

Example 30 · Choose a method despite a misleading word

Given. A machine uses strips of 18 mm and 24 mm to assemble rows of equal total length. Use the fewest pieces possible.

Find. Find the shared length and both counts

Rule. The row length is a common multiple even though the goal mentions fewer pieces.

  1. 18 = 2 × 3² and 24 = 2³ × 3.
  2. The least shared multiple is 2³ × 3² = 72.
  3. 72 ÷ 18 = 4 and 72 ÷ 24 = 3.

Answer. 72 mm; four strips of 18 mm or three strips of 24 mm.

Long answer · Questions 46–50

  1. Find both HCF and LCM of 96 and 144 by prime factorisation and verify the product identity. Advanced

    Show hint

    96 = 2⁵ × 3; 144 = 2⁴ × 3².

    Reveal solution

    HCF = 2⁴ × 3 = 48 and LCM = 2⁵ × 3² = 288. Both 48 × 288 and 96 × 144 equal 13,824.

  2. A 20 m by 28 m surface uses identical whole-metre squares without cuts. Find the largest tile side and tile count. Advanced

    Show hint

    The side divides both dimensions.

    Reveal solution

    HCF is 4 m. There are 20 ÷ 4 = 5 by 28 ÷ 4 = 7 tiles, giving 35.

  3. Show why the HCF of 9r and 9s is 9 if r and s are co-prime. Advanced

    Show hint

    Factor out 9 from both numbers.

    Reveal solution

    The extra factor 9 contributes its prime copies to both lists. Since r and s have no common primes, there is no additional shared multiplier. The HCF is 9.

  4. Use prime factors to explain why the LCM of two inputs cannot exceed their product. Advanced

    Show hint

    The product is divisible by both inputs.

    Reveal solution

    For each prime the LCM keeps the greater exponent, while the product adds both. No greater exponent can exceed their sum, so the LCM divides the product.

  5. Find HCF and LCM of 24, 42 and 60. Explain why not every prime appears in both answers. Advanced

    Show hint

    List the factorisations before choosing exponents.

    Reveal solution

    24 = 2³ × 3; 42 = 2 × 3 × 7; 60 = 2² × 3 × 5. HCF = 2 × 3 = 6. LCM = 2³ × 3 × 5 × 7 = 840. A prime must appear in every input to enter the HCF.

Higher-order thinking · Questions 63–67

  1. Find the least positive multiple of 12 and 18 that leaves remainder 2 when divided by 5. Advanced

    Show hint

    Check successive multiples of 36.

    Reveal solution

    The candidates are 36, 72, … . 36 leaves 1 and 72 leaves 2, so the answer is 72.

  2. Find a pair of composite co-prime numbers whose product is 180. Advanced

    Show hint

    Split prime powers of 180 without sharing a prime.

    Reveal solution

    180 = 2² × 3² × 5. Take 4 and 45. Both are composite and their prime sets are disjoint.

  3. Can two positive integers have HCF 12 and LCM 30? Advanced

    Show hint

    The HCF must divide the LCM.

    Reveal solution

    No. The LCM is a multiple of each input and hence of their HCF. Since 12 does not divide 30, the values are impossible.

  4. What is the smallest positive integer divisible by every number from 1 through 8? Advanced

    Show hint

    Take the largest needed powers of 2, 3, 5 and 7.

    Reveal solution

    The required maximal powers are 2³, 3, 5 and 7. Their product is 840.

  5. Explain why HCF(n,n+2) is 1 for odd n and 2 for even n. Advanced

    Show hint

    Every common divisor must divide 2.

    Reveal solution

    For odd n, 2 cannot divide either odd input, leaving HCF 1. For even n both are divisible by 2 and no greater divisor of 2 is possible.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Confuse factors and multiples

Incorrect. Call 24 a factor of 4.

Correction. Check which value divides the other. 4 divides 24, so 4 is a factor and 24 is a multiple.

Miss one as a factor

Incorrect. Start the list of factors at 2.

Correction. First write 1 × n. Both 1 and n belong in the list for n > 1.

Classify 1 as prime

Incorrect. Say a prime can have just one factor.

Correction. Count distinct positive factors. A prime needs two. The number 1 has one.

Assume every odd number is prime

Incorrect. Classify 49 by parity alone.

Correction. Test odd divisors. 7 × 7 makes 49 composite.

Confuse prime and co-prime

Incorrect. Demand that 8 and 15 each be prime.

Correction. Compare their prime sets. HCF 1 makes the pair co-prime even when both are composite.

Miss partners or duplicate a square root

Incorrect. Stop after checking only divisors below 4 for 24.

Correction. Write both members of each pair and include 4 × 6. In a perfect square, include its square root once.

Lose a copy of a prime factor

Incorrect. Write 72 as 2 × 3².

Correction. Multiply the proposed primes to verify. Three copies of 2 and two of 3 give 72.

Stop at a composite branch

Incorrect. Use 8 × 9 as the prime factorisation of 72.

Correction. Continue splitting 8 and 9 until all displayed leaves are prime.

Use the same digit test for 9 and 11

Incorrect. Apply just the ordinary digit sum to test 11.

Correction. For 9 use the ordinary sum. For 11 subtract the two alternate sums.

Mix factor lists with time lists

Incorrect. Search for 4 and 6 on lists of divisors to solve repeating times.

Correction. Use the multiples of each cycle length and find the first positive meeting.

Select maximum exponents for HCF

Incorrect. Give 72 as HCF(24,36).

Correction. For HCF, select the minimum number of each shared prime. 72 is instead their LCM.

Drop primes occurring in one input only

Incorrect. Omit 5 from LCM(12,20).

Correction. Include maximum powers from all inputs, giving 2² × 3 × 5 = 60.

Apply the product identity directly to three inputs

Incorrect. Use 4 × 6 × 12 as HCF × LCM.

Correction. Compute both measures directly for three inputs. They give 2 × 12 = 24.

Let keywords choose the operation

Incorrect. Use LCM because the problem asks for the fewest floor tiles.

Correction. The tile edge divides each floor side, so find its largest possible value using HCF.

Count a later occurrence as the LCM

Incorrect. Pick 24 as the LCM of 4 and 6.

Correction. 24 works but 12 is the first positive common multiple.

Count any common divisor as HCF

Incorrect. Pick 2 as the HCF of 12 and 18.

Correction. List 1, 2, 3 and 6; choose the greatest value, 6.

What does a factor do?

It divides the given number without a remainder.

Which result is used for the maximum tile size?

HCF, because the tile edge must divide each dimension.

Which result joins two shared-start cycles?

LCM, because the elapsed time must contain whole copies of both cycles.

When can both composite numbers be co-prime?

When they have no common prime factor, as with 8 and 15.

Do you take minimums or maximums for LCM?

Maximum exponents, including primes present in only one input.

How can you check the HCF × LCM identity?

Multiply the HCF and LCM and compare with the product of the two positive integers.
