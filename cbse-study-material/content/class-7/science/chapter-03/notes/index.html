Why does a torch light only when its switch is on? Why can you reverse a filament lamp in a simple circuit, but a bare LED usually works in only one direction? Allow 55–70 minutes for the notes and a first lab challenge.

  • Identify cells, batteries, lamps, LEDs, switches and wires.
  • Build and troubleshoot a complete path in a virtual workbench.

Use classroom power sources

Physical activities use only teacher-approved small cells, holders, and matching components. Keep household sockets and mains-powered appliances out of every activity. Never bridge a cell’s terminals directly. Do not use damaged or leaking cells, or puncture, heat or dismantle them. Use a rated, protected LED module or teacher-selected series resistor. Recycle cells as directed by the school or local collection programme.

3.1 A torchlight

A torch has a source of electrical energy, a switch, a lamp or LED, and conducting contacts. The reflector directs light forwards, and the case protects and supports the parts. Some torches have a removable lamp. Others use a built-in LED module.

Switching on joins the contacts to complete a conducting path from the source, through the lamp and back to the source. A flat cell, an open switch or a loose spring contact can stop it working.

3.2 A simple electrical circuit

3.2.1 Electric cell

A cell supplies electrical energy from chemical reactions inside it. Its two external contacts are the positive (+) and negative (−) terminals. In a familiar cylindrical dry cell the raised cap is usually positive and the flat base negative. Use markings, because shapes vary.

3.2.2 Battery

Two or more cells can be joined to provide a supply. Everyday usage also calls some single-cell devices batteries. In a series holder each intermediate positive terminal meets the next negative terminal. The remaining ends supply the circuit. A reversed cell can oppose the others. Use a holder and matched teacher-approved cells. More cells can exceed a component’s rating.

3.2.3 Electric lamp and LED

An incandescent lamp has a thin filament joined to two supporting wires. One leads to the metal case and one to the isolated metal tip at the base. A suitable current heats the filament until it glows. A broken filament interrupts that path.

An LED is a light-emitting diode with no incandescent filament. A new through-hole LED typically has a longer anode (+ in the forward connection) and a shorter cathode (−). A flat edge often marks the cathode. Check part markings if the leads have been cut. A suitable forward supply and current-limiting protection are required.

3.2.4 Making the lamp glow

Use a matching lamp and cell holder. Connect one lamp terminal to each of the cell’s two terminals. The case and tip are different contacts. Two leads touching the same lamp contact do not establish a path through the filament.

3.2.5–3.2.6 Circuits, current and switches

An electrical circuit is an arrangement of connected electrical components. A closed circuit has an unbroken conducting path. An open circuit has a gap. A working load also needs a suitable source and good contacts. Current is the flow of electric charge. Conventional current through the external circuit is shown from the source’s positive terminal to its negative terminal. In a metal wire, electrons drift the opposite way.

A switch closes or opens part of the path. A switch can be anywhere in a simple single loop. Its ON position connects contacts, and its OFF position separates them. A safety-pin switch is a classroom demonstration on an insulating base, assembled by or with a teacher using only a low-voltage kit.

Troubleshooting order

  1. Open the switch and stop if any component feels warm or looks damaged.
  2. Check whether there is a complete path using two distinct contacts on each component.
  3. Check holder orientation, insulated or loose connections, and source and load ratings.
  4. Check LED polarity. Substitute one known working component at a time with teacher supervision.

3.3 Circuit diagrams

A circuit diagram uses standard symbols and straight lines. What matters is which terminals are connected. The cell’s long line is positive and its short line is negative. An LED’s bar marks the cathode, and two outward arrows indicate emitted light. A filament lamp is shown as a circle with a cross.

SymbolWhat to identify
CellLong positive line and short negative line
BatteryRepeated unequal pairs of lines
LampCircle with cross
LEDDiode with outward light arrows
Switch ONContacts joined
Switch OFFArm leaves a gap
WireContinuous connecting line

Mark a junction when wires actually meet. The lab grid only joins wire selections at matching terminal or endpoint coordinates. A graphical crossing or a line passing a terminal does not create a connection on its own.

3.4 Conductors and insulators

Conductors let electric charge flow easily. Metals such as copper and aluminium typically conduct. Dry plastic, rubber, paper, wood, glass and wax normally behave as insulators in this low-voltage tester. Moisture, coatings and poor contact affect results. A lamp staying dark means no visibly detectable conduction in the test.

SampleExpected observationInference with valid controls
Bare metal key or foilLamp glowsConducts in this test
Dry plastic ruler or eraserLamp stays offInsulates in this test
Dry glass bangle, paper, wood or waxLamp stays offInsulates in this test
Painted metal objectMay remain offCoating or contact may hide the underlying conductor

Use a known conductor to check the tester before testing unknown solids. Ensure clips are separated except through the sample. Copper is common in wires because it conducts well and is practical to use. Insulation protects people and prevents unintended contacts. The human body can conduct electricity. Keep hands dry and tell an adult about damaged wires or plugs.

30 worked questions and examples

NCERT questions 1–11

NCERT Q1 · Identify the incorrect statement about the switch

Worked answer. Option (i). A switch completes or interrupts the path. The cell or battery supplies electrical energy.

NCERT Q2 · Choose a sample for the gap in Fig. 3.16 that leaves the lamp off

Worked answer. Use a dry plastic strip. It does not allow enough current through this low-voltage tester. First check that bare metal makes the lamp glow.

NCERT Q3 · Predict what happens when one lamp filament breaks in Fig. 3.17

Worked answer. Both lamps go out. The figure shows one loop, and the break in either filament opens that loop.

NCERT Q4 · Wire insulation is still covering the contacts

Worked answer. The lamp stays off if the coating prevents metal-to-metal contact. Use teacher-prepared leads with exposed contacts at their intended ends.

NCERT Q5 · Draw a symbolic torch loop

Worked answer. Connect the free battery terminals through a lamp and a switch in one loop. Use the unequal plates for the battery, circle with cross for the filament lamp and the contact-arm symbol for the switch. See the closed-loop figure above.

NCERT Q6 · Determine which lamps light in Fig. 3.18

Worked answer. Both switches and both lamps are in the same path. In (i) and (ii) neither lights because one switch is open. In (iii) both light. In (iv) neither lights.

NCERT Q7 · Diagnose the fault in Fig. 3.19

Worked answer. Possible faults include a discharged cell, poor holder contact, damaged wire, open switch contacts despite the arm position or a broken filament. With a teacher, inspect connections with power off and change one part at a time for a known working match.

NCERT Q8 · Compare the four connections in Fig. 3.20

Worked answer. Only (c) stays off when the switches close. The two filament lamp orientations in (a) and (b) can both work. In (c) the battery polarity is reverse for the LED. In (d) it is forward. Use a protected LED in a real circuit.

NCERT Q9 · Identify the terminals when the marks cannot be read

Worked answer. Use a teacher-supervised meter rated for the classroom cell. A positive reading means the red meter lead is on the positive terminal. A tested, compatible protected LED can also distinguish orientation if the source works and supplies enough voltage.

NCERT Q10 · Test which of the six labelled cells work

Worked answer. Use a known working compatible lamp, holder, switch and leads. First check the setup with a known good cell. Test A–F separately using the same setup and record the result. A dark trial means that cell does not run the test lamp. Check orientation and contact before deciding it is exhausted.

NCERT Q11 · Correct the two-cell arrangement in Fig. 3.21

Worked answer. The joined ends in the figure are both negative, so the cell contributions oppose between the free positive ends. The LED does not get the intended supply. In the corrected series drawing connect one positive to the other negative. Use the free positive for the LED anode and the free negative for the cathode. Include suitable protection in a physical setup.

Additional examples

Example 1 · Why can a torch still fail with fresh cells?

Worked answer. Trace both cell contacts and the switch. If a spring contact is loose, the external loop stays open even with fresh cells.

Example 2 · What does a reflector do?

Worked answer. It directs light emerging from the lamp. Light can still be emitted if a working circuit has no reflector.

Example 3 · What is the cell’s role?

Worked answer. Chemical reactions maintain the terminal difference that supplies energy to the external circuit.

Example 4 · How can you identify poles on a cylindrical cell?

Worked answer. Read its plus and minus markings. On many types the positive cap is raised.

Example 5 · Why use a matched cell holder?

Worked answer. The holder keeps the cells against appropriate contacts and makes intended connections more reliable.

Example 6 · Why does a reversed series cell matter?

Worked answer. It opposes the other cell’s contribution. Equal opposed cells ideally have no net supply across the free ends.

Example 7 · Identify a bulb’s two contacts

Worked answer. One terminal is the metal case. The other is the isolated central tip. The bulb filament joins them internally.

Example 8 · How does a filament produce visible light?

Worked answer. A suitable current heats the thin wire. The hot filament produces light.

Example 9 · Why does an LED have outward arrows in its symbol?

Worked answer. They represent emitted light. They do not mark the direction of current.

Example 10 · How can a cut-lead LED be identified?

Worked answer. Check its body marking and part specification or use a teacher-approved tester. Lead length is no longer reliable.

Example 11 · What does a switch gap do?

Worked answer. It removes the conducting path between the contacts and stops current in that branch.

Example 12 · Why is a switch in a different place still effective?

Worked answer. Each point in a single loop is needed. Opening either segment breaks that loop.

Example 13 · Explain a textbook battery symbol

Worked answer. Repeated unequal plate pairs represent the cells. Each long plate is the positive side of its pair.

Example 14 · Why does the path shape not change the diagram’s meaning?

Worked answer. The essential information is which terminals are connected. Equivalent layouts preserve these connections.

Example 15 · Predict a result for a clean aluminium strip in the tester

Worked answer. Aluminium conducts. With clean contacts and a working circuit, the indicator lamp lights.

Example 16 · Predict a result for a dry glass strip

Worked answer. A tiny current may be undetectable. The lamp stays off and glass is treated as an insulator here.

Example 17 · Why set up a known-metal control?

Worked answer. If the control lamp does not light, you cannot attribute later dark results to the material under test.

Example 18 · Explain the coating around copper wire

Worked answer. The copper core conducts. Plastic insulation reduces accidental contact with other conductors and people.

Example 19 · What happens if a safe model flags a source bypass?

Worked answer. Stop the virtual trial and remove unintended wiring. With real cells a bypass can lead to overheating.

Everyday devices

Torch cells and their springs make a portable supply. A remote uses compact cells, and some watches or hearing aids use button cells. Phones and laptops use rechargeable packs. Observe only sealed devices or teacher-approved kits. Do not dismantle equipment.

Optional enrichment

Two devices in a single loop share the same path. A break stops both. Different branches can have their own paths. The chapter’s Dive Deeper box distinguishes battery direct current from alternating current at the wall socket. The lab represents battery circuits only. A short circuit bypasses the intended load and can cause dangerous heating. The workbench stops virtual current animation when it detects one.
