The world is full of questions. Science helps us find answers!

Why do shadows move? What could a microscope reveal in a leaf? Why do some objects float? You can start investigating by observing one thing closely and asking a question you can explore.

What you’ll learn

  • Recognise curiosity, observation and evidence in everyday science.
  • See how the fields in Grade 7 are connected.
  • Ask better questions and explain why an answer may change.

Plan your study

Allow 45–60 minutes for the chapter and another session for the labs. Recall Grade 6 work on observing materials, measuring and exploring living things.

NCERT Chapter 1 · Science as an evolving way of thinking

Notice, question and explore

The textbook’s butterfly and paper-plane page decorations invite careful observation. The chapter uses flight to connect observations of nature with experimentation and design. A paper-plane question can lead to another: if a new fold flies farther once, will it still do so over several trials?

Science, society and responsibility

Physics, chemistry, biology and Earth science ask different kinds of questions, and they often overlap. Heat changes water, and water affects farms, plants and communities. Questions about safe water or conserving energy involve evidence, decisions and the people who use those resources.

From materials to changing things

The textbook begins its preview with sour substances and a turmeric stain changing colour, then moves to batteries, simple circuits, properties of metals and non-metals, and changes such as melting or ripening. Heat connects ice melting, evaporation, rainfall and water that seeps into the ground.

From living systems to time, light and space

The chapter previews body changes during adolescence, life processes in animals and plants, and the measurement of time and motion. People used changing shadow positions to help tell time. Shadows also connect to eclipses: in a solar eclipse the Moon is between the Sun and Earth, and in a lunar eclipse the Moon passes through Earth’s shadow. Earth’s rotation causes the usual cycle of day and night.

Enrichment · From observation to evidence

Observation

A record of what you noticed or measured. ‘The ground is wet’ is an observation. ‘The shadow measures 15 cm’ is quantitative. ‘The shadow looks indistinct’ is qualitative.

Inference and alternatives

An interpretation of a record. ‘It may have rained’ is one inference from wet ground. A sprinkler is another. Look for clues, such as wet areas outside the sprinkler’s reach.

Prediction and hypothesis

A prediction states what might happen under stated conditions. A hypothesis proposes a testable explanation. ‘Airflow carries moist air away’ can explain a prediction about drying time.

Conclusion

A statement based on the evidence. Include the measured result, the conditions it describes, and an uncertainty or further question when needed.

An investigation cycle

Observe → ask → propose an explanation → predict → plan → investigate safely → record → analyse → conclude → ask again. You may go back to improve a measurement, collect more data or test another explanation.

What makes a comparison fair?

Change one relevant factor at a time. Keep the other relevant conditions consistent. Use the same measuring rule and unit, repeat the test and record all results. If both the lamp angle and the block height change, a shadow length difference cannot be pinned on either change alone.

Claim, evidence and reasoning

A claim answers the question. Evidence consists of observations or measurements. Reasoning connects the observations to the claim. If equal sugar portions disappear in 78, 82 and 80 seconds in cool water and 47, 44 and 46 seconds in lukewarm water, those sample times support faster dissolving in the warmer condition tested. They do not measure the maximum amount that dissolves. These times are illustrative.

Scientific attitude

Be curious, creative, patient and open to a different explanation. Compare results with classmates using the same method, report an unexpected result honestly, respect living things and repeat carefully when results differ.

Dissolving

Aim. Compare how long a fixed amount of sugar takes to dissolve in cool and lukewarm water.

Materials. Two identical clear cups, equal volumes of water, equal sugar portions, timer and spoon.

Question. Does warmer water change the time needed for an equal amount of sugar to dissolve?

Prediction. The sugar may dissolve faster in lukewarm water.

Procedure

  1. Set out equal volumes of cool and lukewarm water.
  2. Add equal sugar portions. Use the same stirring method.
  3. Record seconds until visible grains disappear. Repeat with fresh equal portions.
TrialCool water (s)Lukewarm water (s)
1RecordRecord
2RecordRecord
3RecordRecord

Expected observations. Under these conditions the warm sample will often take less time.

Explanation. Particles move more rapidly in warmer water. This activity compares dissolving time, not the maximum amount that can dissolve.

Conclusion to make after testing. Compare the actual observations, state the conditions tested, and say whether they support your prediction.

Precautions. Use lukewarm water prepared by an adult. Keep the cups stable and do not taste test mixtures.

Seed germination

Aim. Compare germination on damp and dry paper.

Materials. Two dishes, paper towels, equal groups of untreated dry beans, water and a notebook.

Question. How does access to water affect the number of seeds that show a root?

Prediction. More seeds may germinate on damp paper.

Procedure

  1. Place five beans on each paper towel; dampen one towel.
  2. Keep both dishes in the same place. Keep the damp towel moist but not flooded.
  3. On each day count seeds with a visible root. Record the cumulative counts.

Expected observations. More roots may emerge in the damp condition over the same days.

Explanation. Seeds need suitable conditions, including access to water and oxygen. Germination is root emergence here; later shoot height is a separate measurement.

Conclusion to make after testing. Compare the actual observations, state the conditions tested, and say whether they support your prediction.

Precautions. Use ordinary untreated beans. Do not eat them after the activity. Wash hands and discard spoiled materials without opening or investigating them.

Shadows

Aim. Compare how far the edge of a shadow falls from a fixed object as a lamp’s angle changes.

Materials. Battery torch, opaque upright block, paper, ruler and marked positions.

Question. How does the angle of the light change the shadow length?

Prediction. A lower lamp angle may give a longer shadow.

Procedure

  1. Stand a block upright on paper. Mark distances from the base.
  2. Place the torch at two angles, keeping the torch at the same distance from the block.
  3. Mark and measure each shadow from the block base. Repeat at the same positions.
TrialHigher angle (cm)Lower angle (cm)
1RecordRecord
2RecordRecord
3RecordRecord

Expected observations. A lower angle generally produces a longer shadow on the flat surface.

Explanation. Light travels approximately in straight lines in air. An opaque object blocks some rays. A simple beam model gives a longer shadow when the incoming light is shallower.

Conclusion to make after testing. Compare the actual observations, state the conditions tested, and say whether they support your prediction.

Precautions. Use a battery torch, away from eyes. For outdoor observations, mark a shadow on the ground and never look at the Sun.

Try yourself. Can a shadow change direction even when its length is almost unchanged?

Evaporation

Aim. Compare water loss under still air and gentle airflow.

Materials. Identical shallow dishes, water, measuring cup or balance, and a paper fan or supervised battery fan.

Question. Does airflow change how much water evaporates in the same time?

Prediction. The dish with airflow may lose more water.

Procedure

  1. Add equal water to identical dishes under the same room conditions.
  2. Expose one dish to gentle airflow without splashing; leave the other still.
  3. Measure the remaining water after the same interval. Repeat or compare another day.
TrialStill air loss (mL)Airflow loss (mL)
1RecordRecord
2RecordRecord
3RecordRecord

Expected observations. More water may be lost from the dish with airflow.

Explanation. Evaporation happens at the surface. Airflow can replace humid air above the water, helping more water leave the surface.

Conclusion to make after testing. Compare the actual observations, state the conditions tested, and say whether they support your prediction.

Precautions. Keep water away from mains electricity. Wipe spills. Avoid heaters and flames.

Floating and sinking

Aim. Test how changing the shape of equal foil portions affects the load they can support.

Materials. Shallow basin, two equal foil sheets, identical light counters and towel.

Question. Does a boat-shaped foil piece hold a larger load than the same foil pressed into a compact shape?

Prediction. The boat shape may hold more counters before sinking.

Procedure

  1. Shape one equal foil sheet into a dry boat and the other into a compact shape.
  2. Place each in the same basin. Add identical counters one at a time if the foil floats.
  3. Record the largest supported load before water enters or the foil sinks. Repeat with fresh copies.
TrialCompact shape (counters)Boat shape (counters)
1RecordRecord
2RecordRecord
3RecordRecord

Expected observations. A well-made boat shape may support more counters.

Explanation. A hollow boat can displace more water before water overtops it. Floating depends on upward force and total weight, not size alone.

Conclusion to make after testing. Compare the actual observations, state the conditions tested, and say whether they support your prediction.

Precautions. Use a shallow basin, keep counters away from young children and wipe spills.

Paper-plane flight

Aim. Test whether a small change to one fold changes flight distance.

Materials. Two same-size sheets of paper, tape marks, measuring tape and a clear indoor area.

Question. Does bending the same wing fold slightly upwards change the distance travelled?

Prediction. The fold could change distance in either direction.

Procedure

  1. Make two similar paper planes; adjust only the selected wing fold on one.
  2. Launch both from the same mark and direction, alternating the order.
  3. Record distance to first contact with the floor. Look at all trials, including short flights.
TrialOriginal fold (cm)Adjusted fold (cm)

2RecordRecord
3RecordRecord

Expected observations. The distances will vary; the adjusted version may not fly farther in every trial.

Explanation. A fold changes how air flows around the plane, while the throw and small differences between planes also matter. Mixed evidence calls for more trials.

Conclusion to make after testing. Compare the actual observations, state the conditions tested, and say whether they support your prediction.

Precautions. Throw only in a clear space, away from faces, people, pets and windows.

Magnet sorting

Aim. Compare the response of a few objects to a small classroom magnet.

Materials. Classroom magnet, clean steel paper clip, aluminium foil, wooden craft stick and tray.

Question. Are all the objects in this sample attracted to the magnet?

Prediction. The steel clip will be attracted; the wood and aluminium will not show noticeable attraction.

Procedure

  1. List each object before testing it.
  2. Bring the same magnet near one object at a time without touching.
  3. Record visible motion or no visible motion and repeat.
ObjectFirst observationRepeat observation
Steel clipRecordRecord
Aluminium foilRecordRecord
Wooden stickRecordRecord

Expected observations. The steel clip will usually respond clearly, while the other sampled materials will not.

Explanation. Some materials, including many steels, are strongly attracted to ordinary magnets. This test does not support a claim about every metal.

Conclusion to make after testing. Compare the actual observations, state the conditions tested, and say whether they support your prediction.

Precautions. Use an ordinary classroom magnet, away from young children and electronic devices. Do not use small high-strength magnets.

Enrichment · Discovery timeline and instruments

Better instruments can make evidence more detailed. Repeated observations, improved methods and collaboration help researchers refine earlier explanations.

1610 · Jupiter’s moons

Question. Do all objects in the sky orbit Earth?

Earlier understanding. An Earth-centred view was widely accepted.

Evidence. Successive telescope observations showed four small objects changing position around Jupiter.

Instrument. Improved optical telescope and observation records

What changed. Some bodies orbit another planet. The observation did not by itself establish every detail of the Solar System.

Modern link. Astronomers still compare positions over time to identify orbits.

NASA’s account of Galileo’s observing records

1665 · Structures in cork

Question. What becomes visible when thin cork is magnified?

Earlier understanding. Small structures were difficult to examine by eye.

Evidence. Robert Hooke published observations and illustrations of pocket-like structures in cork in Micrographia.

Instrument. Microscope with illumination

What changed. Microscopy opened a way to investigate structures too small to see unaided. These cork observations were not the entire modern cell theory.

Modern link. Microscopes help scientists compare the structures of specimens.

Royal Society archive of Hooke’s cork illustration

1924 · A galaxy beyond the Milky Way

Question. Was the Andromeda object inside our galaxy?

Earlier understanding. Astronomers debated what spiral nebulae were.

Evidence. Distance estimates from variable stars showed that Andromeda lay far beyond the Milky Way.

Instrument. Large ground telescope and observations of changing brightness

What changed. The Milky Way is one of many galaxies. The method built on earlier work on variable stars.

Modern link. Astronomers use several independent distance methods to compare findings.

NASA’s account of the evidence for galaxies

1990 · An observatory in orbit

Question. How can telescopes reduce the blurring caused by Earth’s atmosphere?

Earlier understanding. Ground observatories were limited in some measurements by the atmosphere.

Evidence. The Hubble Space Telescope was launched in 1990. Its images and later servicing illustrate how improved instruments can improve measurements.

Instrument. Space telescope, detectors and later servicing

What changed. Space and ground observatories provide different kinds of evidence. Instrument design and checking remain essential.

Modern link. Astronomers compare images and other measurements across wavelengths and instruments.

NASA’s Hubble mission timeline

Enrichment · Science, technology and the environment

Science develops explanations using evidence. Technology uses knowledge and designs to meet practical needs. For example, light sensors help researchers monitor plant conditions, while results from that research can help improve greenhouse designs.

A school sustainability question

Compare water used by two classroom routines using the same task and measuring container. Record litres used rather than relying on impressions. Consider energy, waste and biodiversity as well: repeat before changing school routines, reuse useful materials and avoid disturbing plants or animals during observations.

Key terms and investigation answer format

TermMeaning
CuriosityDesire to learn or understand something.
ObservationInformation obtained by examining or measuring.
InferenceAn interpretation of the available information.
PredictionAn expectation about what may happen under stated conditions.
HypothesisA proposed explanation that can be investigated.
ExperimentA planned investigation used to examine a question.
EvidenceInformation that helps evaluate an explanation.
ConclusionA judgement based on the evidence and its limitations.
Scientific reasoningUsing evidence and logic to evaluate explanations.
TechnologyPractical application of knowledge, methods and tools.
  1. State the question, aim and a reasoned prediction.
  2. List apparatus, variable changed, conditions kept the same, procedure and precautions.
  3. Record observations in a table with units, repeats and any unexpected readings.
  4. Compare the evidence, then give a conclusion and a further question or limitation.

Common mistakes

Observation vs inference
Misconception. A wet field proves it rained.
Correction. Record the wet ground first, then compare rain, watering and other possible explanations.
Fair tests
Misconception. Change several factors and credit one.
Correction. Keep other relevant conditions consistent, or record why a conclusion is limited.
Predictions
Misconception. Discard results that disagree with your prediction.
Correction. Keep the readings and reconsider the procedure or explanation.
Reliability
Misconception. A single reading settles the question.
Correction. Repeat the comparison and inspect the spread.
Simulation
Misconception. Model numbers are actual lab readings.
Correction. Label model and illustrative data, and record real measurements separately.
Evolving science
Misconception. A revised explanation means evidence is useless.
Correction. New evidence can improve a previously useful explanation or reveal where it needs refinement.

30 worked scientific reasoning examples

Foundations

1 · A wet path

Question. What can you say after seeing wet patches outside?

  1. Record what you see.
  2. List different ways it could have become wet.

Answer. The path is wet. Rain and watering are possible explanations. The observation alone cannot distinguish them.

2 · A measurable shadow

Question. Record a quantitative observation for one shadow.

  1. Choose a length and a unit.
  2. Record the time and measurement point.

Answer. For example: at 10:00, the shadow was 18 cm long from the mark at its base.

3 · Change a broad question

Question. Turn ‘Which plane is best?’ into a test.

  1. Choose a measurable feature.
  2. Use a common launch point and repeat.

Answer. Which of the two designs flies farther, from the same mark under the same conditions?

4 · Classify a sentence

Question. Is ‘the spoon handle warmed’ an observation or a prediction?

  1. The word warmed describes something reported after a check.
  2. A prediction describes what is expected before checking.

Answer. It is an observation if the warming was actually observed or measured.

5 · Choose a thermometer

Question. What tool measures the starting condition of warm and cool samples?

  1. The changed condition is temperature.
  2. Match the tool to the quantity and unit.

Answer. Use a thermometer and record the starting temperatures in °C.

6 · Keep a number and unit

Question. What is missing from ‘the shadow was 28’?

  1. Identify the quantity.
  2. Ask what the ruler reports.

Answer. Add a unit such as cm and note when the reading was made.

7 · Notice a classmate’s comparison

Question. Which is an inference: ‘four roots appeared’ or ‘water helped the seeds germinate’?

  1. One statement records a count.
  2. The other explains why the count differed.

Answer. Water helped the seeds germinate is an inference that needs a comparison.

8 · Think of a new question

Question. A paper plane flew 2 m. What new question does that suggest?

  1. Use an observation as a starting point.
  2. Change one feature while keeping the launch consistent.

Answer. Does bending one wing fold change the distance travelled?

9 · A different question for 42

Question. Give an evidence-based question that could have answer 42.

  1. Choose an observable quantity.
  2. Specify the group and period.

Answer. How many leaves in our sample measured more than 4 cm? 42 would be the count if our records supported it.

Applying the method

10 · Drying cloth

Question. How could you isolate airflow?

  1. Use equal cloths with similar initial moisture.
  2. Change airflow only and compare the same drying endpoint.

Answer. Measure and repeat. A comparison of different cloths in different temperatures would mix effects.

11 · Dissolving sample times

Question. Cool water gives 78, 82, 80 s; warm water gives 47, 44, 46 s. What is supported?

  1. Compare every pair of times.
  2. Check that the test changed temperature only.

Answer. These illustrative trials support faster dissolving in the warm condition. They do not compare how much can dissolve.

12 · One outlying reading

Question. A third flight is much shorter than the other two. What next?

  1. Keep the reading and write down unusual launch conditions.
  2. Repeat consistently and compare all trials.

Answer. Do not discard it silently. A new trial or a noted reason helps interpret the difference.

13 · A cool surface

Question. Does water on a cold cup prove a leak?

  1. Check an identical room-temperature cup under the same conditions.
  2. Look where droplets form.

Answer. Droplets could come from water vapour condensing on the cold surface. Leaks require separate evidence.

14 · A cleaner measurement

Question. Why measure water in a cylinder at eye level?

  1. Compare the liquid surface to the scale.
  2. An angled view shifts the apparent alignment.

Answer. Eye-level reading improves consistency and reduces reading error.

15 · One day of seed counts

Question. Four damp seeds and no dry seeds have roots. Make a cautious claim.

  1. Name the target event as visible root emergence.
  2. Limit it to this sample and period.

Answer. More seeds germinated on damp paper in these conditions. This does not guarantee every seed will germinate.

16 · A shorter shadow

Question. At a higher torch angle the shadow was 4 cm, versus 10 cm at a lower angle. Explain.

  1. Light travels approximately straight.
  2. A shallow path extends farther behind the block.

Answer. The lower angle produced the longer shadow on this surface.

17 · The same foil

Question. A boat holds more counters than a compact foil shape. Why?

  1. Both shapes use equal foil.
  2. The boat can displace more water before water enters.

Answer. Shape affects the load supported. The result does not show that all metals float.

18 · The darker side of Earth

Question. Why can day and night occur at the same time?

  1. The Sun illuminates the side facing it.
  2. Earth rotates, carrying places into or out of that light.

Answer. Places on different sides of Earth can have daylight and darkness at the same time.

19 · A lunar eclipse

Question. Where is Earth during a lunar eclipse?

  1. Identify what casts the shadow.
  2. Find what moves into the shadow.

Answer. Earth is between the Sun and Moon. The Moon passes through Earth’s shadow.

20 · What magnification means

Question. Does a bigger microscope image always show more detail?

  1. Distinguish size from resolving two nearby details.
  2. A blur can get larger without becoming clearer.

Answer. No. Useful detail depends on the instrument’s resolution too.

21 · A model rainfall reading

Question. Can you report simulated rainfall as a local measurement?

  1. Check the source of each value.
  2. Record the conditions of a genuine measurement.

Answer. Use a simulation label. Replace the value with your own record if you conducted a local measurement.

Evidence and evaluation

22 · Reading a mixed trial

Question. Two adjusted paper planes fly farther; a third flies less far. State a conclusion.

  1. Use all results.
  2. Consider whether launch variation could contribute.

Answer. The change is promising here, but the trials are mixed. Repeat before claiming a reliable improvement.

23 · Decide between rain and sprinklers

Question. Wet ground is found only in narrow strips beside sprinklers. How does the evidence affect the inference?

  1. The distribution matches where sprinkler water reaches.
  2. Check when watering was scheduled.

Answer. Watering becomes a more plausible explanation. The observation still does not prove what happened before the record.

24 · Compare group observations

Question. Two groups found different times for sugar to dissolve. What should they compare?

  1. Check temperatures, sugar portions, stirring and end rules.
  2. Agree a method and repeat.

Answer. Different methods can produce different results. Comparable repeated trials clarify the disagreement.

25 · The discovery of Jupiter’s moons

Question. Why were observations on different nights important?

  1. Compare the small objects’ positions relative to Jupiter.
  2. A pattern of changes can distinguish them from distant stars.

Answer. Successive positions supported the inference that these bodies orbit Jupiter.

26 · From cork to broader cell studies

Question. What can a cork drawing tell us about living cells?

  1. It records visible structures in a particular sample.
  2. Other specimens and tests are needed for broader claims.

Answer. It shows why microscopes are useful, while one cork sample cannot describe every living cell.

27 · Define measure and follow-up

Question. How would you compare two school handwashing routines for water use?

  1. Observe the same task and measure water in litres.
  2. Repeat and keep hygiene conditions suitable.

Answer. Compare water used for the same task. Also ask whether either routine affects hygiene or time.

28 · Correct an unsafe plan

Question. A learner proposes looking at the Sun to follow a shadow. Design a safer observation.

  1. Use a fixed object and record its ground shadow.
  2. Measure on a flat surface from the marked base at set times.

Answer. Observe the projected shadow only. Never look directly at the Sun.

29 · Explain a telescope limit

Question. Why do telescope illustrations change from Moon details to nearby bright points around Jupiter?

  1. Different objects are at different distances and have different apparent sizes.
  2. The instrument has finite resolution.

Answer. Different details can be distinguished. Images and positions should be recorded with the observing conditions.

30 · Carry an idea between fields

Question. How could studying heat also help water conservation?

  1. Evaporation depends on conditions such as surface exposure and air movement.
  2. Compare a covered and uncovered container with equal starting amounts.

Answer. Measurements can guide a practical way to reduce water loss in a real setting.

Long answer · Questions 46–50

  1. Plan a fair comparison of sugar dissolving in cool and lukewarm water. Intermediate

    Show hint

    Change only the temperature and give a safety rule.

    Reveal solution

    Use equal sugar portions, water volumes, cups and stirring. An adult supplies lukewarm water. Repeat and compare time in seconds to visible grain disappearance; do not taste the mixtures.

  2. Plan an investigation of the same block’s shadow at two light angles. Intermediate

    Reveal solution

    Show hint

    Reveal solution

    Compare litres used for the same task under two workable routines. Repeat under consistent conditions and keep hygiene standards. Draw a limited conclusion and check it in more classrooms.

Case study · Questions 61–62

  1. Classroom case: times in cool water are 70, 74, 72 s; lukewarm times are 50, 49, 51 s. Identify a pattern and a limitation. Advanced

    Show hint

    Check all three pairs and keep scope.

    Reveal solution

    Every lukewarm trial finished faster; the means are 72 s and 50 s. These sample times do not establish how much sugar will dissolve or what happens at all temperatures.

  2. Playground case: a pole’s shadow measured 60, 38 and 52 cm in morning, midday and afternoon. What can you infer and how would you verify it? Advanced

    Show hint

    Separate the minimum measured value from all possible times.

    Reveal solution

    Midday had the shortest of these three readings, consistent with a higher light angle. Take extra readings across the day and compare another day without looking at the Sun.

HOTS · Questions 63–67

  1. Why does a failed prediction not necessarily mean a poor experiment? Advanced

    Show hint

    Separate the test quality from its outcome.

    Reveal solution

    A fair, careful test can give evidence against the prediction. That helps revise the explanation or plan a better study.

  2. How would you tell a leaking cup from condensation? Advanced

    Show hint

    Look separately at water inside the cup and water outside it.

    Reveal solution

    Dry the cup and compare a cold and room-temperature cup of the same type; check water levels and droplet location. Outside droplets limited to the cold surface support condensation.

  3. A paper boat supports twice the counters of one foil boat. Can you decide the effect of material? Advanced

    Show hint

    Check whether shape and amount are fixed.

    Reveal solution

    Not yet. The designs may differ in shape, seams or amount. Compare similar shapes and load placement across the materials.

  4. Why can total water used be misleading when classes have different numbers of students? Advanced

    Show hint

    Compare equal reference units.

    Reveal solution

    A larger class may use more water in total even with less per learner. Compare litres per learner for the same task.

  5. How could another group check what you concluded? Advanced

    Show hint

    What must they know about the test?

    Reveal solution

    Share the question, conditions, process, units and all observations so they can repeat the investigation and compare results.
