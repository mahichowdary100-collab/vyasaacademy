Become a materials scientist

Why does a bell ring while wood makes a dull sound? Why can copper become a wire, and why does only one nail rust in a controlled test? Start with appearance, then collect evidence from several tests. Allow about 55–70 minutes for the notes and a first lab challenge.

Try the opening challenge.

The opening story visits an ironsmith in Rajasthan. Iron provides strength for tongs, spades and other tools, while a wooden handle can provide grip and reduce heat transfer. Heating the iron makes it easier for a professional to reshape. The orange glow comes from the hot metal. A furnace is professional equipment.

In the virtual and physical lab

Use prepared samples with adult supervision and suitable eye protection. Never strike brittle samples yourself, make dust, touch hot metal, or use a wall socket. Observe the magnesium and sulfur comparisons virtually. Sodium, potassium, white phosphorus, mercury and bromine are discussion examples only.

4.1 Properties of materials

Copper, aluminium and iron are metallic elements. Sulfur and carbon are non-metallic elements. Graphite is a form of carbon. Wood, plastic, rubber, paper and glass can be compared with elements in material tests. These everyday materials are not themselves non-metallic elements. Coal is a complex material, not pure carbon.

4.1.1 Lustre, hardness and malleability

Lustre is the way a surface reflects light. Clean metals usually have metallic lustre. A coating, tarnish or corrosion can dull the same metal. Hardness describes resistance to scratching or indentation. It differs from strength and brittleness. Most familiar solid metals are relatively hard. Sodium and potassium are soft metals, and mercury is liquid at room temperature.

Malleability is the ability to deform into a sheet under compression. Aluminium foil and thin gold or silver sheets show this property. A lump of sulfur or coal tends to fracture instead of spreading. Wood does not make a metal-like sheet and may split depending on its grain.

4.1.2 Ductility

A ductile material can be drawn into a wire. Copper and aluminium wires, fine gold wire, wire mesh and the steel cables in cranes illustrate this property. A jeweller may use both malleability for sheets and ductility for wires. Most non-metal solids do not form wires in this way.

4.1.3 Sonority

Sonority means producing a ringing sound when struck. A metal coin, bell or ghungroo can ring. Wood and coal often give a more muted sound in comparable demonstrations. Shape, mounting, size and strike position affect ringing. A sound by itself does not identify an element.

4.1.4 Conduction of heat

Conduction transfers thermal energy from a warmer region to a cooler region. A metal spoon warms farther along its handle faster than a similar wooden spoon. A metal pan can transfer heat to food, while a suitable insulating handle helps people hold it. Use warm water only under supervision. Use temperature strips or teacher readings, rather than touching any heated part.

4.1.5 Conduction of electricity

Use a checked low-voltage tester from Chapter 3. A bare metal sample can complete the gap and light the lamp. Wood, rubber and many plastics typically give no visible glow. Graphite can conduct despite being a form of a non-metal, and the result depends on contact and resistance.

4.2 Effect of air and water on iron

Rust is the reddish-brown deposit that develops on iron or ordinary steel in moist air. Rusting requires water and oxygen. Rust is a mixture of hydrated iron oxides and related compounds. It can weaken metal over time.

BottleWater availableOxygen availableExpected textbook observation
A · sealed, dry airNoYesNo visible rust
B · cooled boiled water and oilYesGreatly reducedNo or little rust if exclusion works
C · partly immersed, openYesYesVisible rust after the observation period

Use similar clean iron nails and keep location and observation time the same. The textbook observes over 8–10 days. These model outcomes are qualitative. In a real jar, leaks or remaining dissolved oxygen can affect the result.

Prevention and corrosion

Paint, grease and oil can block contact with moisture and oxygen while their barrier stays intact. Galvanisation coats iron or steel with zinc. The zinc acts as a barrier and may continue to protect small exposed areas because it corrodes preferentially. A copper surface can develop a green patina, silver can develop a dark tarnish, and aluminium can form a thin protective oxide layer. These changes are not iron rust.

The Iron Pillar of Delhi is a textbook example of historical Indian ironworking and unusual resistance to rusting. It does not mean all iron withstands outdoor conditions without care.

4.3–4.4 Air, water and oxides

In the NCERT virtual magnesium example, magnesium reacts with oxygen to form a white magnesium oxide powder. The oxide in contact with water gives a basic mixture. Red litmus turns blue, while blue litmus remains blue.

In the sulfur example, sulfur dioxide is the oxide. When dissolved in water it produces an acidic mixture, described in the textbook as forming sulfurous acid. Blue litmus turns red while red litmus remains red. Sulfur itself shows no visible reaction with water in the textbook comparison. These are separate observations.

Textbook comparisonResult with oxygenWith water and litmus
MagnesiumMagnesium oxide, a white solidBasic mixture. Red → blue.
SulfurSulfur dioxide, a gasAcidic mixture when dissolved. Blue → red.
Solid sulfur with waterNo oxide step in this trialNo visible reaction under classroom conditions.

Many metal oxides are basic and many non-metal oxides are acidic. Some behave differently. Aluminium oxide and zinc oxide can react with both acids and bases under suitable conditions. Carbon monoxide is an example of a neutral oxide. These exceptions are enrichment.

Sodium reacts vigorously with water. The textbook discusses protective storage of sodium and white phosphorus. These are hazard examples for reading, with no home or student handling. The oxide simulator represents products and litmus results without generating gases or asking students to look at burning magnesium.

4.5 Everyday uses, alloys and material selection

ApplicationUseful material property
Cooking panFood-safe metal transfers heat. Copper transfers heat efficiently and iron is durable. Practical cookware may have a lining.
Wire and insulationA copper core conducts and can be drawn into wire. The plastic covering insulates.
JewelleryGold and silver can be shaped and polished. Gold resists corrosion well.
Tools and bridgesStrength and toughness matter. Steel combines iron with carbon and can include other elements.
OxygenUsed by many organisms in respiration. This wording allows for organisms that live without oxygen.
Carbon and nitrogenCarbon is part of many molecules in living things. Nitrogen compounds support plant growth and are used in fertilisers.
Chlorine and iodineControlled chlorine compounds are used in water disinfection. Iodine-containing products have antiseptic uses. These examples concern manufactured products and professional controls.

Indian metal crafts

Compare Bastar Dhokra of Chhattisgarh, Bidriware associated with Karnataka, Pembarthi brasswork in Telangana and the Kamrupi metalwork tradition of Assam. Dhokra is practised in several regions. The textbook relates early copper and gold objects to later use of iron for strong tools.

Metal recycling can reduce waste and the need for new extraction. Consider lifetime, maintenance and recyclability as well as the material’s classroom properties.

30 worked questions and examples

NCERT questions 1–12

NCERT Q1 · Choose a suitable low-cost folding metal for packaging

Worked answer. Aluminium, option (i). It can be rolled into thin sheets, and those sheets fold readily.

NCERT Q2 · Identify the metal that can catch fire on contact with water

Worked answer. Sodium, option (iv). Its vigorous reaction releases heat. This is a reading example for students.

NCERT Q3 · Evaluate the four statements about metals, oxides, oxygen and copper vessels

Worked answer. (i) False, aluminium and copper are metals. (ii) False for the textbook basic oxide example, in which red litmus turns blue. (iii) True, oxygen is a non-metal used in respiration by many organisms. (iv) False, the relevant property for boiling water is thermal conductivity.

NCERT Q4 · Explain why only some metals suit jewellery

Worked answer. Jewellery needs a suitable combination of appearance, workability and durability. Gold, for example, can be shaped and polished and resists corrosion.

NCERT Q5 · Match the everyday uses to the five scrambled material names

Worked answer. (i) Copper, (ii) gold, (iii) oxygen, (iv) nitrogen and (v) chlorine. The properties are electrical conduction, workability, use in aerobic respiration, plant growth and controlled water disinfection respectively.

NCERT Q6 · Compare how magnesium and sulfur react with oxygen

Worked answer. Magnesium forms white magnesium oxide, whose water mixture shows basic behaviour. Sulfur forms sulfur dioxide, which gives acidic behaviour in water. Red litmus turns blue in the first example and blue turns red in the second.

NCERT Q7 · Complete the textbook ash and litmus flowchart

Worked answer. Magnesium is the starting material. The white ash is magnesium oxide and its water mixture is basic. Blue litmus stays blue. The diagram already gives blue as the final colour for the red test.

NCERT Q8 · Choose from iron, copper, sulfur, coal, plastic, wood and cardboard for a boiling pan

Worked answer. Copper is a strong choice for quick heat transfer, using suitable food-safe cookware. Iron is also practical because it withstands cooking temperatures and is durable. The non-metal examples and comparison materials are impractical vessel bodies for conventional boiling.

NCERT Q9 · Compare iron nails dipped in oil, water and vinegar

Worked answer. The oil-covered nail is expected to avoid rust if the coating excludes moisture and the nail starts dry. The water and vinegar are water-based and can allow corrosion if oxygen is present. Vinegar can also dissolve existing oxide, so appearance alone does not rank rust rates.

NCERT Q10 · Explain how material properties determine their uses

Worked answer. Choose copper wire for ductility and electrical conduction, a wooden handle to slow heat transfer and a bell material that rings well. Each choice links a property to a need.

NCERT Q11 · Explain why sulfur is unsuitable in place of zinc coating

Worked answer. Being visibly unreactive with water in a brief trial is insufficient. Sulfur is brittle and does not provide the adherent, durable and sacrificial protection that a suitable zinc coating can offer.

NCERT Q12 · Explain why an ironsmith heats iron before shaping tools

Worked answer. Hot iron tends to deform more easily under a craftsperson’s controlled blows. It can be reshaped with less force before cooling. A student can study this in a diagram or video without operating a furnace.

Additional examples

Example 1 · Distinguish malleability and ductility using aluminium and copper

Worked answer. Aluminium foil is a sheet made by compressive shaping. Copper wire is drawn out, illustrating ductility.

Example 2 · Explain why a bright steel spoon can become dull

Worked answer. The surface can collect dirt, a coating or reaction products. The underlying material does not change category based on appearance.

Example 3 · Classify a bare conductor that is actually graphite

Worked answer. The conduction observation stands. Its known identity is a form of carbon and carbon is a non-metal.

Example 4 · Distinguish hardness from brittleness

Worked answer. A hard sample resists indentation or scratching. A brittle sample breaks with little deformation. One solid can be both.

Example 5 · Explain why wood is included in the textbook comparison

Worked answer. It supplies contrasting evidence about sound, heat or shaping. Wood is a complex material rather than a chemical element.

Example 6 · Use a low-voltage control before testing a coated coin

Worked answer. Check the bulb with clean metal first. If the coated coin gives no glow, inspect coating and contact rather than assume it contains no metal.

Example 7 · Explain heat conduction in a metal spoon

Worked answer. Thermal energy transfers through the solid along the temperature difference. A metal handle can warm farther from the water.

Example 8 · Explain a wooden handle’s purpose

Worked answer. Wood transfers heat relatively slowly. This can reduce the rate of warming at the hand.

Example 9 · Compare the sound of a metal coin and a wood block

Worked answer. A similar gentle strike gives a longer ring for the coin and a duller sound for the block in the textbook comparison. Shape and support can also matter.

Example 10 · Explain why bottle A remains clear of visible rust

Worked answer. Silica gel reduces moisture. Oxygen alone is insufficient for the ordinary rusting conditions tested.

Example 11 · Explain why bottle B is different from C

Worked answer. Cooled boiled water in B has much less dissolved air, and oil helps prevent more entering. C has both ordinary water and contact with air.

Example 12 · Predict what a scratched paint layer does

Worked answer. The scratch can let water and oxygen reach iron. Rust may start there even while covered areas remain protected.

Example 13 · Explain the difference between rust and silver tarnish

Worked answer. Rust forms on iron and ordinary steel. Dark tarnish on silver is a different surface reaction.

Example 14 · Interpret an oxide mixture where red litmus becomes blue

Worked answer. The observation supports basic behaviour. The magnesium oxide setup gives this textbook result.

Example 15 · Interpret blue litmus becoming red in the sulfur comparison

Worked answer. Dissolved sulfur dioxide creates acidic conditions. This is a property of the oxide in water, distinct from putting solid sulfur into water.

Example 16 · Choose a material for a ringing bell

Worked answer. Choose a suitable sonorous metal alloy. Its shape and mounting also influence the observed ring.

Example 17 · Explain why material choice for a bridge depends on more than hardness

Worked answer. Strength, toughness, weight, corrosion resistance and maintenance also matter.

Example 18 · Explain why graphite and sulfur cannot be described by one rule about all non-metals

Worked answer. Graphite conducts electricity, while sulfur typically does not in a school tester. Test results and material identity matter.

Quick revision and common mistakes

  • Malleability makes sheets. Ductility makes wires.
  • Lustre, sound or conductivity alone does not establish that a sample is a metal.
  • Graphite is a conducting non-metal. Wood and plastic are comparison materials, not elements.
  • Rust is specific to iron and ordinary steel. Rusting requires oxygen and water.
  • The magnesium and sulfur examples show different indicator changes. Each oxide and test condition matters.
  • A brown deposit is an observation. Rusting is an inference supported by the controls.
