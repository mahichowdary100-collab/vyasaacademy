Class 10 Mathematics – Chapter 7: Coordinate Geometry

Coordinate geometry is the meeting point of algebra and geometry: every point of a plane is given a pair of numbers, and every pair of numbers is a point. Once a location has a number-pair, distances and midpoints can be calculated instead of measured. You already drew graphs of linear equations in earlier classes; in Chapter 6 – Triangles you reasoned with ratios and proportions, and in Chapter 5 – Arithmetic Progressions you studied steady, ordered patterns. This chapter puts both ideas on a numbered grid: it gives the distance formula and the midpoint formula, the two workhorses used everywhere from maps and floor plans to navigation and machine-crafted layouts. By the end you will be able to place points, name their quadrants, read coordinates from a graph, measure the gap between any two points and find the exact middle of any segment.

1. Introduction to Coordinate Geometry

Coordinate geometry answers a simple question: where is this point, and how far apart are two points? Instead of describing positions with words ("roughly in the middle, a bit to the left"), it pins every location to a pair of numbers measured from fixed reference lines. Geometry done this way becomes arithmetic, and arithmetic can be done quickly, exactly and automatically.

The same tool runs the world around you: the grid on a city map that gives every street a number and letter, the floor plan of a house drawn on a grid, the search-by-coordinates of a navigation app, the square-by-square layout of a spreadsheet, and the position of a ship or an aircraft reported as a latitude and longitude. In every case, two numbers locate one point, and nothing about the shape is lost in the process.

Cartesian plane

A flat grid made by two perpendicular number lines that cross at the origin.

Ordered pair \((x,y)\)

Two numbers that name one point; the order matters.

Distance

The straight-line gap between two points, found with the distance formula.

Midpoint

The point exactly halfway between two points, found by averaging coordinates.

What you will learn in this chapter:

  • Describe the Cartesian plane, the axes, the origin and ordered pairs.
  • Name the four quadrants and recall the sign pattern of each.
  • Plot a point from its coordinates and read coordinates back from a graph.
  • Apply the distance formula, including points with negative coordinates and horizontal or vertical segments.
  • Find the midpoint of a segment and recover an unknown endpoint when the midpoint is known.
  • Recognise vertical and horizontal lines from the coordinates of their points.
  • Use distance and midpoint calculations to check simple geometric shapes like right triangles and rectangles.

2. Cartesian Coordinate System

The Cartesian coordinate system places every point of the plane on a grid made of two number lines that cross at right angles at their zero marks. The horizontal number line is the \(x\)-axis; the vertical number line is the \(y\)-axis; and their crossing point is the origin \(O(0,0)\). Positive numbers lie to the right on the \(x\)-axis and upwards on the \(y\)-axis; negative numbers lie to the left and downwards.

A point is named by an ordered pair written as \(P(x,y)\). The first number \(x\) is the x-coordinate (also called the abscissa) and tells how far the point is from the \(y\)-axis, moving right for positive values and left for negative values. The second number \(y\) is the y-coordinate (also called the ordinate) and tells how far the point is from the \(x\)-axis, moving up for positive values and down for negative values.

Ordered pair \(P(x,y)\)

\(P(x,y)\) locates a point whose x-coordinate (abscissa) is \(x\) and whose y-coordinate (ordinate) is \(y\). The pair is ordered: \((x,y)\) and \((y,x)\) are generally different points. For example \(P(2,3)\) is two units right of the \(y\)-axis and three units above the \(x\)-axis, while \(P(3,2)\) is three units right and two units above.

Reading order

Always read "across, then up": the first coordinate is the horizontal step \((x)\), the second is the vertical step \((y)\). This is why \((2,3)\) and \((3,2)\) are two different places on the plane.

3. Quadrants

The two axes divide the plane into four regions called quadrants, numbered anticlockwise starting from the top-right region. The sign pattern of a point tells you instantly which quadrant it lives in.

Quadrantx-coordinatey-coordinateExample
First (I)PositivePositive\((3,4)\)
Second (II)NegativePositive\((-3,4)\)
Third (III)NegativeNegative\((-3,-4)\)
Fourth (IV)PositiveNegative\((3,-4)\)

Points on the axes

A point never belongs to a quadrant if it sits on an axis:

  • A point of the form \((a,0)\) lies on the x-axis; its ordinate is 0.
  • A point of the form \((0,b)\) lies on the y-axis; its abscissa is 0.
  • The point \((0,0)\) is the origin \(O\), where both axes meet.
Memory hook

Scan the signs of \((x,y)\). "Positive, negative" means fourth quadrant, "negative, positive" means second. Zero in either slot means the point is on an axis, not in any quadrant.

4. Plotting Points

Plotting means converting a pair of numbers into a marked dot on the plane. The rule is to move exactly like a set of driving directions: turn right or left for the x-step, then up or down for the y-step.

1
Start at the origin \(O(0,0)\).
2
Move along the \(x\)-axis: right by \(x\) units when \(x\) is positive, left by \(|x|\) units when \(x\) is negative. (If \(x = 0\), do not move horizontally.)
3
From there, move parallel to the \(y\)-axis: up by \(y\) units when \(y\) is positive, down by \(|y|\) units when \(y\) is negative. (If \(y = 0\), do not move vertically.)
4
Mark the point with a clear dot.
5
Label the point with its ordered pair so it can be read back later.
Worked example (plotting)

Plot \(P(-3,4)\). Start at \(O\). The x-coordinate is \(-3\), so move 3 units to the left along the x-axis. The y-coordinate is \(+4\), so move 4 units up parallel to the y-axis. Mark and label the dot \(P(-3,4)\). This point lies in the second quadrant, which is exactly what the signs \(( -, + )\) predict.

5. Reading Coordinates from a Graph

Reading coordinates is the reverse of plotting: start at the point, and travel straight to the axes to find the two numbers. Drop a vertical line from the point to the \(x\)-axis: the number where it lands is the x-coordinate. Drop a horizontal line to the \(y\)-axis: the number where it lands is the y-coordinate.

Reading rule

Read the x-coordinate first, then the y-coordinate second, and write the answer as the ordered pair \((x,y)\). For example, the points above read as \(\mathbf{P}(3,2)\), \(\mathbf{Q}(-2,3)\), \(\mathbf{R}(2,-3)\) and \(\mathbf{S}(-4,1)\).

Careful

The horizontal step is always recorded first. Swapping gives a different point: reading \(P\) as \((2,3)\) instead of \((3,2)\) moves the point to a completely different location.

6. Distance Between Two Points

The distance between two points \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\) is the length of the straight segment joining them. To find it without measuring, we borrow a right triangle from the grid and use the Pythagoras theorem.

Distance formula

\[ \boxed{AB = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}} \]

What each part means:

  • \((x_2 - x_1)\) is the horizontal step between the points; squaring it makes it non-negative even when the step is leftwards.
  • \((y_2 - y_1)\) is the vertical step between the points; squaring removes the sign.
  • The two squares are the two legs of a right triangle, so their sum is the square of the hypotenuse.
  • The square root at the end converts the squared length back into an actual distance.

The point \(C\) shares \(A\)'s y-coordinate and \(B\)'s x-coordinate, so \(AC\) is horizontal and \(CB\) is vertical, making \(ABC\) a right triangle with \(AB\) as its hypotenuse. The horizontal leg has length \(|x_2 - x_1|\), the vertical leg has length \(|y_2 - y_1|\), and the Pythagoras theorem turns those two legs into the hypotenuse \(AB = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}\).

7. Derivation of the Distance Formula

We derive the formula from scratch so you can see exactly why it works. Take two points \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\) and introduce the helper point \(C(x_2,y_1)\), the corner where the horizontal step from \(A\) meets the vertical step from \(B\).

Derivation — Distance formula
Horizontal difference: travelling from \(A\) to \(B\), the x-coordinate changes by \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\). This is the length of leg \(AC\).
Vertical difference: travelling from \(A\) to \(B\), the y-coordinate changes by \(\Delta y = y_2 - y_1\). This is the length of leg \(CB\).
Apply Pythagoras: in the right triangle \(ACB\), the hypotenuse squared equals the sum of the squares of the two legs:
\[ AB^2 = (\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 \]
Take the positive square root: distance is a length, never negative, so
\[ \boxed{AB = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}} \]
Order does not matter

Squares wipe out minus signs, so \(AB\) computed as "from A to B" equals \(BA\) computed as "from B to A". You may subtract either way round within each bracket.

8. Distance Formula — Worked Examples

Each example follows the same skeleton: Given → Formula → Substitution → Simplification → Answer.

Worked example 1 — both coordinates positive

Given: \(A(2,3)\) and \(B(5,7)\). Find \(AB\).

Formula: \(AB = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\)

Substitution: \(AB = \sqrt{(5 - 2)^2 + (7 - 3)^2}\)

Simplification: \(= \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25}\)

Answer: \(AB = 5\) units. ✓

Worked example 2 — negative coordinates

Given: \(P(-2,-1)\) and \(Q(2,2)\). Find \(PQ\).

Formula: \(PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\)

Substitution: \(PQ = \sqrt{(2 - (-2))^2 + (2 - (-1))^2}\)

Simplification: \(= \sqrt{(2 + 2)^2 + (2 + 1)^2} = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25}\)

Answer: \(PQ = 5\) units. ✓

Worked example 3 — points in different quadrants

Given: \(A(-1,2)\) in the second quadrant and \(B(3,-1)\) in the fourth quadrant. Find \(AB\).

Formula: \(AB = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\)

Substitution: \(AB = \sqrt{(3 - (-1))^2 + (-1 - 2)^2}\)

Simplification: \(= \sqrt{(3 + 1)^2 + (-3)^2} = \sqrt{4^2 + 9} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25}\)

Answer: \(AB = 5\) units. ✓

Worked example 4 — horizontal line

Given: \(P(-2,1)\) and \(Q(4,1)\). Find \(PQ\).

Formula: \(PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\)

Substitution: \(PQ = \sqrt{(4 - (-2))^2 + (1 - 1)^2}\)

Simplification: \(= \sqrt{6^2 + 0^2} = \sqrt{36}\)

Answer: \(PQ = 6\) units. The two points share the same y-coordinate, so the segment is horizontal and the distance is simply \(|4 - (-2)| = 6\). ✓

Worked example 5 — vertical line

Given: \(M(3,-1)\) and \(N(3,5)\). Find \(MN\).

Formula: \(MN = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\)

Substitution: \(MN = \sqrt{(3 - 3)^2 + (5 - (-1))^2}\)

Simplification: \(= \sqrt{0^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{36}\)

Answer: \(MN = 6\) units. The two points share the same x-coordinate, so the segment is vertical and the distance is simply \(|5 - (-1)| = 6\). ✓

Worked example 6 — unknown coordinate from a given distance

Given: The distance between \(A(1,2)\) and \(P(x,-1)\) is 5 units. Find \(x\).

Formula: \(AP = \sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + (-1 - 2)^2} = 5\)

Substitution: \(\sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + (-3)^2} = \sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + 9} = 5\)

Simplification: square both sides: \((x - 1)^2 + 9 = 25\), so \((x - 1)^2 = 16\), giving \(x - 1 = \pm 4\).

Answer: \(x = 5\) or \(x = -3\). Both \((5,-1)\) and \((-3,-1)\) are exactly 5 units from \(A(1,2)\). ✓

9. Midpoint of a Line Segment

The midpoint of a segment \(AB\) is the single point \(M\) that lies exactly halfway between \(A\) and \(B\). Split the horizontal step in half and the vertical step in half, and the two halves meet at the midpoint.

Midpoint formula

\[ \boxed{M\left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}\,,\ \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)} \]

Why averaging? To reach the middle of the segment you must cover half of the total horizontal travel and half of the total vertical travel. Halfway between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) on the number line is their average \(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}\), and likewise for the y-coordinates.

10. Midpoint Formula — Derivation

Let \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\) be the endpoints, and let \(M(x,y)\) be their midpoint. Project \(A\), \(M\) and \(B\) straight down onto the x-axis. They land on the x-axis at \((x_1,0)\), \((x,0)\) and \((x_2,0)\) in that order, and projection preserves "halfway between": the projected point of \(M\) is exactly the midpoint of the two projected endpoints on the axis.

Derivation — Midpoint formula
Midpoint on the x-axis: a point halfway between \((x_1,0)\) and \((x_2,0)\) splits the gap \(x_2 - x_1\) into two equal halves. Halfway from \(x_1\) toward \(x_2\) is
\[ x = x_1 + \frac{x_2 - x_1}{2} = \frac{2x_1 + x_2 - x_1}{2} = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2} \]
Midpoint on the y-axis: projecting A, M and B onto the y-axis gives, by exactly the same argument,
\[ y = y_1 + \frac{y_2 - y_1}{2} = \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \]
Conclusion: the midpoint is
\[ \boxed{M\left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}\,,\ \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)} \]
Check it is halfway

Verify with the diagram above: \(A(1,2)\) and \(B(7,6)\) differ by 6 in x and 4 in y. The midpoint moves 3 right and 2 up from \(A\), landing at \((4,4)\), and 3 left and 2 down from \(B\) lands at the same \((4,4)\).

11. Midpoint Formula — Worked Examples

Worked example 1 — midpoint of two points

Given: \(A(2,4)\) and \(B(6,8)\). Find the midpoint \(M\).

Formula: \(M\left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)\)

Substitution: \(M\left( \frac{2 + 6}{2}, \frac{4 + 8}{2} \right)\)

Calculation: \(= \left( \frac{8}{2}, \frac{12}{2} \right) = (4,6)\)

Answer: \(M(4,6)\). ✓

Worked example 2 — negative coordinates

Given: \(P(-4,-2)\) and \(Q(2,6)\). Find the midpoint \(M\).

Formula: \(M\left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)\)

Substitution: \(M\left( \frac{-4 + 2}{2}, \frac{-2 + 6}{2} \right)\)

Calculation: \(= \left( \frac{-2}{2}, \frac{4}{2} \right) = (-1,2)\)

Answer: \(M(-1,2)\). ✓

Worked example 3 — points in different quadrants

Given: \(A(5,-3)\) in the fourth quadrant and \(B(-1,7)\) in the second quadrant. Find the midpoint \(M\).

Formula: \(M\left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)\)

Substitution: \(M\left( \frac{5 + (-1)}{2}, \frac{-3 + 7}{2} \right)\)

Calculation: \(= \left( \frac{4}{2}, \frac{4}{2} \right) = (2,2)\)

Answer: \(M(2,2)\). ✓

Worked example 4 — unknown endpoint

Given: \(M(3,4)\) is the midpoint of \(A(1,2)\) and \(B\). Find \(B\).

Formula: \(M\left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)\) with \(A\) as \((x_1,y_1)\) and \(B\) as \((x_2,y_2)\).

Substitution: \(\frac{1 + x_2}{2} = 3\) and \(\frac{2 + y_2}{2} = 4\).

Calculation: \(1 + x_2 = 6 \implies x_2 = 5\); \(2 + y_2 = 8 \implies y_2 = 6\).

Answer: \(B(5,6)\). ✓ (Check: \(M\) of \((1,2)\) and \((5,6)\) is \(\left(3,4\right)\), correct.)

Worked example 5 — application

Given: Two towns stand at \(A(-3,1)\) and \(B(7,5)\) on a district map. A water tank is to be built at the point exactly halfway between them. Find the tank's position.

Formula: \(M\left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)\)

Substitution: \(M\left( \frac{-3 + 7}{2}, \frac{1 + 5}{2} \right)\)

Calculation: \(= \left( \frac{4}{2}, \frac{6}{2} \right) = (2,3)\)

Answer: The tank should be built at \((2,3)\). ✓

12. Applications of Coordinate Geometry

The same two ideas — distance and midpoint — run a surprising amount of the world. Whenever a position can be pinned to a grid, coordinate geometry can measure it without a tape.

Maps

Every street and landmark has a grid cell; distance and midpoint help locate the real gap between two places.

Floor plans

A house plan drawn on a grid lets builders read exact placements and find the centre of a room or the middle of a wall.

Position tracking

A delivery rider or a ship is a live point on a map; distance-between-points answers "how far away is it now?".

Grid layouts

Games, chessboards, spreadsheets and printed layouts all use number-pairs to place objects precisely.

Measuring distances

Distance on a scaled map, straight-line gaps in a park, and rejection distances in sports can all be computed from coordinates.

Midpoint of a path

Finding the halfway point of a route, the centre of a plot, or the balance point of two ends is a midpoint calculation.

Simple modelling

Ideas like "is this corner square?" or "is this plot a rectangle?" are geometry questions the distance formula can answer with coordinates.

Real-world link

Navigation apps compute straight-line ("as the crow flies") distances with the distance formula, then combine the midpoint idea with road data to give the shortest practical route between two locations.

13. Important Conceptual Relationships

Two coordinates can tell you a geometric fact without any calculation. When the two coordinates of a pair match, the points line up neatly.

ConditionMeaningDistance simplifies to
\(x_1 = x_2\)The segment is vertical (points share one x-coordinate)\(AB = |y_2 - y_1|\)
\(y_1 = y_2\)The segment is horizontal (points share one y-coordinate)\(AB = |x_2 - x_1|\)
\(x_1 = x_2\) and \(y_1 = y_2\)The "two" points are the same point\(AB = 0\)
Common trap

A vertical line is not "x is the same as y". It is "x is the same as each other for every point on it", i.e. all the points share one x-value. Penned the other way: a horizontal line shares one y-value.

14. Coordinate Geometry and Geometric Shapes

Coordinates turn shape-properties into number-checks. Because the distance formula gives every side and diagonal exactly, we can verify whether a figure is a right triangle, a rectangle, a square or a set of collinear points by pure calculation.

Worked example — a right triangle check

Given: \(A(1,2)\), \(B(4,2)\), \(C(4,6)\). Show that \(\triangle ABC\) is right-angled.

Working: find all three side lengths with the distance formula.

\(AB = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (2-2)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 0} = 3\)

\(BC = \sqrt{(4-4)^2 + (6-2)^2} = \sqrt{0 + 16} = 4\)

\(AC = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (6-2)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\)

Conclusion: \(3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5^2\), so the Pythagoras relation holds and \(\triangle ABC\) is right-angled at \(B\) (where the horizontal side \(AB\) meets the vertical side \(BC\)). ✓

Worked example — a rectangle check

Given: \(A(1,1)\), \(B(5,1)\), \(C(5,3)\), \(D(1,3)\). Show that \(ABCD\) is a rectangle.

Working: check sides and diagonals.

Sides: \(AB\) has \(y_1 = y_2 = 1\), so it is horizontal with \(AB = 4\). \(BC\) has \(x_1 = x_2 = 5\), so it is vertical with \(BC = 2\). \(CD\) is horizontal with \(CD = 4\) and \(DA\) is vertical with \(DA = 2\). Every corner joins a horizontal side to a vertical side, giving four right angles.

Diagonals: \(AC = \sqrt{(5-1)^2 + (3-1)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 4} = \sqrt{20}\) and \(BD = \sqrt{(1-5)^2 + (3-1)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 4} = \sqrt{20}\). They are equal, confirming the rectangle. ✓

Also useful

Three points \(A, B, C\) are collinear (on one straight line) precisely when the larger distance equals the sum of the two smaller ones, i.e. \(AB + BC = AC\) for the correct ordering. For example, \(P(-1,2)\), \(Q(1,4)\), \(R(3,6)\) satisfy \(PQ = 2\sqrt{2}\), \(QR = 2\sqrt{2}\) and \(PR = 4\sqrt{2}\), so they lie on one line.

15. Common Mistakes

MistakeWhy it happensFix
Writing y before x, e.g. \((4,3)\) instead of \((3,4)\)Reading up before acrossAlways write \((x,y)\): across first, up second
Wrong quadrant for a pointIgnoring one of the signsCheck both signs; \(( -, + )\) means left and up, quadrant II
Sign errors inside the bracketsSubtracting a negative incorrectlyBracket the negative number: \((x_2 - (-3))^2\)
Forgetting brackets around negative differencesSquaring a negative difference sloppilySquare the whole difference: \((-4)^2\), never \(-4^2 = -16\)
Wrong subtraction orderMixing \(x_1 - x_2\) with \(y_2 - y_1\)Squaring fixes the sign, but stay consistent: both as \(x_2-x_1\) and \(y_2-y_1\)
Forgetting to squareAdding differences without squaringThe formula needs squares: write each bracket squared
Ignoring the square rootStopping at the squared numberFinish with \(\sqrt{\,}\) and report the distance
Using the wrong endpoint's coordinates in midpointAveraging mismatched pairsAverage the x's together and the y's together, never x with y
Dividing instead of averagingMisremembering the midpoint ruleAdd the two coordinates first, then divide each sum by 2

Pattern to internalise

\((3,-4)\) is not the same as \((-3,4)\). The first is right and down (quadrant IV); the second is left and up (quadrant II). One careless sign moves the point across two quadrants.

16. Exam Tips

Exam-ready checklist
  • Always write coordinates in the \((x,y)\) order, and label plots with their ordered pair.
  • Check the signs of both coordinates before substituting.
  • Write the formula first, then substitute values underneath it.
  • Use brackets for negative numbers: \((x_2 - (-3))^2\) removes sign confusion.
  • Simplify the squares carefully and add them before taking the root.
  • Confirm the final distance is non-negative; a distance can never be negative.
  • For midpoints, average the x-coordinates separately and the y-coordinates separately.
  • Label points clearly on diagrams so the examiner can follow your working.
  • Show enough steps: each substitution line earns marks, even if the final answer is wrong.
Time-saving check

When the coordinates share an x or a y, skip the full formula mentally: vertical distance is \(|y_2 - y_1|\), horizontal distance is \(|x_2 - x_1|\). Spotting this can save a full minute per question.

19. Worked Examples — Consolidated Section

The ten examples below travel through the whole chapter: quadrants, plotting, reading, distances (positive, negative, horizontal, vertical), midpoints, unknown endpoints and a geometric figure.

Worked Example 1
Question: Identify the quadrant of each point: \(P(-3,5)\), \(Q(4,-2)\), \(R(-2,-6)\), \(S(5,1)\), and the locations of \(T(0,3)\) and \(U(4,0)\).
Concept: the sign pattern \((x,y)\) decides the quadrant; a zero coordinate means the point is on an axis.
Working: \(P(-3,5)\) is \((-,+)\) → quadrant II. \(Q(4,-2)\) is \((+,-)\) → quadrant IV. \(R(-2,-6)\) is \((-,-)\) → quadrant III. \(S(5,1)\) is \((+,+)\) → quadrant I. \(T(0,3)\) has \(x = 0\) → on the y-axis. \(U(4,0)\) has \(y = 0\) → on the x-axis.
Final Answer: P → II, Q → IV, R → III, S → I, T on the y-axis, U on the x-axis.
Worked Example 2
Question: Plot the point \((3,-2)\).
Concept: move along x first, then parallel to y.
Working: start at \(O(0,0)\); move 3 units right along the x-axis; then move 2 units down parallel to the y-axis.
Final Answer: the dot at \((3,-2)\), in quadrant IV.
Worked Example 3
Question: On a grid, a point is 2 units right of the y-axis and 5 units below the x-axis. Write its coordinates.
Concept: "right of the y-axis" fixes the x-coordinate; "below the x-axis" fixes the y-coordinate.
Working: right by 2 means \(x = 2\); below the x-axis by 5 means \(y = -5\).
Final Answer: the point is \((2,-5)\).
Worked Example 4
Question: Find the distance between \(A(1,2)\) and \(B(4,6)\).
Formula: \(AB = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}\)
Substitution: \(AB = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (6-2)^2}\)
Working: \(=\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\)
Final Answer: \(AB = 5\) units.
Worked Example 5
Question: Find the distance between \(P(-2,3)\) and \(Q(4,-5)\).
Formula: \(PQ = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}\)
Substitution: \(PQ = \sqrt{(4 - (-2))^2 + (-5 - 3)^2}\)
Working: \(=\sqrt{(4+2)^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{6^2 + 64} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\)
Final Answer: \(PQ = 10\) units.
Worked Example 6
Question: Find the distance between \(P(-3,4)\) and \(Q(5,4)\).
Concept: the y-coordinates are equal, so the segment is horizontal.
Formula: \(PQ = |x_2 - x_1| = |5 - (-3)|\)
Working: \(= |5+3| = 8\)
Final Answer: \(PQ = 8\) units.
Worked Example 7
Question: Find the distance between \(A(2,-1)\) and \(B(2,7)\).
Concept: the x-coordinates are equal, so the segment is vertical.
Formula: \(AB = |y_2 - y_1| = |7 - (-1)|\)
Working: \(= |7+1| = 8\)
Final Answer: \(AB = 8\) units.
Worked Example 8
Question: Find the midpoint of \(A(-2,3)\) and \(B(6,-7)\).
Formula: \(M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\)
Substitution: \(M\left(\frac{-2+6}{2}, \frac{3+(-7)}{2}\right)\)
Working: \(= \left(\frac{4}{2}, \frac{-4}{2}\right) = (2,-2)\)
Final Answer: midpoint \(M(2,-2)\).
Worked Example 9
Question: \(M(-1,2)\) is the midpoint of \(A(3,5)\) and \(B(p,q)\). Find \(p\) and \(q\).
Formula: \(M\left(\frac{x_A+x_B}{2}, \frac{y_A+y_B}{2}\right)\)
Substitution: \(\frac{3+p}{2} = -1\) and \(\frac{5+q}{2} = 2\)
Working: \(3 + p = -2 \implies p = -5\); \(5 + q = 4 \implies q = -1\)
Final Answer: \(B(-5,-1)\). Check: midpoint of \((3,5)\) and \((-5,-1)\) is \(\left(\frac{-2}{2}, \frac{4}{2}\right) = (-1,2)\). ✓
Worked Example 10
Question: \(A(1,1)\), \(B(5,1)\), \(C(5,3)\) and \(D(1,3)\) are the corners of a field on a map. Show that the field is a rectangle and state its perimeter.
Concept: parallel-to-the-axes corners give right angles; equal diagonals confirm the rectangle.
Formula: \(AB = |x_2-x_1|\), \(BC = |y_2-y_1|\), \(AC = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}\)
Working: \(AB = 5 - 1 = 4\), \(BC = 3 - 1 = 2\), \(CD = 5 - 1 = 4\) (horizontal), \(DA = 3 - 1 = 2\) (vertical). Diagonals \(AC = \sqrt{16+4} = \sqrt{20}\) and \(BD = \sqrt{16+4} = \sqrt{20}\) are equal. Perimeter \(= 4 + 2 + 4 + 2 = 12\).
Final Answer: \(ABCD\) is a rectangle of perimeter 12 units.
