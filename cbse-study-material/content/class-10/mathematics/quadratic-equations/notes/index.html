Class 10 Mathematics – Chapter 4: Quadratic Equations

This chapter introduces equations in which the highest power of the variable is 2. You will learn to recognise a quadratic equation, write it in standard form, solve it by factorisation or with the quadratic formula, and use the discriminant to predict the nature of its roots. The same tools then help you answer real-life problems about areas, consecutive numbers, train speeds, heights and revenues. In Chapter 1 – Real Numbers and Chapter 2 – Polynomials, factorisation helped split numbers and expressions; here it becomes the key to solving a whole family of equations.

Notes Practice Paper Quiz Chapter Test

1. Introduction to Quadratic Equations

A quadratic equation in one variable is an equation that can be written in the form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), where the unknown appears with highest exponent 2. The word "quadratic" comes from quadratus, Latin for "square".

Why do they matter? Whenever two things multiply to a fixed product — the two dimensions of a garden, the speed and time of a journey, the number of rows and benches in a hall — the relationship often becomes quadratic. Squaring also appears when a ball is thrown: its height is a quadratic function of time.

One variable

A single letter, usually \(x\), carrying the square.

Highest power 2

After simplification the \(x^2\) term must survive.

Three coefficients

\[a x^2 + b x + c\] with \(a \neq 0\).

Two roots

Up to two real roots — equal, distinct or none.

2. What You Will Learn

  • Recognise a quadratic equation and express it in standard form.
  • Identify \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\), including equations not yet in standard form.
  • Find roots — by substitution, by factorisation and by the quadratic formula.
  • Compute the discriminant and predict the nature of the roots.
  • Fix unknown coefficients when a condition on the roots is given.
  • Form and solve quadratic equations from word problems.
  • Answer competency and case-based questions with reasoning, not just calculation.

3. Identifying a Quadratic Equation

A quadratic equation contains a variable with highest power 2. When it is written out, collected and simplified, the term with \(x^2\) must still be present with a non-zero coefficient.

Definition

An equation of the form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), where \(a \neq 0\), and \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) are real numbers, is a quadratic equation in one variable \(x\).

Not every equation that contains an \(x^2\) term is automatically quadratic — sometimes the squared terms cancel when both sides are simplified.

EquationSimplify / noteQuadratic?
\(2x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0\)Already in standard formYes
\(x^2 = 25\)\(x^2 - 25 = 0\), so \(b = 0\)Yes
\(x(x + 3) = 0\)\(x^2 + 3x = 0\), so \(c = 0\)Yes
\(4x - 7 = 0\)No \(x^2\) term at allNo — linear
\((x + 1)^2 = x^2 + 5\)\(x^2 + 2x + 1 = x^2 + 5\) gives \(2x = 4\)No — the \(x^2\) terms cancel
\(x^2(x - 1) = 0\)Expands to \(x^3 - x^2 = 0\)No — highest power is 3
\((x - 2)(x + 2) = 0\)\(x^2 - 4 = 0\)Yes
Careful

Always simplify both sides completely before deciding. A squared term that cancels does not make the equation quadratic.

4. Standard Form

Exams expect a quadratic to be written as

Standard form

\[ax^2 + bx + c = 0, \qquad a \neq 0\]

where:

  • \(a\) is the coefficient of \(x^2\) (must not be 0, otherwise there is no \(x^2\) term and the equation is no longer quadratic);
  • \(b\) is the coefficient of \(x\);
  • \(c\) is the constant term.

Either \(b\) or \(c\) may be 0 — the equation is still quadratic as long as \(a \neq 0\).

Worked example

Write \(x(2x - 5) = 3\) in standard form. Expand: \(2x^2 - 5x = 3\). Bring everything to one side: \(2x^2 - 5x - 3 = 0\). Here \(a = 2\), \(b = -5\), \(c = -3\).

5. Identifying \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\)

Before applying any method, put the equation in standard form and read the coefficients with their signs. A common exam pitfall is misreading a negative coefficient.

Tip

Multiplying an equation by a non-zero constant does not change its roots, so \(x^2 + 6x - 2 = 0\) and \(2x^2 + 12x - 4 = 0\) have exactly the same solutions.

6. Roots and Solutions

A root (or solution) of the quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) is a value of \(x\) that satisfies the equation. Putting the value into the left-hand side must give 0.

Root

\(x = k\) is a root of \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) if and only if \(ak^2 + bk + c = 0\).

A quadratic equation can have at most two real roots. Depending on the coefficients it has:

  • two distinct real roots, or
  • two equal real roots (one distinct value repeated), or
  • no real roots.

Which of these happens is decided by the discriminant, introduced in Section 10.

7. Verifying a Root

To check whether \(x = k\) is a root, substitute \(k\) into the left-hand side of the standard form. If the result is 0, it is a root.

1Write the equation in standard form, \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\).
2Substitute \(x = k\): compute \(ak^2 + bk + c\).
3If the value is 0, \(k\) is a root; otherwise it is not.
Worked example

Check whether \(x = -2\) and \(x = 3\) are roots of \(x^2 - x - 6 = 0\).

For \(x = -2\): \((-2)^2 - (-2) - 6 = 4 + 2 - 6 = 0\) ✓

For \(x = 3\): \((3)^2 - 3 - 6 = 9 - 3 - 6 = 0\) ✓

Both values are roots. Indeed \((x + 2)(x - 3) = x^2 - x - 6\).

Note

Verification alone does not prove you have found all roots. It proves the values you tested are correct. Solving the equation systematically shows the full set.

8. Solving by Factorisation

The most reliable first method is factorisation: rewrite the quadratic as a product of two linear factors, then use the zero product rule.

Zero product rule

If \(p \times q = 0\), then \(p = 0\) or \(q = 0\) (or both).

1Write the equation in standard form: \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\).
2Find two numbers \(m\) and \(n\) such that \(m + n = b\) and \(m \times n = a \times c\).
3Split the middle term: \(ax^2 + mx + nx + c = 0\).
4Group and take out common factors to write \((px + q)(rx + s) = 0\).
5Apply the zero product rule and solve the two linear equations.
Worked example (basic)

Solve \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\).

Step 1: Standard form done: \(a = 1\), \(b = -5\), \(c = 6\).

Step 2: Need \(m + n = -5\), \(mn = 6 \implies m = -2, n = -3\).

Step 3–4: \(x^2 - 2x - 3x + 6 = x(x - 2) - 3(x - 2) = (x - 2)(x - 3)\).

Step 5: \((x - 2)(x - 3) = 0 \implies x = 2\) or \(x = 3\).

Verify: \(4 - 10 + 6 = 0\) ✓ and \(9 - 15 + 6 = 0\) ✓. So the roots are \(x = 2\) and \(x = 3\).

Worked example (moderate)

Solve \(2x^2 + 7x + 3 = 0\).

Step 2: \(a \times c = 2 \times 3 = 6\), and \(b = 7\). Find \(m + n = 7\), \(mn = 6 \implies m = 1, n = 6\).

Step 3–4: \(2x^2 + x + 6x + 3 = x(2x + 1) + 3(2x + 1) = (2x + 1)(x + 3)\).

Step 5: \((2x + 1)(x + 3) = 0 \implies x = -\dfrac{1}{2}\) or \(x = -3\).

Verify: \(2\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) - \frac{7}{2} + 3 = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{7}{2} + 3 = 0\) ✓; \(18 - 21 + 3 = 0\) ✓.

Worked example (rearrange first)

Solve \(x(x - 3) = 10\).

Step 1: \(x^2 - 3x - 10 = 0\), so \(a = 1, b = -3, c = -10\).

Step 2: \(m + n = -3\), \(mn = -10 \implies m = -5, n = 2\).

Step 3–4: \(x^2 - 5x + 2x - 10 = x(x - 5) + 2(x - 5) = (x - 5)(x + 2)\).

Step 5: \(x = 5\) or \(x = -2\).

Verify: \(5(2) = 10\) ✓; \((-2)(-5) = 10\) ✓.

When does factorisation work best?

It works quickly when the two numbers \(m\) and \(n\) are easy to spot, i.e. when the roots are rational. When the numbers are not obvious, switch to the quadratic formula.

9. Solving with the Quadratic Formula

Every quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) (with \(a \neq 0\)) can be solved by the quadratic formula:

Quadratic formula

\[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\]

1Write in standard form and identify \(a\), \(b\), \(c\).
2Compute the discriminant \(D = b^2 - 4ac\).
3If \(D \geq 0\), substitute into the formula and simplify the two roots.
4If \(D < 0\), state that there are no real roots.
Worked example

Solve \(x^2 - 4x - 5 = 0\).

Here \(a = 1\), \(b = -4\), \(c = -5\). Discriminant \(D = (-4)^2 - 4(1)(-5) = 16 + 20 = 36\).

\[x = \frac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{36}}{2(1)} = \frac{4 \pm 6}{2}\]

So \(x = \dfrac{4 + 6}{2} = 5\) and \(x = \dfrac{4 - 6}{2} = -1\).

Verify: \(25 - 20 - 5 = 0\) ✓; \(1 + 4 - 5 = 0\) ✓.

Worked example (rational but factorisation hidden)

Solve \(2x^2 - 5x + 2 = 0\). Here \(a = 2\), \(b = -5\), \(c = 2\).

\(D = 25 - 16 = 9\).

\[x = \frac{5 \pm 3}{4}\]

So \(x = \dfrac{5 + 3}{4} = 2\) and \(x = \dfrac{5 - 3}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}\).

Worked example (irrational roots)

Solve \(x^2 + 6x + 6 = 0\). Here \(a = 1\), \(b = 6\), \(c = 6\).

\(D = 36 - 24 = 12\).

\[x = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{12}}{2} = \frac{-6 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = -3 \pm \sqrt{3}\]

So the two roots are \(-3 + \sqrt{3}\) and \(-3 - \sqrt{3}\).

Warning

Do not drop the \(\pm\) symbol, and do not forget the factor \(2a\) in the denominator. Write \(b\) with its sign: if \(b = -4\), then \(-b = 4\).

10. The Discriminant

The expression under the square root in the formula has its own name and role.

Discriminant

For \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), the discriminant is \(D = b^2 - 4ac\).

Without solving the equation, the discriminant tells you what kind of roots to expect:

DiscriminantNature of roots
\(D > 0\)Two distinct real roots
\(D = 0\)Two equal real roots (one real root, repeated)
\(D < 0\)No real roots
Worked example (\(D > 0\))

\(2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0\): \(D = 49 - 24 = 25 > 0\), so there are two distinct real roots. Indeed \(x = \dfrac{7 \pm 5}{4} = 3, \dfrac{1}{2}\).

Worked example (\(D = 0\))

\(x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0\): \(D = 36 - 36 = 0\), so there are two equal real roots: \(x = \dfrac{6}{2} = 3\) (repeated). Note \(x^2 - 6x + 9 = (x - 3)^2\).

Worked example (\(D < 0\))

\(3x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0\): \(D = 4 - 60 = -56 < 0\), so the equation has no real roots.

Worked example (parameter via discriminant)

For what value of \(k\) does \(x^2 + 2x + k = 0\) have equal roots?

Equal roots \(\iff D = 0\), i.e. \(4 - 4k = 0 \implies k = 1\).

Reasoning

Suppose two equations have the same coefficients except one has \(c = 2\) and the other \(c = -2\). The one with \(c = -2\) has \(D = b^2 + 8 > 0\), so it always has two distinct real roots. You can answer such questions purely from the sign of \(D\).

The table below summarises everything about the nature of roots in one place.

Tip

When a standard-form equation is a perfect square like \((x - 3)^2\), the two roots coincide. Exam questions sometimes word this as "equal roots" or "one root repeated twice".

12. Unknown Parameters \(k\), \(p\), \(m\)

A parameter is an unknown number inside the coefficients. The discriminant links it to the nature of roots, and substitution links it to a given root.

Worked example (a root is given)

If \(x = 2\) is a root of \(x^2 + px - 6 = 0\), find \(p\).

Substitute \(x = 2\): \(4 + 2p - 6 = 0 \implies 2p = 2 \implies p = 1\).

So the equation is \(x^2 + x - 6 = 0 = (x + 3)(x - 2)\), confirming 2 is a root and giving the other root \(-3\).

Worked example (equal roots condition)

For which \(k\) does \(kx^2 + 6x + 1 = 0\) have equal roots?

Equal roots \(\iff D = 0\): \(36 - 4k = 0 \implies k = 9\). The equation is \(9x^2 + 6x + 1 = (3x + 1)^2\), root \(x = -\dfrac{1}{3}\) (repeated).

Worked example (two distinct real roots)

For which \(p\) does \(px^2 - 2x + 1 = 0\) have two distinct real roots?

Need \(a \neq 0\) and \(D > 0\): \(4 - 4p > 0 \implies p < 1\), and \(p \neq 0\). So \(p < 1\), \(p \neq 0\).

Worked example (no real roots)

For which \(m\) does \(2x^2 + mx + 8 = 0\) have no real roots?

Need \(D < 0\): \(m^2 - 64 < 0 \implies m^2 < 64 \implies -8 < m < 8\).

13. Application Word Problems

Word problems ask you to frame the equation first. The 4-step plan below works for every such problem.

1Choose a variable and state what it means (e.g. "let breadth = \(x\) m").
2Translate the words into a quadratic equation using the given relation.
3Solve by factorisation or the formula.
4Discard the root that does not make sense in the situation (negative length, time, count) and verify the remaining root.
Application 1 — Consecutive integers

The product of two consecutive positive integers is 306. Let the integers be \(x\) and \(x + 1\). Then \(x(x+1) = 306\), i.e. \(x^2 + x - 306 = 0\). Factorising: \((x + 18)(x - 17) = 0 \implies x = 17\) (we reject \(-18\) as negative). The integers are 17 and 18. Check: \(17 \times 18 = 306\) ✓.

Application 2 — Rectangular garden

A garden is 4 m longer than it is wide. Let breadth be \(x\) m; length is \(x + 4\) m. Area \(= x(x+4) = 96\), so \(x^2 + 4x - 96 = 0\). Factorising: \((x + 12)(x - 8) = 0 \implies x = 8\) (reject \(-12\)). The garden is 8 m by 12 m. Check: \(8 \times 12 = 96\) ✓.

Application 3 — Train speed

A train travels 200 km at a uniform speed. If the speed had been 10 km/h more, the journey would have taken 1 hour less. Let the speed be \(x\) km/h. Time at speed \(x\) is \(\dfrac{200}{x}\), at \(x+10\) it is \(\dfrac{200}{x+10}\). Given \(\dfrac{200}{x} - \dfrac{200}{x+10} = 1\). Multiplying by \(x(x+10)\): \(200(x+10) - 200x = x(x+10)\), so \(x^2 + 10x - 2000 = 0\). Factorising: \((x + 50)(x - 40) = 0 \implies x = 40\) km/h (reject \(-50\)). Speed = 40 km/h. Check: \(\frac{200}{40} = 5\) h, \(\frac{200}{50} = 4\) h, difference 1 h ✓.

Application 4 — Right triangle

The hypotenuse of a right triangle is 13 cm and the difference between the other two sides is 7 cm. Let the shorter side be \(x\) cm; the other is \(x + 7\) cm. By Pythagoras: \(x^2 + (x+7)^2 = 13^2\). Expanding: \(x^2 + x^2 + 14x + 49 = 169\), so \(2x^2 + 14x - 120 = 0\), i.e. \(x^2 + 7x - 60 = 0\). Factorising: \((x + 12)(x - 5) = 0 \implies x = 5\). Sides are 5 cm and 12 cm. Check: \(5^2 + 12^2 = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13^2\) ✓.

Application 5 — Height of a ball

A ball thrown upward has height \(h = 25t - 5t^2\) metres after \(t\) seconds. On the ground, \(h = 0\): \(25t - 5t^2 = 0 \implies 5t(5 - t) = 0\). So \(t = 0\) (when thrown) or \(t = 5\). The ball returns to the ground after 5 seconds. Check: \(25(5) - 5(25) = 125 - 125 = 0\) ✓.

Application 6 — Sum of squares

The sum of the squares of two consecutive positive integers is 365. Let them be \(n\) and \(n+1\). Then \(n^2 + (n+1)^2 = 365\), so \(n^2 + n^2 + 2n + 1 = 365\), giving \(2n^2 + 2n - 364 = 0\), i.e. \(n^2 + n - 182 = 0\). Factorising: \((n + 14)(n - 13) = 0 \implies n = 13\). The integers are 13 and 14. Check: \(169 + 196 = 365\) ✓.

Application 7 — Rectangular park

A rectangular park has area 240 m². Its length is 4 m more than twice its breadth. Let breadth be \(b\) m; length is \(2b + 4\) m. Then \(b(2b+4) = 240\), so \(2b^2 + 4b - 240 = 0\), i.e. \(b^2 + 2b - 120 = 0\). Factorising: \((b + 12)(b - 10) = 0 \implies b = 10\). Breadth is 10 m, length \(2(10)+4 = 24\) m. Check: \(10 \times 24 = 240\) ✓.

Application 8 — Perimeter and fencing

A rectangular garden has perimeter 64 m and area 240 m². Let length be \(l\) m and breadth \(b\) m. Perimeter \(2(l+b) = 64 \implies l + b = 32\), and \(lb = 240\). So \(l(32 - l) = 240 \implies 32l - l^2 = 240 \implies l^2 - 32l + 240 = 0\). Factorising: \((l - 20)(l - 12) = 0 \implies l = 20\) (or \(12\)). The garden is 20 m by 12 m. If fencing costs ₹12 per metre, cost \(= 64 \times 12 =\) ₹768. Check: \(2(32) = 64\), \(20 \times 12 = 240\) ✓.

14. Competency and Reasoning Questions

Competency questions test whether you understand why a method works, not just whether you can run it. They often hide a trap or ask for a choice of method.

Recognise the non-quadratic immediately

From the following, pick the equation that is not quadratic: \(x^2 + 2x + 1 = 0\), \((x+1)(x-1) = 0\), \(x(x+3) = x^2 + 7\).
Reasoning. The third: \(x(x+3) = x^2 + 7\) gives \(x^2 + 3x = x^2 + 7\), so \(3x = 7\) — the \(x^2\) terms cancel. Seeing this without expanding wastes ten seconds; expanding still proves it.

Choose the quicker method

Solve \(x^2 - 49 = 0\) in the fastest way.
Reasoning. Differences of squares: \(x^2 - 49 = (x - 7)(x + 7) = 0\), so \(x = \pm 7\). The quadratic formula would also work but is slower here.

Parameter from nature of roots

For what value of \(k\) does \((k-1)x^2 + 2x - 8 = 0\) have two distinct real roots?
Reasoning. Coarse conditions: \((k-1) \neq 0 \implies k \neq 1\), and \(D = 4 + 32(k-1) > 0 \implies 32k - 28 > 0 \implies k > \frac{7}{8}\). Together, \(k > \frac{7}{8}\) with \(k \neq 1\).

Spot the error

A student solves \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\), factors as \((x - 2)(x - 3)\), then writes roots \(-2, -3\). Where is the error?
Reasoning. The factorisation is right, but the roots are wrong. \((x - 2) = 0\) gives \(x = +2\), not \(-2\); \((x - 3) = 0\) gives \(x = +3\). Correct roots: 2 and 3. Substitution confirms: \(4 - 10 + 6 = 0\), \(9 - 15 + 6 = 0\).

Interpret the discriminant

Can \(x^2 + 1 = 0\) be solved in the real numbers?
Reasoning. Rewriting gives \(x^2 = -1\). A square of a real number is never negative, so there is no real solution. Using \(D = 0 - 4 = -4 < 0\) gives the same answer immediately: no real roots.

Find the other root

One root of \(x^2 - 9x + 20 = 0\) is 4. Find the other root without dividing polynomials.
Reasoning. The linear factor for root 4 is \((x - 4)\). Factorising the whole quadratic: \(x^2 - 9x + 20 = (x - 4)(x - 5)\), so the other root is 5. Verify: \(25 - 45 + 20 = 0\) ✓.

15. Case-Based Examples

Case-based questions describe a situation and then ask 2–3 related sub-questions. Answer the sub-questions using the case data only, and show all working.

Case Study 1 — School Ground

A school ground is 12 m longer than it is wide, and its area is 405 m². The school plans to fence the ground all around.

Question 1

Taking the breadth as \(x\) m, form the quadratic equation for the area of the ground.

Question 2

Solve the equation to find the breadth and length of the ground.

Question 3

Find the cost of fencing the ground at ₹45 per metre.

Solution.  (1)  Length \(= x + 12\), area \(= x(x + 12)\). So the equation is \(x^2 + 12x - 405 = 0\).

(2)  Factorising: \((x + 27)(x - 15) = 0\), so \(x = 15\) (reject \(-27\)). Breadth = 15 m, Length = 27 m. Check \(15 \times 27 = 405\) ✓.

(3)  Perimeter \(= 2(15 + 27) = 84\) m. Cost \(= 84 \times 45 =\) ₹3780.

Case Study 2 — Study Tour

A class plans a tour. A coach charges a total of ₹2400 for the trip. If 4 more students join, the cost per student decreases by ₹20.

Question 1

Let the number of students be \(n\). Write the equation relating the cost per student before and after 4 more students join.

Question 2

Find the number of students on the trip.

Question 3

Find the cost per student originally and after the extra students join, and verify the difference.

Solution.  (1)  Original cost per student \(= \dfrac{2400}{n}\); after \(n+4\) students it is \(\dfrac{2400}{n+4}\). Given \(\dfrac{2400}{n} - \dfrac{2400}{n+4} = 20\). Multiplying by \(n(n+4)\): \(2400(n+4) - 2400n = 20n(n+4)\), so \(9600 = 20n^2 + 80n\), i.e. \(n^2 + 4n - 480 = 0\).

(2)  Factorising: \((n + 24)(n - 20) = 0\), so \(n = 20\) (reject \(-24\)). 20 students.

(3)  Original: \(\dfrac{2400}{20} = \) ₹120 each; with 24 students: \(\dfrac{2400}{24} = \) ₹100 each. Difference \(= 20\) ✓.

Case Study 3 — Consecutive Odd Numbers

The product of two consecutive odd positive integers is 323.

Question 1

Let the smaller integer be \(x\). Form the quadratic equation.

Question 2

Find the two integers.

Question 3

Verify by substitution that both integers satisfy the product condition.

Solution.  (1)  Consecutive odd integers are \(x\) and \(x + 2\). Product: \(x(x+2) = 323\), so \(x^2 + 2x - 323 = 0\).

(2)  Factorising: \((x + 19)(x - 17) = 0\), so \(x = 17\) (reject \(-19\)). Integers are 17 and 19.

(3)  \(x = 17\): \(17(19) = 323\) ✓. The factorisation itself guarantees both factors satisfy the equation.

Case Study 4 — Fair Tickets

A fair charges ₹\(x\) per ticket. The number of visitors that evening is \((400 - 2x)\), so the revenue is \(R(x) = x(400 - 2x)\).

Question 1

Express \(R(x)\) in standard form \(ax^2 + bx + c\) and write \(a\), \(b\), \(c\).

Question 2

For which ticket prices is the revenue zero?

Question 3

Find the revenue when the ticket price is ₹50.

Solution.  (1)  \(R(x) = -2x^2 + 400x\), so \(a = -2\), \(b = 400\), \(c = 0\).

(2)  \(x(400 - 2x) = 0 \implies x = 0\) (free entry, no revenue) or \(x = 200\). Revenue is zero at ₹0 and ₹200.

(3)  \(R(50) = 50(400 - 100) = 50 \times 300 = \) ₹15,000.

Case Study 5 — Lawn and Path

A rectangular garden is 40 m by 30 m. A path of uniform width \(x\) m is cut around the inside edge, leaving a lawn of area 600 m² in the middle.

Question 1

Write the dimensions of the lawn in terms of \(x\), and form the quadratic equation.

Question 2

Solve to find the width of the path.

Question 3

The equation has two solutions. Why is one of them rejected?

Solution.  (1)  Lawn dimensions: \((40 - 2x)\) and \((30 - 2x)\). Area: \((40-2x)(30-2x) = 600\). Expanding: \(1200 - 80x - 60x + 4x^2 = 600\), so \(4x^2 - 140x + 600 = 0\), i.e. \(x^2 - 35x + 150 = 0\).

(2)  Factorising: \((x - 30)(x - 5) = 0 \implies x = 5\) (reject \(30\)). Path width = 5 m.

(3)  \(x = 30\) is rejected because a path of 30 m on each side would exceed half of the 30 m breadth, giving a negative lawn dimension. Only \(x = 5\) satisfies the geometry. Check: \((40-10)(30-10) = 30 \times 20 = 600\) ✓.

16. Important Formulae

Standard form

\(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), where \(a \neq 0\).

Quadratic formula

\[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\]

Discriminant

\[D = b^2 - 4ac\]

\(D > 0\): two distinct real roots; \(D = 0\): two equal real roots; \(D < 0\): no real roots.

Warning

Identify \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) correctly before applying the formula. Write the equation in standard form first, keep the signs of \(b\) and \(c\), and never drop the \(2a\) in the denominator.

17. Common Examination Mistakes

Common mistakeCorrect method
Not writing the equation in standard form before reading \(a\), \(b\), \(c\)Simplify to \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) first, then read the coefficients with their signs
Stating \(a = 0\) or ignoring that conditionIf \(a = 0\), the equation is linear, not quadratic
Dropping the \(\pm\) sign in the formulaAlways compute both roots: \(x = \dfrac{-b + \sqrt{D}}{2a}\) and \(x = \dfrac{-b - \sqrt{D}}{2a}\)
Wrong sign substitution, e.g. using \(b = -4\) as \(b = +4\)Substitute with signs: \(-b\) means the negative of \(b\), so \(b = -4\) gives \(-b = 4\)
Splitting the middle term using product \(c\) instead of \(ac\)For \(ax^2 + bx + c\), find two numbers with product \(ac\) and sum \(b\)
Saying "no solution" for \(x^2 - 9 = 0\)It has two real roots: \(x = \pm 3\). \(D \geq 0\) means real roots exist
Keeping a negative root as the answer in a word problemDiscard roots that make no sense (negative length, time, count), but state why you reject them
Forgetting to verify the found rootsSubstitute each root back into the equation and check it gives 0
Confusing "equal roots" with "no roots"Equal roots still exist (they are real); write the repeated root once or twice as asked
Not simplifying, e.g. leaving \(\dfrac{-6 \pm \sqrt{48}}{2}\)Simplify radicals: \(\sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt{3}\), then reduce the fraction
Worked pitfall

For \(2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0\), the middle term split needs numbers with product \(2 \times 3 = 6\) and sum \(-7\), so they are \(-1\) and \(-6\): \(2x^2 - x - 6x + 3 = (2x - 1)(x - 3)\). Correct roots: \(x = \dfrac{1}{2}\) and \(x = 3\).

18. CBSE Exam Tips

  • Read whether the question wants real roots, integer roots or roots "as decimals" — this decides how you present the answer.
  • Prefer factorisation when the numbers split easily; otherwise go straight to the quadratic formula. Do not waste time hunting for factors when \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) are large.
  • Write the standard form before touching the formula, and show the substitution step — it carries marks even if the arithmetic slips.
  • Compute the discriminant \(D\) before solving; it tells you whether roots exist and whether to expect a neat or radical form.
  • In word problems, state what your variable represents (e.g. "let breadth = \(x\) m") and label the final answer with units.
  • Always reject and explain impossible roots such as negative lengths, times or counts.
  • Verify every root by substitution if time permits — it catches most sign errors.
  • For case-based sections, read the whole case once before answering any sub-question.

A1.

\(x^2 + 5x + 7 = 0\), with \(a = 1\), \(b = 5\), \(c = 7\).

A2.

\((x+1)^2 = x^2 + 3\) is not quadratic: expanding gives \(x^2 + 2x + 1 = x^2 + 3 \Rightarrow 2x = 2\), a linear equation.

A3.

If \(a = 0\), the \(x^2\) term disappears and the equation becomes linear (\(bx + c = 0\)), so it is no longer quadratic.

A4.

It tells whether the roots are real and whether they are equal: \(D > 0\) two distinct real, \(D = 0\) two equal real, \(D < 0\) no real roots.

A5.

No. \(D = 0 - 16 = -16 < 0\), so there are no real roots.

A6.

For \(x = 2\): \(4 - 10 + 6 = 0\) ✓. For \(x = 3\): \(9 - 15 + 6 = 0\) ✓.

A7.

\((x-3)(x-4) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 3\) or \(x = 4\).

A8.

\((x+2)(x+4) = 0 \Rightarrow x = -2\) or \(x = -4\).

A9.

\((x-2)(x+2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm 2\).

A10.

\((x+2)(x+5) = 0 \Rightarrow x = -2\) or \(x = -5\).

A11.

\((3x-2)(2x+3) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{2}{3}\) or \(x = -\dfrac{3}{2}\).

A12.

\(2(x-2)^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 2\) (repeated).

A13.

\((2x-1)^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{1}{2}\) (repeated).

A14.

\((x+2)(x+3) = 0 \Rightarrow x = -2\) or \(x = -3\).

A15.

\((3x-4)(3x+4) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm \dfrac{4}{3}\).

A16.

\(D = 9 + 16 = 25\); \(x = \dfrac{3 \pm 5}{2} \Rightarrow x = 4\) or \(x = -1\).

A17.

\((2x-3)(x+2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{3}{2}\) or \(x = -2\).

A18.

\(D = 36 - 36 = 0\): two equal real roots. \((x+3)^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = -3\) (repeated).

A19.

\((3x-2)(x-1) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{2}{3}\) or \(x = 1\).

A20.

\((2x-1)(x-3) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{1}{2}\) or \(x = 3\).

A21.

\(x(x+1) = 240 \Rightarrow x^2 + x - 240 = 0 \Rightarrow (x+16)(x-15) = 0\). Positive \(x = 15\); the integers are 15 and 16.

A22.

\(x(x+3) = 108 \Rightarrow x^2 + 3x - 108 = 0 \Rightarrow (x+12)(x-9) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 9\). Dimensions: breadth 9 m, length 12 m.

A23.

\(20t - 5t^2 = 0 \Rightarrow 5t(4-t) = 0 \Rightarrow t = 0\) or \(t = 4\). It returns to the ground after 4 seconds.

A24.

\(n^2 + (n+1)^2 = 61 \Rightarrow n^2 + n - 30 = 0 \Rightarrow (n+6)(n-5) = 0 \Rightarrow n = 5\). The integers are 5 and 6.

A25.

\(\dfrac{180}{x} - \dfrac{180}{x+15} = 1 \Rightarrow x^2 + 15x - 2700 = 0 \Rightarrow (x+60)(x-45) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 45\). Speed = 45 km/h.

A26.

Incorrect. \((x-3)(x-4) = 0\) gives \(x = +3\) and \(x = +4\). The claimed roots \(-3, -4\) would make the product of factors positive and the signs of the middle terms flip.

A27.

Equal roots \(\iff D = 0\): \(4 - 4k = 0 \Rightarrow k = 1\).

A28.

Two distinct real roots \(\iff D > 0\): \(4 - 4c > 0 \Rightarrow c < 1\), and the equation is quadratic for any real \(c\).

A29.

(i) \(x(x+4) = 96\), i.e. \(x^2 + 4x - 96 = 0\). (ii) \((x+12)(x-8) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 8\): breadth 8 m, length 12 m. (iii) Perimeter \(= 2(8+12) = 40\) m; cost \(= 40 \times 25 =\) ₹1000.

A30.

(i) \(R = -3x^2 + 300x\), so \(a = -3\), \(b = 300\), \(c = 0\). (ii) \(x(300 - 3x) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0\) or \(x = 100\). (iii) \(R(20) = 20(300 - 60) = 20 \times 240 =\) ₹4800.
