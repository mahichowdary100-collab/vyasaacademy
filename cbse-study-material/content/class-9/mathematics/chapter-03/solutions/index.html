Chapter 3 overview

This chapter follows natural numbers, zero and integers, rational numbers, irrational numbers, real numbers and decimal patterns. A recurring idea is to use definitions and properties to explain why a result must hold.

How to use these solutions: Try each exercise first, then compare your reasoning. Check signs, denominator restrictions and whether a decimal terminates or repeats.

Reference: Chapter 3, Exercise Set 3.1, Questions 1–4. Prompts below are original paraphrases.

Question 1 · Trading a ratio

Use the stated spice-bag to copper-ingot exchange rate to find the ingots received for 12 bags.

Two bags correspond to 15 ingots; 12 bags are six groups of two. Thus \(6\times15=90\).

Answer: 90 copper ingots.

Question 2 · Prime-number pattern

Identify what 11, 13, 17 and 19 share, then give the next three numbers of that kind.

Each listed number has exactly two positive factors, 1 and itself. The next primes after 19 are 23, 29 and 31.

Answer: They are primes; the next three are 23, 29 and 31.

Question 3 · Closure under subtraction

Decide whether natural numbers are closed under subtraction and support the conclusion with examples.

A set is closed only if every allowed pair gives a result in the set. \(8-3=5\) is natural, but \(3-8=-5\) is not.

Answer: Natural numbers are not closed under subtraction.

Count the joints on the four fingers and connect the count to a base-12 grouping.

The thumb is a pointer. The other four fingers have three joints each: \(4\times3=12\).

Question 4 · Subtracting a debt

Explain why taking away a negative amount has the effect of adding a positive amount.

If a ledger has ₹10 and a debt of ₹5 is removed, the balance rises by ₹5: \(10-(-5)=10+5=15\).

Answer: Subtracting a debt removes a negative amount, increasing the balance.

Question 2 · Proving √5 is irrational

Prove by contradiction that \(\sqrt5\) cannot be a ratio of integers.

  1. Assume \(\sqrt5=p/q\) in lowest terms, with \(q\ne0\).
  2. Then \(p^2=5q^2\), so 5 divides \(p\). Write \(p=5k\).
  3. Substitution gives \(q^2=5k^2\), so 5 divides \(q\) too.
  4. This contradicts that \(p/q\) was in lowest terms.

Answer: \(\sqrt5\) is irrational.

Question 3 · Repeating decimals as fractions

Convert each listed decimal into a fraction, using the printed repeating block.

  • (i) \(12.6=126/10=63/5\).
  • (ii) \(0.0120=120/10000=3/250\).
  • (iii) \(3.0\overline{52}\): let \(x=3.052525\ldots\). \(1000x-10x=3022\), so \(x=1511/495\).
  • (iv) \(1.2\overline{35}=1+(235-2)/(10\times99)=1223/990\).
  • (v) \(0.\overline{23}=23/99\).
  • (vi) \(2.0\overline5=2+5/90=37/18\).
  • (vii) \(2.12\overline5=2+(125-12)/(100\times9)=1913/900\).
  • (viii) \(3.12\overline5=3+113/900=2813/900\).
  • (ix) \(2.16\overline{25}=2+(1625-16)/(100\times99)=21409/9900\).

Method: Subtract the number before the repetend from the number through one full repetend. Use one 9 per repeating digit and one 0 per non-repeating decimal digit.

Answers: \(63/5,3/250,1511/495,1223/990,23/99,37/18,1913/900,2813/900,21409/9900\).

Question 10 · Decimal place and reduced denominator

Relate a fourth-place last non-zero decimal digit to the reduced denominator.

Write the decimal as \(p/10^4=p/(2^4 5^4)\), where \(p\) is not divisible by 10. If \(p\) is odd, no factor 2 cancels, so \(2^4\) remains. If 5 does not divide \(p\), \(5^4\) remains. Since \(p\) is not divisible by both 2 and 5, one full prime-power block survives.

Answer: Yes; the reduced denominator is divisible by \(2^4\) or \(5^4\), possibly both.

Question 13 · Five rationals with a common denominator Starred challenge

Answer: Denominator 36 gives five valid fractions. The printed strict inequality is stronger than needed.

Question 14 · Three rationals with two constraints Starred challenge

Use the zero sum and pairwise-product sum to show \(x,y,z\) all vanish.

\((x+y+z)^2=x^2+y^2+z^2+2(xy+yz+zx)=0\). The pairwise sum is zero, so \(x^2+y^2+z^2=0\). Each square is non-negative; therefore every square is zero.

Answer: \(x=y=z=0\).

Common errors and exam tips

  • For addition or subtraction, make denominators equal before combining numerators; do not add denominators.
  • For division by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal; keep the divisor non-zero.
  • When subtracting a negative, rewrite it as addition of its positive opposite.
  • A rational in lowest terms terminates only when its denominator has no prime factors except 2 and 5.
  • In repeating decimals, mark the complete repetend and align it before subtracting.
  • For an irrationality proof, state the lowest-terms assumption and derive the contradiction for both numerator and denominator.
  • On a number line, compare values first and label the scale.
Exam tip: Show the denominator factorisation or common-denominator step; a final decimal alone may not explain why it terminates or repeats.
