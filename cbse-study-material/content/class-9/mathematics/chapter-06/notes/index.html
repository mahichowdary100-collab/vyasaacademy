Class 9 Maths Chapter 6 – Measuring Space: Perimeter and Area

These notes follow Chapter 6 of the NCERT book Ganita Manjari (Grade 9, Part I). You will begin with a real puzzle — the staggered starting lines of a 4×100 m relay race — and discover that to solve it we must first answer a deceptively simple question: what is the perimeter of a circle? From there you will follow the C/D ratio, meet the constant \(\pi\) (with its long and glorious history), measure arcs and areas, verify Heron’s formula and Brahmagupta’s formula, and reconstruct Baudhāyana’s ancient construction for squaring a rectangle.

1. The 4 × 100 m Relay: Why Do Outer Lanes Start Ahead?

Look at the photograph of athletes assembled at the start of a 4×100 m relay race. The tracks are laid out on the field, and every runner is ready to sprint — but the athletes are not standing in one straight line. Those in the outer lanes seem to be starting ahead of those in the inner lanes, while the finish line is the same for all of them. What could be the reason for this?

Each runner must cover exactly the same total distance. Over the two straight sections of the track this is automatic, because a straight stretch has the same length in every lane. But the track also has two curved (semicircular) bends, and a runner in an outer lane travels around a bend of larger radius. A larger circle has a larger circumference, so the outer-lane runner covers more distance around the bends. To compensate, the outer lanes are given a small head start.

Key Term

Stagger: the distance between the starting points of two adjacent lanes in a race on a curved track. It continues, growing, all the way to the outermost lane. It exists so that the extra curved distance in an outer lane is exactly compensated.

Do you think the stagger gives anyone (outer lanes or inner lanes) an unfair advantage? It should not — every racer, in every lane, runs the same total distance. On what basis can the organisers work out the length of the stagger between lanes? To answer this we must know how to find the length around a circle. That is exactly the mathematics of this chapter!

Think and Reflect

In my school, the playground is too small to have a 400 m track, so the school constructed a 200 m track instead. Does this mean that we need a smaller stagger for the race tracks in my school (i.e., smaller than the stagger used in the Olympics), for the same 4×100 m relay race?

Yes. The stagger comes only from the curved parts of the track. On a shorter track the bends have a smaller radius, so the extra curved distance from one lane to the next is smaller — and so the stagger is smaller.

2. Perimeter of a Shape

Definition

Perimeter: the total length around the border of a shape. Imagine a tiny insect walking around the border, never turning around, until it returns to its starting point. The perimeter is the total distance the insect travels.

Let us recall the perimeter formulas you already know, and notice a beautiful pattern: the ratio of the perimeter to the side stays fixed for a family of similar shapes.

Perimeter Formulas

  • Square of side \(a\): \(P = 4a\)
  • Rectangle of length \(a\) and width \(b\): \(P = 2(a+b)\)
  • Equilateral triangle of side \(a\): \(P = 3a\)
  • Circle of radius \(r\): its perimeter is called the circumference, \(C = 2\pi r\) (we will derive this soon).

The formula for the perimeter of a square is a special case of the formula for the perimeter of a rectangle with \(a = b\): indeed \(P = 2(a+a) = 4a\). You will see this idea of a “special case of a general formula” repeated throughout mathematics — and it appears again at the end of this chapter when Heron’s formula emerges as a special case of Brahmagupta’s formula.

If we double the side of a square, its perimeter also doubles; for every square the ratio of perimeter to side is \(4:1\). For every equilateral triangle the ratio of perimeter to side is \(3:1\). This fixed ratio is a first glimpse of the same idea we will meet for circles: every circle has the same ratio of circumference to diameter, and that constant ratio is called \(\pi\).

Solved Example 1 · Fencing a rectangular field

Question: A rectangular field is 24 m long and 16 m wide. A farmer wants to put a fence all round it. What length of fencing is needed?

Concept: The fencing length is the perimeter of the rectangle. Formula: \(P = 2(a+b)\).

Solution: With \(a = 24\) m and \(b = 16\) m: \(P = 2(24 + 16) = 2 \times 40 = 80\) m.

Answer: 80 m (of fencing).

Solved Example 2 · Perimeter of an equilateral triangle

Question: The side of an equilateral triangle is 12 cm. Find its perimeter.

Concept: All three sides are equal. Formula: \(P = 3a\).

Solution: \(P = 3 \times 12 = 36\) cm.

Answer: 36 cm.

Perimeter is always measured in units of length (cm, m, km). Compare this with area, which is measured in square units (cm², m²) — we will stress this contrast later in the chapter.

3. Perimeter of a Circle — The C/D Ratio

Here is a circle with radius \(r\) units. What is its perimeter? If we simply call it the circumference, we have only given it a name — we still need a formula to compute it. In Question 1 we saw that a square’s perimeter is always \(4\) times its side; the same trick works for circles: find the ratio of the circumference \(C\) to the diameter \(D\).

The C/D Ratio

People realised in ancient times that the ratio of the circumference \(C\) to the diameter \(D\) of a circle does not change when we change the size of the circle. This constant is now called \(\pi\):

\(\dfrac{C}{D} = \pi\)   so   \(C = \pi d\)   and since \(d = 2r\),   \(C = 2\pi r\)

Home Measurement Activity

You can estimate the C/D ratio at home. Take a cotton reel with a thin thread. Measure the diameter \(D\) of the reel as accurately as you can. Unwrap the thread, wrap it tightly around the reel 20 times, unwrap again and measure the length \(L\). Then compute \(L \div (20 \times D)\). This is an estimate of the C/D ratio.

Do the experiment! Do you get a ratio between 3 and 4? Between 3.1 and 3.2? Remember: measurement always gives an approximation, not an exact value. It is also possible to estimate the C/D ratio using pure geometry — for example, by trapping the circumference between the perimeters of an inscribed hexagon and a circumscribed hexagon.

Solved Example 3 · Circumference of a circle

Question: Find the circumference of a circle of radius 7 cm. (Use \(\pi \approx 22/7\).)

Concept: The perimeter of a circle is its circumference. Formula: \(C = 2\pi r\).

Solution: \(C = 2 \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 7 = 44\) cm.

Answer: 44 cm.

Solved Example 4 · Radius from the circumference

Question: The perimeter of a circle is 44 cm. What is its radius?

Concept: Use \(C = 2\pi r\) and solve for \(r\).

Solution: \(44 = 2 \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times r\)  ⇒  \(r = 44 \times \dfrac{7}{44} = 7\) cm.

Answer: 7 cm.

4. The Story of \(\pi\)

We say “pi” as in “pie”, not as in “pizza”. This one constant, \(\pi\), is a bridge between many areas of mathematics — it connects the straight-edged world of polygons with the infinite curves of nature. Long before anyone called it \(\pi\), many civilisations had studied the C/D ratio. Here is the adventurous journey described in Ganita Manjari.

Time & PlaceMathematicianContribution to \(\pi\)
c. 1900 BCE, Mesopotamia—Realised a circle’s perimeter is slightly larger than that of the hexagon inscribed in it, so \(\pi > 3\); used \(\pi = 3 + \frac{1}{8} = 3.125\).
c. 250 BCE, SyracuseArchimedesTrapped \(\pi\) between the perimeters of inscribed and circumscribed polygons. Working up to 96-sided polygons he found \(3\,\frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\,\frac{1}{7}\).
c. 150 CE, AlexandriaPtolemyRefined Archimedes’ computations for his astronomical tables: \(\pi \approx \frac{377}{120} \approx 3.14167\).
263 CE, ChinaLiu HuiDeveloped a “circle-cutting” method that laid the groundwork for the next breakthrough.
480 CE, ChinaZu ChongzhiPushed the polygon method to 24,576 sides; gave the Yuelü \(\frac{22}{7} \approx 3.1428\) and the Miü \(\frac{355}{113} \approx 3.1415929\). No fraction with denominator below 15,000 is closer to \(\pi\).
499 CE, IndiaĀryabhaṭaGave \(\frac{62832}{20000} = 3.1416\), and crucially called it asanna (“approaching”) — suggesting the ratio cannot be given exactly as one fraction.
628 CE, IndiaBrahmaguptaSuggested \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.1622\) — slightly less accurate, but elegant and easy to manipulate; it dominated the Arab world and medieval Europe for centuries.
c. 1400, IndiaMādhava of SangamagrāmaChanged the game forever: \(\pi\) is not just a number to be approximated, but a limit. Mādhava discovered the first exact formula (an infinite series) and computed \(\pi\) to 11 decimal places: 3.14159265358.
Later breakthroughsNīlakaṇṭha (c. 1500), Machin (1706), Ramanujan (1914), Chudnovsky brothers (1988)Better and faster infinite series. Today, using such ideas, we know \(\pi\) to trillions of digits.

Mādhava’s Exact Formula

Mādhava of Sangamagrāma (Kerala, c. 1400) discovered that

\(\dfrac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \dfrac{1}{3} + \dfrac{1}{5} - \dfrac{1}{7} + \dots\)

One of the most beautiful formulas in mathematics — a bridge from the geometric cutting of circles to the analytical summing of numbers. By moving to “infinite series”, Mādhava helped birth the area of mathematics now called calculus.

Where did the name \(\pi\) come from? The perimeter of a circle is the boundary length around it, and the Greek word for perimeter is perimetros, which begins with the letter \(\pi\). In 1706 the Welsh mathematician William Jones used the Greek symbol \(\pi\) to denote the C/D ratio; the symbol was made popular by the Swiss-German mathematician Leonhard Euler (say “oiler”). We still use it today.

Fun Fact · Pi Day

“How I wish I could recollect pi.” Count the letters of each word: 3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2 — the first digits of \(\pi\). Since \(\pi \approx 3.14\) and \(\pi \approx 22/7\), 14 March (“3-14”) is celebrated each year as Pi Day, and 22 July (“22-7”) as Pi Approximation Day (in India, where dates are written day-month).

5. \(\pi\) Is Irrational

Recall

A rational number is a number that can be written as a fraction \(\dfrac{a}{b}\), where \(a\), \(b\) are integers and \(b \neq 0\). Examples: \(\dfrac{1}{2},\ \dfrac{3}{1},\ \dfrac{-7}{11},\ 1.4\). A number that is not rational is called irrational.

The digits of \(\pi\) go on forever with no visible pattern. Fractions give decimal expansions with a rhythmic pattern, e.g., \(\frac{1}{3} = 0.33333\dots\), \(\frac{1}{11} = 0.09090909\dots\), \(\frac{1}{7} = 0.142857\ 142857\dots\). But for \(\pi\) there is no such pattern. It turns out that \(\pi\) cannot be written as a ratio of two integers — that is, \(\pi\) is an irrational number.

You already know from Grade 8 that \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational. From their writings it seems that Āryabhaṭa and Zu Chongzhi already regarded \(\pi\) as irrational. Centuries later (1761), the mathematician Lambert showed that \(\pi\) is indeed irrational — but proving it requires more advanced mathematics, which you will learn in later grades. This Grade 9 chapter simply establishes the fact that \(\pi\) is irrational, without giving the full advanced proof.

\(\pi \approx 22/7\) but \(\pi \neq 22/7\)

Since \(\pi\) is irrational, there is no “best fraction” for \(\pi\): if there is any fraction close to \(\pi\), we can find another fraction that is closer. The well-known fraction \(\dfrac{22}{7}\) is good enough for most practical uses, but it is not equal to \(\pi\). So we write \(\pi \approx \dfrac{22}{7}\) and also \(\pi \neq \dfrac{22}{7}\). In the same way \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\) but \(\sqrt{2} \neq 1.414\). A much better approximation is \(\dfrac{355}{113}\) (Zu Chongzhi’s Miü).

Solved Example 5 · Approximating — not equating

Question: A student writes “\(\pi = 22/7\)” in her notebook. Is the statement correct?

Concept: \(\pi\) is irrational; \(22/7\) is rational. Reasoning: They are close but not equal. The correct way to record the idea is \(\pi \approx 22/7\) and \(\pi \neq 22/7\).

Answer: Incorrect statement; write \(\pi \approx 22/7\) instead.

6. Length of an Arc of a Circle

The whole circumference is \(2\pi r\). What part of it is one semicircle or one quarter-circle? Mathematicians use the symmetry of the circle to answer — and then generalise to any arc subtending a given angle at the centre.

Definitions

  • Arc: a part of the circumference of a circle.
  • Semicircle: half of a circle — an arc subtending \(180^\circ\) at the centre.
  • Quarter-circle: an arc subtending \(90^\circ\) at the centre.
  • Central angle: the angle \(\theta^\circ\) that an arc subtends at the centre of the circle.
1

A full turn around the circle is the whole circumference: \(2\pi r\).

2

Reflect the circle in a diameter, or rotate it through a half-turn (\(180^\circ\)). A semicircle must be exactly half the circumference, so its arc length is \(2\pi r \div 2 = \pi r\). This may also be written \(2\pi r \times \frac{180^\circ}{360^\circ}\).

3

Rotate the circle through \(90^\circ\) (a quarter-turn): each quarter-circle covers another quarter-circle, so they have equal length \(2\pi r \div 4 = \frac{\pi r}{2}\). Also written \(2\pi r \times \frac{90^\circ}{360^\circ}\).

4

Generalise: an arc \(AB\) that subtends an angle \(\theta^\circ\) at the centre is \(\theta/360\) of the whole circle.

Arc Length

\(l = 2\pi r \times \dfrac{\theta^\circ}{360^\circ}\)

where \(r\) is the radius and \(\theta^\circ\) is the angle the arc subtends at the centre.

Solved Example 6 · Arc with a 60° central angle

Question: Find the length of an arc of a circle of radius 3.5 cm if the angle at the centre is \(60^\circ\).

Concept: The arc is \(60/360 = 1/6\) of the circle. Formula: \(l = 2\pi r \times \frac{\theta}{360}\).

Solution: \(l = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 3.5 \times \frac{60}{360} = 2 \times 11 \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{22}{6} = \frac{11}{3}\) cm \(\approx 3.67\) cm.

Answer: \(\frac{11}{3}\) cm (about 3.67 cm).

Solved Example 7 · Arc with a 120° central angle

Question: Find the length of an arc of a circle of radius 6.3 m if the angle at the centre is \(120^\circ\).

Concept: \(120^\circ\) is one-third of a full turn. Formula: \(l = 2\pi r \times \frac{\theta}{360}\).

Solution: \(l = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 6.3 \times \frac{120}{360} = 2 \times 19.8 \times \frac{1}{3} = 13.2\) m.

Answer: 13.2 m.

Solved Example 8 · Perimeter of a semicircular field

Question: A semicircular field has diameter 14 m. Find its perimeter (the straight diameter plus the semicircular arc).

Concept: Perimeter of a semicircle = arc + diameter. Formula: arc length \(= \pi r = \pi \times \frac{d}{2}\).

Solution: \(r = 7\) m. Arc \(= \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = 22\) m. Perimeter \(= 22 + 14 = 36\) m.

Answer: 36 m.

7. A Closer Look at a 400 m Athletics Track

The figure below is a schematic diagram of a 400 m athletics track, exactly as described in Ganita Manjari. It has two straight sections of length 84.39 m each, and two curved portions that are semicircles with a common centre; the inner semicircle on each side has radius 36.5 m. The width of each lane is 1.22 m.

Now imagine an athlete running one complete circuit of the track. Where exactly does she run? A runner cannot hug the lane border itself, so let us assume she runs at a distance of 0.3 m from the inner border of her lane.

Solved Example 9 · One lap of the innermost lane

Question: How far does an athlete run, in one complete circuit of the innermost lane, if she runs 0.3 m from the inner border?

Concept: Two straights + two semicircles that together make one full circle. Formula: \(C = 2\pi r\).

Solution:
Straights: \(2 \times 84.39 = 168.78\) m.
Bends: the running circle has radius \(36.5 + 0.3 = 36.8\) m, and its circumference is \(2 \times 3.1416 \times 36.8 = 231.22\) m.
Total: \(168.78 + 231.22 = 400.00\) m.

Answer: 400 m — the full lap matches the track length exactly.

Solved Example 10 · The stagger between two lanes

Question: The lane width is 1.22 m. Find the stagger needed by the runner in the second lane compared with the innermost lane.

Concept: On the straights all runners run the same distance; the difference is only in the curved parts. Two semicircles make one full circle, so the extra distance is the difference of two circumferences.

Solution: The two circles have radii separated by one lane width: \(r_2 - r_1 = 1.22\) m. Extra distance \(= 2\pi (r_2 - r_1) = 2 \times 3.1416 \times 1.22 = 7.6655 \approx 7.67\) m.

Answer: About 7.67 m of stagger between the first and second lanes.

Think and Reflect

Will an equal stagger be needed between the third and second lanes?

Yes. The lane width (1.22 m) is the same between every pair of adjacent lanes, and the stagger depends only on the difference in running-circle radii. So the stagger is the same, about 7.67 m, between every adjacent pair of lanes.

8. Problems, Puzzles and Paradoxes on Perimeter

Perimeter behaves in wonderfully surprising ways. Here are two classic problems from the chapter — reason through them before you read the solutions!

Solved Example 11 · Two circles through each other’s centres

Question: Two circles of equal radius are drawn so that each circle passes through the centre of the other circle. If the radius of each circle is \(r\) units, find the perimeter of the shape formed by the two circles (the two red arcs), ignoring the dotted portions that lie within the circles.

Concept: The centres \(A\) and \(B\) are one radius apart, so triangle \(ABC\) (and \(ABD\)) is equilateral.

Solution: Since \(AB = AC = BC = r\), triangle \(ABC\) is equilateral, so \(\angle CAB = 60^\circ\) and \(\angle CBA = 60^\circ\). Similarly \(\angle BAD = 60^\circ = \angle ABD\). Hence \(\angle CAD = 120^\circ = \angle CBD\). Each dotted arc is therefore \(120/360 = 1/3\) of its circle’s circumference, so each red arc is the remaining \(2/3\) of a circumference. Total red arc length:

\(2 \times \dfrac{2}{3} \times 2\pi r = \dfrac{8\pi r}{3}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{8\pi r}{3}\) units.

Solved Example 12 · One semicircle or three semicircles?

Question: Points \(P\) and \(Q\) are joined by two paths. The first path is a single semicircle of radius \(a'\). The second path is three semicircles of radii \(b', c', d'\) drawn on the same line \(PQ\). Which path is longer?

Concept: Length of a semicircle of radius \(R\) is \(\pi R\). Compare \(\pi a'\) with \(\pi(b' + c' + d')\).

Solution: The diameter \(PQ = 2a'\) equals the sum of the three diameters: \(2a' = 2b' + 2c' + 2d'\). Dividing by 2 gives \(a' = b' + c' + d'\). So the two path lengths \(\pi a'\) and \(\pi(b' + c' + d')\) are equal.

Answer: The two paths have equal length.

Why does this happen? The length of a semicircle depends only on its diameter (length \(=\frac{\pi d}{2}\)). If the small semicircles gobble up the whole diameter \(PQ\) in pieces, the total of their diameters is exactly \(PQ\) — so the total of their arcs equals the arc of the single semicircle, no matter how the pieces are arranged.

Solved Example 13 · Four petals on a square

Question: Four petals are drawn on the sides of a square of side 14 cm, such that the centres of the arcs are the midpoints of the sides of the square. Find the total perimeter of all the petals.

Concept: Each side of the square has length 14 cm, so the arc-centres sit at the midpoints of the sides and every arc has radius \(14 \div 2 = 7\) cm. Each petal is bounded by two arcs: one from a side-midpoint circle and one from the neighbouring side-midpoint circle.

Solution: Consider the two circles centred at the midpoints of two adjacent sides; they meet at the shared corner of the square and at the centre of the square. The chord joining these two meeting points has length \(\sqrt{7^2+7^2} = 7\sqrt{2}\) cm. For a circle of radius \(7\), a chord of length \(7\sqrt{2}\) subtends \(90^\circ\) at the centre (using \(2r\sin(\theta/2)\)). So each arc is a quarter-circle: length \(= \frac{\pi r}{2} = \frac{22}{7} \times \frac{7}{2} = 11\) cm. Each petal has two such arcs (22 cm), giving \(4 \times 22 = 88\) cm.

Answer: 88 cm.

Try this favourite puzzle

A big semicircle of diameter 24 cm is replaced by three smaller semicircles of diameters 6, 8 and 10 cm sharing the same line. Show that the total length of the three smaller arcs equals the length of the big arc.

Total small-arc length \(= \frac{\pi}{2}(6+8+10) = \frac{\pi}{2}(24) = 12\pi\) cm; big-arc length \(= \frac{\pi}{2}(24) = 12\pi\) cm. Equal!

9. Area of a Rectangle

From perimeter we move to area: the amount of space occupied by a two-dimensional region in a plane. Measurement is always relative to a unit. For length the unit is, say, a centimetre; for area the unit is a \(1 \times 1\) square, whose area is taken to be \(1\) sq. unit (written \(1\) unit²).

Definitions

  • Area: the amount of space occupied by a plane figure, counted in unit squares.
  • Area of a square of side \(a\): \(A = a^2\)
  • Area of a rectangle with sides \(a\), \(b\): \(A = ab\)

Why is multiplication the right operation? If a rectangle is exactly laid out with unit squares, a rectangle of sides \(a\) and \(b\) fits \(a\) unit squares along one side and \(b\) unit squares along the other, giving \(a \times b\) unit squares in all. The square is again the special case with \(b = a\).

Solved Example 14 · Floor area

Question: A rectangular room is 6 m long and 4 m wide. Find its area.

Concept: Count unit squares. Formula: \(A = a b\).

Solution: \(A = 6 \times 4 = 24\) m².

Answer: 24 m².

10. Area of a Parallelogram

You know from Grade 8 that the area of a parallelogram is base × height. The key subtlety this year is to understand what “height” means.

Definitions

  • Base: any chosen side of the parallelogram.
  • Height: the length of the perpendicular dropped from a vertex onto the line containing the chosen base.

Slant side ≠ Height

The height is the perpendicular distance, not the slanted side. Only use the slant side as the height if it happens to be perpendicular to the base (as in a rectangle).

Area of a Parallelogram

\(A = \text{base} \times \text{height} = bh\)

Why the formula works (cut-and-shift): Given a parallelogram \(ABCD\), cut off a right triangle at one end and shift it to the other end. The pieces fit together to make a rectangle with the same base \(b\) and the same height \(h\) (see the figure — \(A'B'C'D'\) is a copy of \(ABCD\)). The two shapes are different, but their areas are the same: \(bh\).

The “thin” parallelogram: what if the parallelogram is so thin that the foot of the perpendicular from a vertex does not lie on the side itself? The cut-and-shift construction then seems to fail. Here is the fix from the source: take a point \(D'\) on \(DA\) close to \(D\), and a point \(A'\) on \(DA\) produced, with \(A'A = D'D\). Then \(A'BCD'\) is a parallelogram with the same area as \(ABCD\) (because \(\triangle CDD' \cong \triangle BAA'\)). Repeat this step as many times as needed!

Think and Reflect

The area of a rectangle can be found just from the lengths of its sides. Is the same true for a parallelogram? (Hint: keep the side lengths fixed and change the angle between the sides.)

No. If we change the angle, the height changes, and so does the area — the side lengths alone cannot determine a parallelogram’s area.

Solved Example 15 · Area of a parallelogram

Question: A parallelogram has base 12 cm and perpendicular height 5 cm. Find its area.

Concept: Use the perpendicular height, not any slant side. Formula: \(A = bh\).

Solution: \(A = 12 \times 5 = 60\) cm².

Answer: 60 cm².

11. Area of a Triangle

Area of a Triangle

\(A = \dfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \dfrac{1}{2} bh\)

The height is always perpendicular to the chosen base (or to its extension).

Why it works: Enclose the triangle in a rectangle with base \(b\) and height \(h\). The rectangle is split into two smaller rectangles, and the triangle is exactly half of each, so it is half of the whole rectangle: \(\frac{1}{2} bh\).

The obtuse-angle gap: if the triangle has an obtuse angle, the perpendicular may fall outside the triangle. Then use the extension of the base; the enclosed rectangle still works, at the cost of a small adjustment — you are invited to work out the details.

A nicer derivation: two congruent copies of any triangle can be fitted together (matching equal angles) to make a parallelogram — in fact a rhombus when the triangle is isosceles. Since the parallelogram has base \(b\), height \(h\) and area \(bh\), the single triangle has area \(\frac{1}{2} bh\).

Solved Example 16 · Area of a triangle

Question: A triangle has base 12 cm and height 5 cm. Find its area.

Concept: Half of a rectangle/parallelogram of the same base and height.

Solution: \(A = \frac{1}{2} \times 12 \times 5 = 30\) cm².

Answer: 30 cm².

Solved Example 17 · Area of a right triangle

Question: In a right-angled triangle, the legs are 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the area.

Concept: For a right triangle, one leg is the base and the other is the (perpendicular) height.

Solution: \(A = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 8 = 24\) cm².

Answer: 24 cm².

12. Median and Equal-Area Triangles

From the area formula we reach a simple but beautiful conclusion about the medians of a triangle.

Definition

Median: the line segment joining a vertex of a triangle to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Theorem

A median of a triangle divides it into two triangles with equal area.

Why: In \(\triangle ABC\), let \(AD\) be a median, so \(BD = DC\). Draw the altitude from \(A\) to \(BC\); call its length \(h\). Both \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle ACD\) have height \(h\), and their bases are equal (\(BD = DC\)). So each area is \(\frac{1}{2} \times\) (base) \(\times h\), with equal bases — hence equal areas, each \(\frac{ah}{2}\) sq. units.

Equal area ≠ congruent shape

\(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle ACD\) are, in general, differently shaped — they need not be congruent. Equal area does not mean identical shape.

Can we cut \(\triangle ABD\) into pieces and rearrange them to exactly cover \(\triangle ACD\)? Surprisingly, yes — the chapter invites you to find the (surprisingly small) number of pieces required. Equal-area polygons are always dissectable into one another in this way; try this with a square and a non-square rectangle of equal area, or two differently shaped triangles of equal area, and formulate your own conjecture.

Solved Example 18 · Using the median property

Question: In \(\triangle ABC\), \(\text{area}(\triangle ABC) = 40\) cm², and \(AD\) is a median. Find the areas of \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle ACD\).

Concept: The median splits the triangle into two equal-area triangles.

Solution: Each area \(= \frac{40}{2} = 20\) cm².

Answer: 20 cm² each.

13. Heron’s Formula for the Area of a Triangle

So far, to find a triangle’s area we need a base and a height. But a triangle is completely determined by its three sides alone — so it must be possible to compute the area from the three sides with no height at all. This is exactly what Heron’s formula does. It is named after the Greek mathematician-scientist Heron of Alexandria (c. 62 CE), who worked on mechanics, pneumatics, geometry and geodesy.

1

Compute the semiperimeter: \(s = \dfrac{a + b + c}{2}\) — half of the perimeter. (Calling it \(s\) rather than \(\frac{P}{2}\) shortens every formula below.)

2

Evaluate the product \((s - a)(s - b)(s - c)\) — the three “excess factors”.

3

Take the square root: the area is \(\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\).

Heron’s Formula

\(A = \sqrt{s\,(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)},\quad s = \dfrac{a+b+c}{2}\)

where \(a, b, c\) are the sides of the triangle.

Why Heron’s formula is special

In later grades you will see that Heron’s triangle formula is a special case of a much more general result for any polygon whose vertices lie on a circle (Brahmagupta’s formula, Section 15). In modern mathematics, similar ideas are used, for example, in surveying — where the tribes (as in the Amazon) use the area formula for polygons from perimeter data.

Solved Example 19 · 3-4-5 triangle

Question: Find the area of a triangle whose sides are 3, 4 and 5 units.

Concept: Use Heron’s formula. Steps: \(s = \frac{3+4+5}{2} = 6\). Then \(A = \sqrt{6(6-3)(6-4)(6-5)} = \sqrt{6 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = \sqrt{36} = 6\).

Answer: 6 sq. units (which also equals \(\frac{1}{2} \times 3 \times 4\), as expected for a right triangle).

Solved Example 20 · 7-24-25 triangle

Question: A triangular park has sides 7 m, 24 m and 25 m. Find its area.

Concept: Heron’s formula. Steps: \(s = \frac{7+24+25}{2} = 28\). Area \(= \sqrt{28(28-7)(28-24)(28-25)} = \sqrt{28 \times 21 \times 4 \times 3} = \sqrt{7056} = 84\) m².

Answer: 84 m².

Solved Example 21 · Sides in the ratio 3 : 5 : 7

Question: The sides of a triangle are in the ratio 3 : 5 : 7 and its perimeter is 300 m. Find its area.

Concept: Perimeter \(3x + 5x + 7x = 15x = 300\) gives \(x = 20\).

Solution: Sides \(= 60, 100, 140\) m. \(s = 150\). Area \(= \sqrt{150 \times 90 \times 50 \times 10} = \sqrt{6\,750\,000} = 1500\sqrt{3} \approx 2598.08\) m².

Answer: \(1500\sqrt{3}\) m² (about 2598.08 m²).

Solved Example 22 · Sides 8, 11, perimeter 32

Question: The sides of a triangle are 8 cm, 11 cm and its perimeter is 32 cm. Find the area.

Concept: Third side \(= 32 - 8 - 11 = 13\) cm.

Solution: \(s = 16\). Area \(= \sqrt{16 \times 8 \times 5 \times 3} = \sqrt{1920} = 8\sqrt{30} \approx 43.82\) cm².

Answer: \(8\sqrt{30}\) cm² (about 43.82 cm²).

Solved Example 23 · Special cases of Heron’s formula

Question: Write Heron’s formula in the special cases of (clearly non-degenerate) triangles: (i) equilateral of side \(a\); (ii) isosceles with equal sides \(a\) and base \(2b\).

Solution: (i) \(s = \frac{3a}{2}\) so \(A = \sqrt{\frac{3a}{2}\cdot\frac{a}{2}\cdot\frac{a}{2}\cdot\frac{a}{2}} = \frac{a^2\sqrt{3}}{4}\).
(ii) \(s = a + b\) so \(A = \sqrt{(a+b)(b)(b)(a-b)} = b\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}\). Check by direct height: height \(= \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}\), giving the same product.

Answer: (i) \(\dfrac{a^2\sqrt{3}}{4}\); (ii) \(b\sqrt{a^2-b^2}\).

Try these special cases quickly

Use the results of Example 23 to verify: (a) an equilateral triangle of side 6 has area \(9\sqrt{3} \approx 15.59\) units², and (b) an isosceles triangle with equal sides 5 and base 8 has area \(4\sqrt{25-16} = 12\) units².

(a) \(\frac{36\sqrt{3}}{4} = 9\sqrt{3} \approx 15.59\) ✓ (b) \(a = 5, b = 4\): \(4\sqrt{25-16} = 4 \times 3 = 12\) ✓

14. Circumradius and Inradius of a Triangle

Every triangle fits inside a circle (the circumcircle, through all three vertices) and contains one (the incircle, touching all three sides). The corresponding radii are the circumradius \(R\) and inradius \(r\). Both are elegantly linked to the area of the triangle.

Formulas (stated; full proofs come in later classes)

  • \(\text{Area} = \dfrac{abc}{4R}\) — for circumradius \(R\).
  • \(\text{Area} = r \times s\) — for inradius \(r\), where \(s = \frac{a+b+c}{2}\).

Reason for the second: split the triangle into three smaller triangles, each with the incircle’s centre as one vertex and a side as the base; each has height \(r\), so the total area is \(\frac{ar + br + cr}{2} = rs\).

Solved Example 24 · Circumradius and inradius of a 3-4-5 triangle

Question: A right triangle has sides 3, 4 and 5 (units). Find (i) the circumradius \(R\) and (ii) the inradius \(r\).

Solution: Area \(A = \frac{1}{2} \times 3 \times 4 = 6\); semiperimeter \(s = 6\).
(i) \(A = \frac{abc}{4R} \Rightarrow 6 = \frac{3\cdot4\cdot5}{4R} \Rightarrow R = \frac{60}{24} = 2.5\) units.
(ii) \(A = rs \Rightarrow r = \frac{6}{6} = 1\) unit.

Answer: \(R = 2.5\), \(r = 1\) (unit).

Solved Example 25 · Equilateral triangle of side 6

Question: For an equilateral triangle of side 6, find the inradius \(r\) and circumradius \(R\).

Solution: Area \(= \frac{6^2\sqrt{3}}{4} = 9\sqrt{3}\); \(s = 9\). Then \(r = \frac{A}{s} = \frac{9\sqrt{3}}{9} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73\), and \(R = \frac{abc}{4A} = \frac{216}{36\sqrt{3}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{3}} = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.46\).

Answer: \(r = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73\), \(R = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.46\) (units).

15. Brahmagupta’s Formula for Cyclic Quadrilaterals

Can we find the area of a quadrilateral from its four sides alone? Usually no: for example, a rhombus can have four equal sides yet different areas, depending on its angles. The area of a rectangle is a product of its sides, so two different rectangles with equal perimeters have equal areas; but they are congruent too. For general quadrilaterals the four sides are not sufficient. However, there is a glorious exception: a quadrilateral whose four vertices lie on a circle.

Definition

A quadrilateral with all four vertices lying on a circle is called a cyclic quadrilateral (its opposite angles add to \(180^\circ\)).

Around 628 CE, the great Indian mathematician Brahmagupta gave the area of a cyclic quadrilateral purely in terms of its sides.

Brahmagupta’s Formula

\(A = \sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)},\quad s = \dfrac{a+b+c+d}{2}\)

for a cyclic quadrilateral with sides \(a, b, c, d\). (Note: the \(s\) factor is not present — only the four \((s - \cdot)\) factors are multiplied.)

Think and Reflect · The \(s\)-factor disappears

Compare Heron’s formula \(A = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\) with Brahmagupta’s formula \(A = \sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}\). What happens to the lone \(s\) factor, and where does \(s\) go?

Do not confuse Brahmagupta with Heron: Brahmagupta multiplies exactly four factors \((s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)\) with no leading \(s\). Memorising both side by side is the best way to avoid exam mistakes (see Section 27).

Solved Example 26 · Rectangle as a cyclic quadrilateral

Question: Apply Brahmagupta’s formula to a rectangle with sides \(a\), \(b\). Show that it returns the correct area.

Solution: \(s = a + b\). Each factor \((s-a) = b\), \((s-b) = a\), \((s-c) = b\), \((s-d) = a\). Product \(= a^2 b^2\), so \(A = ab\).

Answer: \(A = ab\) — the rectangle area, as required.

Solved Example 27 · Sides 5, 5, 7, 7

Question: A cyclic quadrilateral has sides 5, 5, 7, 7 (taken in order). Find its area.

Solution: \(s = 12\). Factors: \((s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d) = 7 \times 7 \times 5 \times 5 = 1225\). Area \(= \sqrt{1225} = 35\) sq. units.

Answer: 35 sq. units.

Solved Example 28 · Sides 3, 3, 5, 5

Question: A cyclic quadrilateral has sides 3, 3, 5, 5. Find its area.

Solution: \(s = 8\). Product \(= (8-3)(8-3)(8-5)(8-5) = 5 \times 5 \times 3 \times 3 = 225\). Area \(= \sqrt{225} = 15\) sq. units.

Answer: 15 sq. units.

Special case: isosceles trapezium

An isosceles trapezium with parallel sides \(2a\), \(2b\) and legs \(c\) is cyclic. Brahmagupta’s formula simplifies to \(A = (a+b)\sqrt{c^2 - (a-b)^2}\). Try checking this yourself from the figure (the height of the trapezium is the square-root factor, and the average base is \(a + b\)).

16. From Brahmagupta to Heron

Brahmagupta’s formula for the area of polygons is extremely influential: it eventually remarked that “the area is the root of the product of the deviations of the sides”. One of its most celebrated consequences is a deduction of Heron’s formula.

The trick: regard a triangle as a degenerate cyclic quadrilateral with one side of length \(d = 0\). Then points \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) (and the degenerate \(D\)) still lie on a circle, and Brahmagupta’s formula reads

\(\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-0)} = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\),

with \(s = \frac{a+b+c+0}{2} = \frac{a+b+c}{2}\). That is exactly Heron’s formula!

Think and Reflect

Is this “deduction” a rigorous proof of Heron’s formula?

It is an elegant and useful observation, but a rigorous proof of Heron’s formula should not rely on taking a limit of quadrilaterals. The purpose here is to show how closely the two formulas sit together and how generically one family of formulas works.

Solved Example 29 · Verify the connection

Question: Use Brahmagupta’s formula (with \(d = 0\)) to find the area of the 3-4-5 triangle. Compare with Heron’s result.

Solution: \(s = \frac{3+4+5+0}{2} = 6\). Product \(= (6-3)(6-4)(6-5)(6-0) = 3 \times 2 \times 1 \times 6 = 36\), area \(= 6\). Heron’s gave \(6\) too, and \(\frac{1}{2}\times3\times4 = 6\).

Answer: 6 sq. units — all three routes agree.

17. Squaring a Rectangle: A Showcase of Constructive Geometry

Here is a lovely problem from ancient Indian geometry: can we draw a square with exactly the same area as a given rectangle? The Indo-Arabic tradition answers with an explicit construction based on a circle arc. The earliest rules of this kind appear in the Śhulba-sūtras (c. 800 BCE — the “rules of the cord”, used to lay out Vedic fire-altars). The problem’s construction is given below as in the Gujarati geometry commentary (Arabic-speaking tradition influenced by Āryabhaṭa and Brahmagupta).

1

Let the rectangle be \(ABCD\), with \(\;AB = CD = b\) (the smaller side) and \(AD = BC = a\) (the larger side), where \(a > b\).

2

On the side \(AD\), mark the point \(E\) so that \(AE = AB = b\).

3

Let \(F\) be the midpoint of \(ED\). Then, because \(ED = a - b\), we have \(AF = b + \dfrac{a-b}{2} = \dfrac{a+b}{2}\).

4

On the segment \(AF\) construct the square \(AFGH\). The square has side \(\dfrac{a+b}{2}\).

5

With centre \(H\) and radius \(HG = \dfrac{a+b}{2}\), draw an arc. Let it meet the line drawn through a suitable point \(K\) parallel to \(AB\), giving the point \(P\).

6

Complete the square \(HPQS\). Then \(HPQS\) is the required square — its area equals the rectangle’s area \(ab\).

Why it works

The arc encodes a right triangle with hypotenuse \(\dfrac{a+b}{2}\) and one leg \(\dfrac{a-b}{2}\) (the two “pieces” cut off the longer side). By Pythagoras,

\(HP^2 = \left(\dfrac{a+b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\dfrac{a-b}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{(a+b)^2 - (a-b)^2}{4} = ab\)

So the square \(HPQS\) has side \(\sqrt{ab}\) and area \(ab\) — exactly the rectangle’s area. The band between the square and the arc is the “remaining strip” that the classical texts speak of.

Solved Example 30 · Squaring a \(8 \times 6\) rectangle

Question: A rectangle has sides \(a = 8\) and \(b = 6\). Find the side of the equal-area square and verify the area.

Solution: Square side \(= \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt{3} \approx 6.93\). Check: \((4\sqrt{3})^2 = 48 = 8 \times 6\).

Answer: \(4\sqrt{3}\) units (about 6.93), area 48 sq. units in both figures.

Think and Reflect

Apply the same construction with \(a = b\) (a square). What does the construction produce?

Then \(E = D\), \(F\) is the midpoint of \(AD\), and the construction simply recreates the original square: the square of side \(\sqrt{a \cdot a} = a\). The construction is consistent rather than degenerate, which is a good sanity check.

18. Area of a Circle

Ancient peoples needed the area of a circle long before anyone had a formula for it — for altars, storage vessels, land borders and more. Their approximations were all built from the same idea: measure the diameter, then compare with whatever square the circle “falls between”.

  • Babylonians (c. 1900 BCE) used the circumference: \(A \approx \dfrac{C^2}{12}\), i.e. taking \(\pi\) to be 3.
  • Egyptians used \(\dfrac{C^2}{4} \times \left(\dfrac{8}{9}\right)^2\), that is, \(A \approx \left(\dfrac{8d}{9}\right)^2 = \dfrac{64}{81}d^2\).
  • Śhulba-sūtras (c. 800 BCE) gave an equivalent square construction based on about \(\dfrac{7}{8}\) of the diameter.
  • Archimedes (c. 250 BCE) discovered a route that is exact in principle: cut the circle into a large number of thin sectors and lay them out as a near-parallelogram. He found the area is \(A = \dfrac{1}{2}Cr\).
  • Nīlakaṇṭha (Kerala, c. 1500) gave a beautiful proof by the same “slicing” idea, which you can reproduce below.

Area of a Circle

\(A = \pi r^2\)

Why (Nīlakaṇṭha’s slicing argument): cut the circle into \(2n\) thin wedges. Match each top wedge with the wedge opposite and lay the wedges in a row, alternating tip-up and tip-down. As \(n\) grows, the rearrangement tends to a parallelogram/rectangle of base \(\pi r\) (half the circumference) and height \(r\). Hence the area is \(\pi r \times r = \pi r^2\).

Try · Squares vs circles with equal perimeters

Which has the larger area: a square or a circle with the same perimeter \(P\)? (Compare \(P^2/A\) for the two shapes.)

For a square of side \(a\), \(P = 4a\) so \(P^2/A = 16a^2/a^2 = 16\), i.e. \(A = P^2/16\). For the circle, \(P = 2\pi r\) so \(A = \pi (P/2\pi)^2 = P^2/(4\pi)\). Since \(4\pi \approx 12.57 < 16\), the circle’s area is larger. In general, of all plane regions of a given perimeter, the circle encloses the largest area.

Solved Example 31 · Area of a circular pool

Question: A circular backyard pool has diameter 14 m. Taking \(\pi \approx 22/7\), find its area.

Solution: \(r = 7\) m. \(A = \pi r^2 = \frac{22}{7} \times 49 = 154\) m².

Answer: 154 m².

Solved Example 32 · Radius from the area

Question: The area of a circle is 154 m². Find its radius.

Solution: \(\pi r^2 = 154 \Rightarrow r^2 = 154 \times \frac{7}{22} = 49 \Rightarrow r = 7\) m.

Answer: 7 m.

19. Area of a Sector and of a Segment

Definitions

  • Sector: the region between two radii and the arc they cut off (like a pizza slice).
  • Segment: the region between a chord and the arc it cuts off.

By the same “fraction of the circle” reasoning used for arc length, a sector that subtends \(\theta^\circ\) at the centre fills the fraction \(\theta/360\) of the whole circle.

Sector Formulas

  • Area of sector \(= \pi r^2 \times \dfrac{\theta^\circ}{360^\circ}\)
  • Arc length \(l = 2\pi r \times \dfrac{\theta^\circ}{360^\circ}\)
  • Perimeter of sector \(= 2r + l\) (two radii, plus the arc)

The area of a segment is found by subtracting the triangle from the sector:

\(\text{Segment} = \text{Sector} - \text{triangle}\)

Solved Example 33 · Sector area, r = 7, θ = 60°

Question: Find the area of a sector of a circle of radius 7 cm with central angle \(60^\circ\).

Solution: \(A = \frac{22}{7} \times 49 \times \frac{60}{360} = 22 \times 7 \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{154}{6} = 25\frac{2}{3}\) cm².

Answer: \(25\frac{2}{3}\) cm² (about 25.67 cm²).

Solved Example 34 · Quadrant of a circle of circumference 44

Question: A circle has circumference 44 cm. Find the area of one quadrant (a quarter of the circle).

Solution: \(2\pi r = 44 \Rightarrow r = 7\) cm. Quadrant area \(= \frac{1}{4}\pi r^2 = \frac{1}{4} \times 154 = 38.5\) cm².

Answer: 38.5 cm².

Solved Example 35 · Sectors swept by a minute hand

Question: The minute hand of a clock is 7 cm long. Find the area it sweeps in 10 minutes.

Concept: 10 minutes is \(\frac{10}{60} = \frac{1}{6}\) of a full turn, i.e. \(60^\circ\).

Solution: \(A = \frac{22}{7} \times 49 \times \frac{60}{360} = 25\frac{2}{3}\) cm².

Answer: \(25\frac{2}{3}\) cm² (about 25.67 cm²).

Solved Example 36 · The windscreen-wiper blades

Question: A vehicle has two windscreen wiper blades, each 28 cm long and pivoted at one end. Each wipes an arc of \(120^\circ\). Find the total area wiped.

Solution: One blade: \(\frac{120}{360} \pi \times (28)^2 = \frac{1}{3} \times \frac{22}{7} \times 784 = \frac{17248}{21} \approx 821.33\) cm². Two blades: \(\approx 1642.67\) cm².

Answer: about 1642.67 cm².

Solved Example 37 · Segment of a circle of radius 15

Question: A chord subtends an angle of \(60^\circ\) at the centre of a circle of radius 15 cm. Find the area of the minor and the major segments. (Use \(\pi = 3.14\), \(\sqrt{3} = 1.73\).)

Solution: Whole circle \(= 3.14 \times 225 = 706.5\) cm². Sector area \(= \frac{60}{360} \times 706.5 = 117.75\) cm². The triangle with the two radii is equilateral: area \(= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \times 225 = \frac{1.73 \times 225}{4} = 97.3125\) cm².
Minor segment \(= 117.75 - 97.3125 = 20.4375 \approx 20.44\) cm². Major segment \(= 706.5 - 20.4375 = 686.0625 \approx 686.06\) cm².

Answer: minor \(\approx 20.44\) cm², major \(\approx 686.06\) cm².

Avoid a common slip

A sector and a segment are different regions. The segment is the sector minus the isosceles triangle formed by the two radii and the chord — never confuse the two.

23. More Solved Examples

Solved Example 39 · Perimeter of a sector

Question: Find the perimeter of a sector of a circle of radius 14 cm with central angle \(75^\circ\).

Solution: Arc \(l = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14 \times \frac{75}{360} = 88 \times \frac{5}{24} = 18.33\) cm. Perimeter \(= 2r + l = 28 + 18.33 = 46.33\) cm.

Solved Example 40 · Area of a trapezium

Question: The parallel sides of a trapezium are 40 cm and 20 cm, and the non-parallel sides are each 26 cm. Find the area.

Solution: Difference of parallel sides \(= 20\) cm; half is 10 cm (the “overhang” on each side). Height \(h = \sqrt{26^2 - 10^2} = \sqrt{676 - 100} = 24\) cm. Area \(= \frac{40+20}{2} \times 24 = 30 \times 24 = 720\) cm².

Answer: 720 cm².

Solved Example 41 · Radii from areas

Question: The areas of two circles are in the ratio \(16 : 25\). Find the ratio of their radii.

Solution: \(\frac{\pi r_1^2}{\pi r_2^2} = \frac{16}{25} \Rightarrow \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \frac{4}{5}\).

Answer: \(4 : 5\).

Solved Example 42 · Isosceles triangle from perimeter

Question: An isosceles triangle has perimeter 40 cm, and each of the equal sides is 15 cm. Find its area. (Use \(\sqrt{2} = 1.414\).)

Solution: Base \(= 40 - 2 \times 15 = 10\) cm. Height \(= \sqrt{15^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{200} = 10\sqrt{2}\) cm. Area \(= \frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 10\sqrt{2} = 50\sqrt{2} \approx 70.71\) cm².

24. Applications

Perimeter and area are everywhere. Here are nine typical real-world uses — work each one with the formulas above:

  • A1 · Fencing a garden: a rectangular garden 30 m by 20 m needs fencing all around — find perimeter \(= 100\) m.
  • A2 · Circular park: a park of diameter 14 m needs a boundary fence — circumference \(= 44\) m; at ₹120 per metre the fence costs ₹5280.
  • A3 · Tiling: a 6 m × 4 m room is tiled with 50 cm square tiles. Area \(= 24\) m² \(= 240{,}000\) cm²; each tile \(= 2500\) cm² ⇒ 96 tiles.
  • A4 · Painting: painting a parallelogram-shaped wall needs its area \(\text{base} \times \text{height}\).
  • A5 · Triangular field: a field of sides 13, 14, 15 m has semiperimeter \(s = 21\) and area \(\sqrt{21 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6} = 84\) m² (Heron).
  • A6 · Pizza slice: a slice (sector) of central angle \(60^\circ\) from a 14 cm-radius pizza has area \(\approx 102.67\) cm².
  • A7 · Jogging track: a circular track of radius 35 m needs its circumference \(= 220\) m per lap; 5 laps ⇒ 1100 m.
  • A8 · Farm land: a parallelogram farm with base 120 m and height 80 m has area 9600 m².
  • A9 · Semicircular garden: a semicircular garden of radius 7 m needs a fence along its boundary: arc 22 m + diameter 14 m = 36 m, plus grass area \(\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 = 77\) m².

Solved Example 43 · The pizza-slice question, precisely

Question: A pizza is circular with radius 14 cm. A slice cut with a central angle of \(60^\circ\) is eaten. Find the area of the slice and the perimeter of its upper (arc) side.

Solution: Sector area \(= \frac{22}{7} \times 196 \times \frac{60}{360} = 616 \times \frac{1}{6} \approx 102.67\) cm². Arc \(= 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14 \times \frac{1}{6} = 88 \times \frac{1}{6} \approx 14.67\) cm.

Answer: area \(\approx 102.67\) cm², arc \(\approx 14.67\) cm.

25. Competency-Based Questions

These questions check your understanding, not just your recall of formulas.

C1 · Same chord, two regions

In a circle of radius \(r\), a chord divides the circle into two regions — a minor segment and a major segment. A student says: “Both regions are sectors.” Is she correct? Explain.

No. A sector needs two radii as boundaries; the minor and major segments are bounded by chords and arcs. The two radii to the chord’s endpoints enclose a sector, but the chord cuts that sector into a triangle and a segment.

C2 · Which is the height?

A rhombus is 24 cm along every side and 10 cm between any two opposite sides. A student finds the area as \(24 \times 24\). What is wrong, and what is the correct area?

\(24 \times 24\) would be the area of a square; a rhombus’s area is base × perpendicular height — here height \(= 10\) cm (not a side). Area \(= 24 \times 10 = 240\) cm².

C3 · True or false

State with reason whether each is true or false: (i) \(\pi = 22/7\). (ii) Two triangles of equal area are always congruent.

(i) False — \(\pi\) is irrational and \(\frac{22}{7}\) is rational; \(\pi \approx 22/7\) and \(\pi \neq 22/7\). (ii) False — the median splits a triangle into two triangles of equal area that need not be congruent.

C4 · Draw the outline

A compound shape is made by placing a semicircle of diameter 8 cm on top of a rectangle 8 cm by 6 cm. A student adds the semicircular arc length to the whole rectangle perimeter. What is wrong, and what is the correct perimeter?

The shared diameter is not part of the outline. Correct: arc \(\pi \times 4 = 4\pi\) cm + three rectangle sides \(8 + 6 + 6 = 20\) cm ⇒ perimeter \(= (20 + 4\pi)\) cm ≈ 32.57 cm.

C5 · Choosing a formula

You know only the three sides of a scalene triangle. Should you use \(\frac{1}{2}bh\) or Heron’s formula? Explain.

Use Heron’s formula, because no base–height pair is known; once you compute the height (if needed) you may use \(\frac{1}{2}bh\), but Heron’s formula needs only the three sides.

C6 · Why cyclic?

A rhombus has sides 5, 5, 5, 5. Two students use Brahmagupta’s formula: one gets area 25 sq. units, another gets area 12 sq. units. Could both be reasoning consistently?

Yes, in the sense that a rhombus is not generally cyclic, so Brahmagupta’s formula does not apply. A square rhombus (cyclic) has area 25; a very flat rhombus has area approaching 0. The sides alone cannot give the area.

26. Case-Based Questions

Case 1 · The 400 m track (based on Section 7)

A 400 m track has two straights of 84.39 m each and two semicircular bends. The inner radius is 36.5 m, lanes are 1.22 m wide, and runners stay 0.3 m from the inner border of their lane.

  1. What is the total length of the two straights?
  2. Why is the athlete’s running radius in lane 1 equal to 36.8 m?
  3. Show that the athlete’s lap distance is exactly 400 m.
  4. Find the stagger between lane 1 and lane 2.

(1) \(2 \times 84.39 = 168.78\) m. (2) She runs 0.3 m out from the inner border: \(36.5 + 0.3 = 36.8\) m. (3) Bends form a full circle: \(2\pi(36.8) \approx 231.22\) m; total \(168.78 + 231.22 = 400.00\) m. (4) \(2\pi(36.8 + 1.22) - 2\pi(36.8) = 2\pi \times 1.22 \approx 7.67\) m.

Case 2 · Circular park

A circular park has diameter 14 m. The city wants to (i) fence it and (ii) lay grass inside.

  1. How much fencing is required to go around it once?
  2. What area of grass is needed?
  3. If fencing costs ₹120 per metre, what does one round of fencing cost?

(1) \(C = \pi d = \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 44\) m. (2) \(A = \pi r^2 = 154\) m². (3) \(44 \times 120 =\) ₹5280.

Case 3 · Tiling a room

A room is 6 m long and 4 m wide. Square tiles of side 50 cm are to be laid on the floor without cutting.

  1. How many tiles fit along the length? Along the width?
  2. How many tiles are needed in all?
  3. What if the same tiles are used in a 5 m × 5 m room?

(1) Length: \(600 \div 50 = 12\) tiles; width: \(400 \div 50 = 8\). (2) \(12 \times 8 = 96\) tiles (\(= 24\) m² ÷ \(0.25\) m²). (3) \(500 \div 50 = 10\) each way ⇒ \(10 \times 10 = 100\) tiles.

Case 4 · Cyclic quadrilateral 5-5-7-7

A cyclic quadrilateral has sides 5, 5, 7, 7 taken in order, and the diagonals divide it into special triangles.

  1. Write Brahmagupta’s formula for it.
  2. Compute the semiperimeter and the area.

(1) \(A = \sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}\), \(s = \frac{a+b+c+d}{2}\). (2) \(s = 12\), \(A = \sqrt{7 \times 7 \times 5 \times 5} = 35\) sq. units.

Case 5 · The clock

The minute hand of a clock is 7 cm long. Note the arcs and sectors it sweeps.

  1. What central angle is swept in 10 minutes?
  2. What area does the hand sweep in 10 minutes?
  3. How far does the tip of the hand travel in one hour?

(1) \(10\) min \(= \frac{10}{60} \times 360^\circ = 60^\circ\). (2) \(\frac{22}{7} \times 49 \times \frac{60}{360} = 25\frac{2}{3}\) cm². (3) Full circle circumference \(= 2\pi r = 44\) cm.

28. Exam Focus

  • Read the units. If the radius is given in cm and the answer expected in m (or vice versa), convert before substituting.
  • State formulas first. Write \(A = \frac{1}{2}bh\) (or Heron) before plugging in — it scores method marks even if the arithmetic hiccups.
  • Heron: compute \(s\) first and write the three factors explicitly. A slip in \((s-a)\) is the most common way to lose marks.
  • Fractions vs decimals. With \(\pi \approx 22/7\), cancelling is usually cleaner; with \(\pi = 3.14\), decimals are natural. Keep the answer form consistent with what is asked.
  • Perimeter of a sector = arc + 2 radii — it comes up again and again as a trap.
  • Brahmagupta needs a cyclic quadrilateral; Heron needs a triangle. State which you are using and why it applies.
  • Check the plausibility of the answer. An area can never be a tiny number if the sides are measured in metres; a perimeter can never be in square units.
  • Track problems: straights + two semicircles = straights + one circle. That single trick converts a scary word problem into a one-line computation.
