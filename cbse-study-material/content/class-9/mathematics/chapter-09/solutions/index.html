Exercise Set 9.1 · Propositions and converses

Question 1 · Parallel lines and corresponding angles

Question: Test the statement that parallel lines cut by a transversal make equal corresponding angles, and test its converse.

  1. Proposition: True. When a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding angles are equal by the parallel-line angle theorem.
  2. Converse: If a transversal makes a pair of corresponding angles equal, the two lines are parallel. This is also true by the converse of the corresponding-angles theorem.

Answer: Both the proposition and its converse are true.

Question 2 · Square and equal angles

Question: Test the claim “square ⇒ all angles equal” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: True. Each angle of a square is 90°, so all four angles are equal.
  2. Converse: “If a quadrilateral has all angles equal, then it is a square.” This is false: a 2 cm by 3 cm rectangle has four equal right angles but is not a square.

Answer: Proposition true; converse false (a non-square rectangle is a counterexample).

Question 3 · Angle-bisector segments in a triangle

Question: In triangle ABC, the angle bisectors from B and C meet at in-centre I and continue to AC and AB at E and F. Test “AB = AC ⇒ IE = IF” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: True. If AB = AC, the triangle is symmetric about the perpendicular bisector of BC. Reflection swaps B with C, E with F, and fixes I; hence IE = IF.
  2. Converse: “If IE = IF, then AB = AC.” This is false. For example, take angles A = 60°, B = 50°, C = 70°. The in-centre has equal perpendicular distances r to AB and AC. In right-triangle decompositions, IE = r/sin(B/2 + C) = r/sin 95° and IF = r/sin(C/2 + B) = r/sin 85°. Since sin 95° = sin 85°, IE = IF, while AB and AC are unequal because their opposite angles C and B differ.

Answer: Proposition true; converse false (the 60°–50°–70° triangle is a counterexample).

Question 4 · Adding the same number to equal numbers

Question: Test “if x = y, then a + x = a + y” for arbitrary numbers a, x, y, and test its converse.

  1. Proposition: True. Adding a to both sides of x = y gives a + x = a + y.
  2. Converse: If a + x = a + y, subtract a from both sides to get x = y. This is true for all numbers.

Answer: Both directions are true.

Question 5 · Product of two perfect squares

Question: Test “if a and b are perfect squares, then ab is a perfect square” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: True. If a = m² and b = n², then ab = m²n² = (mn)².
  2. Converse: “If ab is a perfect square, then both a and b are perfect squares.” This is false: 2 × 8 = 16 is a square, although neither 2 nor 8 is a square.

Answer: Proposition true; converse false (a = 2, b = 8).

Question 6 · Squaring equal real numbers

Question: For real x and y, test “x = y ⇒ x² = y²” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: True. Equal numbers have equal squares.
  2. Converse: x² = y² gives (x − y)(x + y) = 0, so x = y or x = −y. It does not force x = y; for example, x = 1, y = −1 have equal squares but are unequal.

Answer: Proposition true; converse false.

Question 7 · Cubing equal real numbers

Question: For real x and y, test “x = y ⇒ x³ = y³” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: True by cubing both sides of x = y.
  2. Converse: If x³ = y³, then x³ − y³ = (x − y)(x² + xy + y²) = 0. For real numbers, the cubic function is one-to-one, so this implies x = y.

Answer: Both the proposition and its converse are true.

Question 8 · Divisibility by 24

Question: For a positive integer n, test “24 | n ⇒ 4 | n and 6 | n” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: True. If n = 24k, then n = 4(6k) and n = 6(4k).
  2. Converse: False. n = 12 is divisible by both 4 and 6, but 24 does not divide 12.

Answer: Proposition true; converse false (n = 12).

Question 9 · Divisibility by 60

Question: For a positive integer n, test “60 | n ⇒ 5 | n and 12 | n” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: True because 60 is divisible by both 5 and 12.
  2. Converse: If 5 | n and 12 | n, then n is divisible by lcm(5, 12) = 60, since 5 and 12 are coprime.

Answer: Both directions are true.

Question 10 · A prime square has three factors

Question: For a positive integer n, test “if n is the square of a prime, then it has exactly three factors” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: If n = p² for prime p, its positive factors are 1, p and p², exactly three.
  2. Converse: True. If n has exactly three positive factors, its prime factorisation must be p² for a prime p. In general, the number of positive factors is the product of one plus each prime exponent; the only way to obtain 3 (prime) is a single exponent 2.

Answer: Both directions are true.

Question 11 · Product of two distinct primes

Question: For a positive integer n, test “if n is a product of two unequal primes, then it has exactly four divisors” and its converse.

  1. Proposition: If n = pq for distinct primes p and q, its divisors are 1, p, q and pq, exactly four.
  2. Converse: False. 8 = 2³ has exactly four positive divisors (1, 2, 4, 8), but it is not a product of two unequal primes.

Answer: Proposition true; converse false (n = 8).

Question 12 · Common factors of n and n + 3

Question: For positive integer n, test “if n and n + 3 have no common factor greater than 1, then 3 does not divide n” and its converse.

  1. Any common divisor of n and n + 3 divides their difference, 3. Thus their greatest common divisor is either 1 or 3.
  2. Proposition: If they have no common factor greater than 1, their gcd is 1, so it cannot be 3; therefore 3 does not divide n.
  3. Converse: If 3 does not divide n, the gcd cannot be 3. Since the gcd is either 1 or 3, it must be 1. Thus the numbers have no common factor greater than 1.

Answer: Both directions are true.

Question 13 · Counterexamples to prime-generating expressions

Question: Find counterexamples to the claims that 4n² + 1, n² + n + 11 and 4ⁿ + 3 are always prime.

  1. For 4n² + 1, choose n = 4: 4(16) + 1 = 65 = 5 × 13.
  2. For n² + n + 11, choose n = 10: 100 + 10 + 11 = 121 = 11².
  3. For 4ⁿ + 3, choose n = 4: 256 + 3 = 259 = 7 × 37.

Answer: n = 4, 10 and 4 respectively make the expressions composite.

Question 14 · Counterexamples involving powers of 2

Question: Find counterexamples to (i) “n prime ⇒ 2ⁿ − 1 prime” and (ii) “n even ⇒ 2ⁿ + 1 prime.”

  1. For (i), take prime n = 11: 2¹¹ − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89, so it is composite.
  2. For (ii), take even n = 6: 2⁶ + 1 = 65 = 5 × 13, also composite.

Answer: n = 11 disproves (i); n = 6 disproves (ii).

Question 15 · Divisibility by 8, 2 and 4

Question: Justify that divisibility by 8 implies divisibility by both 2 and 4. Is checking divisibility by 2 and 4 enough to prove divisibility by 8?

  1. If n = 8k, then n = 2(4k) and n = 4(2k); hence 2 | n and 4 | n.
  2. The reverse test is not enough. For example, 12 is divisible by 2 and 4 but not by 8. Indeed, lcm(2, 4) = 4, not 8.

Answer: The implication is true; the divisibility checks by 2 and 4 do not guarantee divisibility by 8.

Question 16 · Divisibility by 3 and the digit sum

Question: Write both if-then statements that express the relationship between a number being divisible by 3 and its digit sum being divisible by 3.

  1. First direction: If a whole number is divisible by 3, then the sum of its decimal digits is divisible by 3.
  2. Reverse direction: If the sum of a whole number’s decimal digits is divisible by 3, then the number is divisible by 3.
  3. To see why, write N = Σdₖ10ᵏ. Since 10ᵏ ≡ 1 (mod 3), N ≡ Σdₖ (mod 3). Thus N and its digit sum leave the same remainder on division by 3.

Answer: The two statements are converses, and both are true.

Question 17 · Constructing a quadrilateral from diagonal sticks

Question: Two sticks form the diagonals of a quadrilateral of a chosen type Q. Compare the construction when Q ⇒ “diagonals equal” and when “diagonals equal” ⇒ Q.

  1. (i) Q ⇒ equal diagonals: Equal sticks are necessary, because any quadrilateral of type Q must have equal diagonals. They may not be sufficient: the property gives a necessary condition only. The way the sticks cross can affect the resulting quadrilateral, and equal diagonals alone do not identify Q.
  2. (ii) Equal diagonals ⇒ Q: Equal sticks are again needed. Under this assumption, equal diagonals are sufficient for membership in Q, so any crossing arrangement that forms a valid quadrilateral has type Q; the particular crossing position does not change the conclusion.

Answer: In both cases use equal-length sticks. In (i) arrangement may matter and may need extra conditions; in (ii) the stated converse makes every valid arrangement a member of Q.
