Exercise Set 7.1 · Probability scale

Question 1 · Rank events on the probability scale

Question: Rank each event on the probability scale, label its likelihood, and explain your reasoning: Monday follows Sunday; snow in Mumbai in July; an elephant enters your classroom today; you greet a friend at school tomorrow.

Idea: (0) represents an impossible event and (1) a certain event. “More likely” and “less likely” describe events between these endpoints.

  1. Monday follows Sunday in the weekly cycle, so the event is certain: (P=1).
  2. Snow in Mumbai in July is treated as impossible in the textbook context: (P=0).
  3. An elephant entering an ordinary classroom that day is treated as impossible for this model: (P=0).
  4. Greeting a friend at school tomorrow is more likely than not, assuming it is a normal school day and the student attends.

Answer: (i) certain; (ii) impossible; (iii) impossible in the stated everyday model; (iv) more likely (conditional on attendance and a normal school day).

Exercise Set 7.2 · Measuring probability objectively

Question 1 · Sweet sample and population estimate

Question: A sample of 30 sweets has 10 red, 8 green, 7 yellow and 5 blue. Find the sample probability of green and estimate the number of yellow sweets in a bag of 600.

Sample size (30); green (8), yellow (7). Experimental estimate uses relative frequency.

(i) (P(green)=8/30=4/15).

(ii) Yellow share is (7/30). Estimated yellow sweets in a bag of (600): (600(7/30)=140).

Answer: (i) (4/15); (ii) about (140) yellow sweets.

Question 2 · Club survey estimate

Question: In a random sample of 40 students, 14 prefer Science Club, 11 Arts, 9 Sports and 6 Debate. Find the sample probability of Arts and estimate Sports preference in a school of 800.

There are (40) responses: (11) Arts and (9) Sports; school population (800).

(i) (P(Arts)=11/40).

(ii) Sports proportion (=9/40); estimated number (=800(9/40)=180).

Answer: (i) (11/40); (ii) approximately (180) students.

Question 3 · Coin toss activity

Question: Toss a fair coin 20 times and record heads and tails. Find the observed counts and experimental probability of heads, then state the probability of tails on one further toss.

Record your actual 20 tosses; the head and tail counts depend on the experiment. If your counts are (h) and (t), then (h+t=20), (P(exp)(H)=h/20), and (P(exp)(T)=t/20). For one valid sample record with (11) heads and (9) tails, these are (11/20) and (9/20).

The probability of tails on one further toss of a fair coin is theoretical and does not depend on the earlier record.

Answer: use your observed (h/20) and (t/20); for the next fair toss, (P(T)=1/2).

Question 4 · Paper-cup landing activity

Question: Toss a paper cup 100 times. Record bottom, top and side landings and assign experimental probabilities to the three outcomes.

Count the outcomes in your 100 trials as (b) (bottom), (u) (upside down) and (s) (side), where (b+u+s=100). Then (P(exp)(B)=b/100), (P(exp)(U)=u/100), (P(exp)(S)=s/100). These add to (1).

For illustration only, if a student observes (15,10,75), the estimates are (3/20,1/10,3/4). Your own observed frequencies should replace these example values.

Answer: experimental probabilities are each outcome count divided by 100.

Question 5 · Even result on a fair die

Question: For a fair six-sided die, find the probability of rolling an even number.

The outcomes are ({1,2,3,4,5,6}). Even outcomes are ({2,4,6}), so there are (3) favourable out of (6) equally likely outcomes.

(P(even)=3/6=1/2).

Answer: (1/2).

Question 6 · Compare experimental and theoretical die probabilities

Question: A die shows 3 on 3 of 12 rolls. Find experimental and theoretical probabilities of 3, explain the difference, and describe what more trials may show.

Three 3s in (12) trials gives (P(exp)(3)=3/12=1/4). For a fair die, only one of the six equally likely faces is 3, so (P(theory)(3)=1/6).

The values differ because a short run varies by chance. With many repeated rolls, the relative frequency is expected to move closer to (1/6), though it need not equal it at every stage.

Answer: experimental (1/4); theoretical (1/6); more trials generally bring relative frequency nearer (1/6).

Exercise Set 7.3 · Sample spaces and events

Question 1 · One six-sided die

Question: When one six-sided die is rolled, state the sample space and its number of outcomes.

The sample space is (S={1,2,3,4,5,6}). Counting its elements gives (n(S)=6).

Answer: (6) outcomes.

Question 2 · Three sample spaces

Question: Write sample spaces for: rolling a die with a coin toss; selecting an integer strictly between -5 and 5; drawing one ball from a box with green and red balls.

(i) Die and coin: (S={(1,H),(1,T),…,(6,H),(6,T)}), all 12 die–coin pairs.

(ii) Integers strictly between (-5) and (5): (S={-4,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,4}).

(iii) If outcomes record colour, (S={green,red}). The ball counts affect probabilities but do not add new colour outcomes.

Answer: the three sample spaces are the 12 ordered die–coin pairs, the 9 integers from (-4) to (4), and ({green,red}).

Question 3 · Snack and drink combinations

Question: A fair has Samosa, Pakora or Bhaji, and Chai or Lassi. List all snack-drink pairs and the event of selecting Samosa.

Pair each of 3 snacks with each of 2 drinks:

(S={(Samosa,Chai),(Samosa,Lassi),(Pakora,Chai),(Pakora,Lassi),(Bhaji,Chai),(Bhaji,Lassi)}).

The event “Samosa” consists of the two outcomes with Samosa.

Answer: (n(S)=6); event (={(Samosa,Chai),(Samosa,Lassi)}).

Exercise Set 7.4 · Tree diagrams

Question 1 · One fruit from each basket

Question: Basket A has one apple and two oranges; basket B has one banana and one mango. Draw a tree, list fruit-type pairs and find the probability of apple with banana.

Basket A: (P(Ap)=1/3), (P(O)=2/3). Basket B: (P(Ba)=P(M)=1/2). Branches from either first choice split to Banana or Mango.

Start
├─ Apple (1/3) ─┬─ Banana (1/2) → Apple-Banana
│               └─ Mango  (1/2) → Apple-Mango
└─ Orange (2/3) ┬─ Banana (1/2) → Orange-Banana
                └─ Mango  (1/2) → Orange-Mango

For one apple and one banana, multiply along that path: ((1/3)(1/2)=1/6).

Answer: 4 fruit-type pairs; (P(Apple and Banana)=1/6).

Question 2 · Two pen picks with replacement

Question: A box has 3 red, 4 black and 2 green pens. One pen is drawn, replaced, then another is drawn. List ordered colour pairs and find the probability of matching colours.

Total pens (=3+4+2=9). Since the first pen is replaced, the second draw has the same probabilities: (P(R)=3/9), (P(B)=4/9), (P(G)=2/9).

First colour R, B or G; from each, second colour R, B or G.
S = {RR, RB, RG, BR, BB, BG, GR, GB, GG}

Same-colour paths are (RR,BB,GG): (P= (3/9)^2+(4/9)^2+(2/9)^2=(9+16+4)/81=29/81).

Answer: 9 ordered colour pairs; probability of matching colours (=29/81).

End-of-Chapter Exercises

Question 1 · Basic probability terms

Question: Fill in the blanks for the probability of an impossible event, the name of the set of all outcomes, the probability of a certain event, and the probability of heads on a fair coin.

(i) An impossible event has probability (0). (ii) All possible outcomes form the sample space. (iii) A certain event has probability (1). (iv) For a fair coin, (P(H)=1/2).

Answer: (0); sample space; (1); (1/2).

Question 2 · Relative frequency from a survey

Question: In a survey, 15 of 50 students like football. Find the relative frequency as a fraction and decimal.

15 of 50 surveyed students liked football. Relative frequency (=15/50=3/10=0.3).

Answer: (3/10) or (0.3).

Question 3 · Equally likely outcomes

Question: Identify which listed experiments have equally likely outcomes: starting a car; tossing a fair coin; rolling a fair die; choosing from 3 red and 7 blue marbles; a baby being a boy or girl.

(i) Car start/not start: not necessarily equally likely. (ii) Fair coin: equally likely. (iii) Fair die: its six faces are equally likely. (iv) Bag with 3 red and 7 blue: colours are not equally likely. (v) In the simple two-outcome model stated, boy/girl are treated as equally likely.

Answer: (ii), (iii) and (v).

Question 4 · Five elementary probability calculations

Question: Find the probabilities for: at least one head with two coins; an even card from 1–10; a die result above 4; a non-red ball from 3 red, 2 blue and 1 green; exactly two heads from three coins.

(i) Two coins, at least one head: ({HH,HT,TH}) out of 4, so (3/4).

(ii) Number cards 1–10, even: 5 of 10, so (1/2).

(iii) Die result above 4: ({5,6}) of 6, so (1/3).

(iv) 3 red, 2 blue, 1 green; not red: (3) of (6), so (1/2).

(v) Three coins, exactly two heads: ({HHT,HTH,THH}) of 8, so (3/8).

Answer: (i) (3/4); (ii) (1/2); (iii) (1/3); (iv) (1/2); (v) (3/8).

Question 5 · Three flavours

Question: A bag contains one strawberry, one lemon and one mint candy. Find the probability of picking strawberry.

With one strawberry, one lemon and one mint candy, the sample space has 3 equally likely candies and just 1 is strawberry.

(P(strawberry)=1/3).

Answer: (1/3).

Question 6 · Outfit combinations

Question: List in a table every outfit made from one of two shirts (red or blue) and one of three pants (jeans, khakis or shorts).

Pair both shirts (red, blue) with each of the three pants options (jeans, khakis, shorts).

Question 7 · Tyre lifetime data

Question: From 1,000 tyre records (20 below 4,000 km, 210 from 4,001–9,000, 325 from 9,001–14,000, 445 above 14,000), find the probabilities of the three requested lifetime ranges.

From 1000 cases, counts are 20 (<4000 km), 210 (4001–9000), 325 (9001–14000), 445 (>14000).

(i) (20/1000=1/50).

(ii) Middle two bands: ((210+325)/1000=535/1000=107/200).

(iii) (445/1000=89/200).

The interval labels in the printed table should be checked against the NCERT PDF; the calculation treats “between 4000 and 14000 km” as the two stated middle bands.

Answer: (i) (1/50); (ii) (107/200); (iii) (89/200).

Question 8 · Letter cards from PEACE

Question: Cards spell PEACE. Find the probability of drawing P, E or C, and the probability of drawing a letter other than E.

There are 5 cards: P, E, A, C, E. For P, E or C, count both E cards: (4/5). Not E means P, A or C: (3/5).

Answer: (i) (4/5); (ii) (3/5).

Question 9 · Eight-sector spinner

Question: An eight-sector spinner is numbered 1–8 with equal chances. Find the probabilities of 8, an odd number, a number above 2, a number below 9, and a multiple of 3.

Outcomes 1–8 are equally likely. (i) (P(8)=1/8). (ii) Odd values (1,3,5,7): (4/8=1/2). (iii) Values above 2: (6/8=3/4). (iv) All 8 are below 9: (1). (v) Multiples of 3 are 3 and 6: (2/8=1/4).

Answer: (1/8,1/2,3/4,1,1/4), respectively.

Question 10 · Two balls without replacement

Question: A basket has 4 red and 5 blue balls. Draw one and set it aside, then draw another. Draw a probability tree and find red then blue and two blue.

Initially there are 4 red and 5 blue balls, 9 total. First-draw probabilities: red 4/9, blue 5/9. After red, the chance of blue is 5/8; after blue, the chance of blue is 4/8.

(i) Red then blue: (4/9) × (5/8) = 20/72 = 5/18.

(ii) Blue then blue: (5/9) × (4/8) = 20/72 = 5/18.

Answer: both probabilities are 5/18.

Question 11 · Certain and impossible events with two dice

Question: For two six-sided dice, give one event with probability 0 and one event with probability 1.

The minimum sum is (2), maximum (12). A sum of 13 is impossible; a sum from 2 through 12 is certain.

Answer: probability 0 event: sum 13. Probability 1 event: sum lies in ([2,12]).

Question 12 · Multi-step probability

Question: Find: the chance that two dice sum to a prime above 5; two balls from 4 red, 3 green, 2 blue are different colours without replacement; the first of three coins is heads and exactly two heads occur; a four-digit arrangement of 1,2,3,4 without repetition is even; exactly two correct guesses on a three-question, four-option test.

(i) Two dice: prime sums greater than 5 are 7 and 11, with (6+2=8) ordered outcomes of 36; probability (2/9).

(ii) Two balls from (4R,3G,2B), without replacement. Same-colour pairs count (C(4,2)+C(3,2)+C(2,2)=6+3+1=10); total pairs (C(9,2)=36). Different colours: (1-10/36=13/18).

(iii) Three coins, first H and exactly two heads total: (HHT,HTH) among 8; probability (1/4).

(iv) Permutations of 1,2,3,4: 24 total. Even numbers end in 2 or 4; each choice leaves (3!=6) arrangements, so (12/24=1/2).

(v) Exactly two correct among three guesses: 3 arrangements; each has probability ((1/4)^2(3/4)=3/64), total (9/64).

Answer: (i) (2/9); (ii) (13/18); (iii) (1/4); (iv) (1/2); (v) (9/64).

Question 13 · Two numbered draws

Question: Draw sample spaces (using trees) for two draws from balls numbered 1–4, with replacement and without replacement, and compare their sizes.

Numbered balls are 1–4. With replacement there are (4×4=16) ordered pairs (including equal pairs). Without replacement there are (4×3=12) ordered pairs (no equal pair).

Answer: sample-space sizes are 16 with replacement and 12 without.

Question 14 · Coin and die together

Question: A coin is tossed and a six-sided die rolled. List the combined sample space and count its outcomes.

Pair each coin result with each die face: (S={(H,1),…,(H,6),(T,1),…,(T,6)}). By the product rule, (2×6=12) outcomes.

Answer: 12 outcomes.

Question 15 · Number of heads in three tosses

Question: Three coins are tossed and only the number of heads is recorded. Choose the correct sample space from {1,2,3}, {0,1,2}, {0,1,2,3,4}, or {0,1,2,3}.

Three coins can produce 0, 1, 2 or 3 heads. Four heads cannot occur because only three coins are tossed.

Answer: sample space for the recorded number is ({0,1,2,3}).

Question 16 · Point chosen in rectangle with circle

Question: A point is selected uniformly from a 3 m by 2 m rectangle containing a circle of diameter 1 m. Find the probability that the point falls inside the circle.

Rectangle area = 3 m × 2 m = 6 m². The circle has diameter 1 m, so radius = 1/2 m and area = π × (1/2)² = π/4 m².

For a point chosen uniformly over the rectangle, probability is the ratio of areas: (π/4) ÷ 6 = π/24.

Answer: π/24 (approximately 0.131).
