1.1 Introduction

Coordinates name a position with an ordered pair. The first value measures horizontal movement from the y-axis; the second measures vertical movement from the x-axis. Keeping the order matters: \((x,y)\) and \((y,x)\) usually identify different points.

1.2 Settling In

The chapter uses a scaled floor plan to connect coordinates to room layouts. With the stated scale of 1 coordinate unit to 1 foot, horizontal and vertical differences describe floor distances. A top-view plan does not encode an object's height.

1.3 The 2-D Cartesian Coordinate System

The axes meet at the origin \((0,0)\). A point on the x-axis has y-coordinate 0; a point on the y-axis has x-coordinate 0. In the four quadrants, the signs are \((+,+),(-,+),(-,-),(+,-)\), in order from I to IV.

1.4 Distance Between Two Points in the 2-D Plane

Distance formula. For \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\), \(AB=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\). Squaring removes the sign of each change, so either point may be used first.

For horizontal or vertical segments, distance is the absolute difference of the changing coordinates. The midpoint of \(S(x_1,y_1)\) and \(T(x_2,y_2)\) is \(M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\).

Question 1(i) Figure reading

From the room plan, state how far door D₁ is from the left wall and from the x-axis.

  1. The left wall is the y-axis, so the horizontal distance of \(D_1\) from it is the x-coordinate of \(D_1\).
  2. Reading the plan gives \(D_1=(8,0)\). The door lies on the x-axis, so its vertical distance from that axis is 0.

Answer: \(D_1\) is 8 ft from the left wall and 0 ft from the x-axis.

Question 1(ii)

Read the coordinate pair for door D₁ from the plan.

The point is on the x-axis, so its second coordinate is 0. Its horizontal position is 8 units from the y-axis on the positive side.

Answer: \(D_1=(8,0)\).

Question 1(iii)

Use the endpoints of the room doorway to find its opening width.

  1. Both endpoints lie on the x-axis, so the opening width is the difference of x-coordinates.
  2. \(11.5-8=3.5\) ft.

Answer: The illustrated opening is 3.5 ft wide. That is a generous width in the plan. A single width measurement alone cannot certify wheelchair accessibility; clear opening, approach space, threshold and turning space also matter.

Question 1(iv)

Find the bathroom doorway width and compare it with the room doorway width.

The bathroom-door endpoints have the same x-coordinate, so subtract their y-coordinates: \(4-1.5=2.5\) ft. Compare with the room-door width of 3.5 ft.

Answer: The bathroom door is 2.5 ft wide, so it is 1 ft narrower than the room door.

Exercise Set 1.2

Reference: Chapter 1, Exercise Set 1.2, Questions 1–4. Layout answers give one valid example; other placements may also work.

Question 1 · Rectangular study table

Complete the rectangular table plan: locate the fourth foot and identify which dimensions the plan shows.

(i) Fourth foot. The given points include a horizontal side from \((8,9)\) to \((11,9)\) and a vertical side from \((11,9)\) to \((11,7)\). A rectangle shares the remaining x- and y-values, so the fourth point is \((8,7)\).

(ii) Position. The table occupies \(8\le x\le11\), \(7\le y\le9\). On the plan this sits in the open upper-right area of the bedroom, clear of the mapped bed, wardrobe and door swing, so it is a suitable proposed position.

(iii) Dimensions and height. The horizontal side is \(11-8=3\) ft and vertical side is \(9-7=2\) ft. The longer side (length) is 3 ft and shorter side (width) is 2 ft. A top-view plan contains no height coordinate.

Answer: Fourth foot \((8,7)\); length 3 ft; width 2 ft; height cannot be read from a 2-D floor plan.

Question 2 · Bathroom-door swing

Check whether the bathroom door can swing without hitting the wardrobe, and consider what widening the doorway changes.

The hinge \(B_1=(0,1.5)\) is on the y-axis and the other endpoint is \(B_2=(0,4)\), so the leaf is \(4-1.5=2.5\) ft long. When it opens into the bedroom through a right angle, its free end reaches at most \(x=2.5\). The wardrobe starts at \(x=3\), so the door clears it by 0.5 ft in this plan.

If widened to 3 ft, its free end reaches the wardrobe's nearest edge; a greater width could collide. Moving the wardrobe, changing hinge/swing direction, or using a sliding door would preserve clearance.

Answer: The 2.5 ft door does not hit the wardrobe in the shown swing. Keep at least the same clearance if widening it.

Question 3 · Bathroom plan

Use the bathroom plan to identify its corners, shower area and suitable fixture coordinates.

(i) Outer corners. The bathroom spans from \(x=-6\) to \(x=0\) and \(y=0\) to \(y=9\). Thus \(O=(0,0), F=(0,9), R=(-6,9), P=(-6,0)\).

(ii) Showering area. Its vertices are \(S=(-6,6), H=(-3,6), W=(-2,9), R=(-6,9)\). The top and bottom edges are horizontal and unequal in length, making the region a trapezium.

(iii) One possible fixture plan. The washbasin can occupy \(x\in[-6,-3],y\in[0,2]\); the toilet can occupy \(x\in[-3,-1],y\in[3,6]\). These have dimensions 3 ft × 2 ft and 2 ft × 3 ft, do not overlap each other or the shower, and leave space by the doorway. Other placements are possible if they fit and preserve access.

Answer: Outer corners \(O(0,0),F(0,9),R(-6,9),P(-6,0)\); shower \(S(-6,6),H(-3,6),W(-2,9),R(-6,9)\), a trapezium. Fixture coordinates show one valid layout.

Question 4 · Dining room and centred table

Use the dining-room plan to locate its corners and the feet of a centred table.

(i) Room corners. The side \(PA\) lies on the x-axis with \(P=(-6,0), A=(12,0)\), length \(12-(-6)=18\) ft. Extend 15 ft below the x-axis for the other corners \((12,-15),(-6,-15)\).

(ii) Table feet. The room centre is the midpoint of \((-6,0)\) and \((12,-15)\): \((3,-7.5)\). Centre the 5 ft side horizontally and 3 ft side vertically. Half-sizes are 2.5 and 1.5 ft, so the x-values are \(3\pm2.5\), y-values \(-7.5\pm1.5\).

Answer: Dining room corners \((-6,0),(12,0),(12,-15),(-6,-15)\). One centred table has feet \((0.5,-6),(5.5,-6),(5.5,-9),(0.5,-9)\).

End-of-Chapter Exercises

Starred questions. NCERT marks Questions 6–13 as higher-order challenge problems and says they are not intended for assessment. They are included for practice, not presented as exam requirements.

Question 1 · The axes' intersection

State the distance from the origin to each coordinate axis.

The axes meet at the origin. By definition, the origin is zero units from each axis.

Answer: \((0,0)\).

Next: Question 2 →

Question 2 · A vertical line through \(x=-5\)

Describe the coordinates and possible positions of points on the vertical line x = −5.

A line parallel to the y-axis keeps x fixed, so every point \(H\) has form \(H=(-5,y)\), where y may be any real number. If \(y>0\), H is in Quadrant II; if \(y<0\), Quadrant III; if \(y=0\), it lies on the x-axis.

Answer: \(H=(-5,y)\); Quadrant II, Quadrant III, or the x-axis are possible.

← PreviousNext: Question 3 →

Question 3 · Quadrilateral RAMP

For quadrilateral RAMP, identify perpendicular sides, parallel sides and a reflected pair.

For \(R(3,0), A(0,-2), M(-5,-2), P(-5,2)\), compare coordinate changes along each side.

  1. \(AM\) has constant y-coordinate and is horizontal. \(MP\) has constant x-coordinate and is vertical, so \(AM\perp MP\).
  2. \(AM\parallel x\)-axis and \(MP\parallel y\)-axis.
  3. \(M,P\) share x and have opposite y, so they are reflections in the x-axis.

Answer: \(AM\perp MP\); \(AM\parallel x\)-axis and \(MP\parallel y\)-axis; M and P mirror across the x-axis.

Question 4 · A right triangle using \(Z(5,-6)\)

Use Z(5, −6) to form an axis-parallel right triangle and find its side lengths.

Choose \(I=(0,0)\) and \(N=(0,-6)\). Segment IN is vertical and NZ horizontal, so the right angle is at N.

  1. \(IN=|0-(-6)|=6\) units.
  2. \(NZ=|5-0|=5\) units.
  3. \(IZ=\sqrt{6^2+5^2}=\sqrt{61}\) units.

Answer: \(IN=6, NZ=5, IZ=\sqrt{61}\) units (about 7.81).

← PreviousNext: Question 5 →

Question 5 · Coordinates without negative numbers

Decide whether coordinates restricted to non-negative values can locate every point in the plane.

Without negative values, only \(x\ge0,y\ge0\) and the axes can be represented. Points left of the y-axis or below the x-axis cannot be named.

Answer: No. Such a system does not cover the whole plane.

Question 6 · Testing collinearity Starred challenge

Determine whether M(−3, −4), A(0, 0) and G(6, 8) are collinear.

Use the distance formula and test whether the middle point splits the longest segment. For \(M(-3,-4),A(0,0),G(6,8)\):

  • \(MA=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\).
  • \(AG=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10\).
  • \(MG=\sqrt{9^2+12^2}=15\).

Since \(MA+AG=MG\), A is between M and G on one straight line.

Answer: Yes, M, A, and G are collinear.

Question 7 · A second collinearity check Starred challenge

Test whether the three given points R, B and C lie on one straight line.

Direction vectors \(\overrightarrow{RB}=(3,-4)\) and \(\overrightarrow{BC}=(6,-7)\) have determinant \(3(-7)-(-4)(6)=3\ne0\), so they are not parallel.

Answer: \(R(-5,-1),B(-2,-5),C(4,-12)\) are not collinear. A plot shows a bend at B.

Question 8 · Constructing isosceles triangles Starred challenge

Give coordinate examples of isosceles triangles satisfying the stated position conditions.

  1. Choose \(O=(0,0),A=(2,0),B=(0,2)\). The legs are each 2 units and meet at a right angle.
  2. Choose \(O=(0,0),C=(-2,-2),D=(2,-2)\). C and D lie in Quadrants III and IV, and \(OC=OD=\sqrt8\).

Answer: One valid pair is \((0,0),(2,0),(0,2)\) and \((0,0),(-2,-2),(2,-2)\).

Question 10 · Recovering an endpoint from a midpoint Starred challenge

Find the unknown endpoint B when A(3, −4) and B have midpoint (−7, 1).

  1. \(\frac{3+x}{2}=-7\Rightarrow 3+x=-14\Rightarrow x=-17\).
  2. \(\frac{-4+y}{2}=1\Rightarrow -4+y=2\Rightarrow y=6\).

Answer: \(B=(-17,6)\). Substitution gives midpoint \((-7,1)\).

Question 11 · Trisection points Starred challenge

Find the two trisection points of the segment from A(4, 7) to B(16, −2).

From \(A=(4,7)\) to \(B=(16,-2)\), the total change is \((12,-9)\). One third is \((4,-3)\). Thus \(P=A+(4,-3)=(8,4)\), and \(Q=A+2(4,-3)=(12,1)\).

Answer: \(P=(8,4), Q=(12,1)\). The equal vector steps give \(AP=PQ=QB\).

Question 12 · Points on, inside, or outside a circle Starred challenge

Classify listed points relative to a circle centred at the origin; decide whether D and E lie inside or outside.

(i) Squared distances from O are \(OA^2=1^2+(-8)^2=65\), \(OB^2=(-4)^2+7^2=65\), and \(OC^2=(-7)^2+(-4)^2=65\). The points lie on a circle of radius \(\sqrt{65}\).

(ii) \(OD^2=(-5)^2+6^2=61<65\), so D is inside. \(OE^2=0^2+9^2=81>65\), so E is outside.

Answer: Radius \(\sqrt{65}\); D is inside, E is outside. Comparing squared distances is enough.

Question 13 · Recovering vertices from side midpoints Starred challenge

Recover the triangle vertices from the coordinates of its side midpoints.

Let D, E, F be the midpoints of AB, BC, CA respectively. Twice each midpoint gives \(A+B=(10,2), B+C=(12,10), C+A=(0,6)\). Then \(2A=(A+B)+(A+C)-(B+C)=(-2,-2)\), so \(A=(-1,-1)\). Similarly, \(2B=(A+B)+(B+C)-(C+A)=(22,6)\) and \(2C=(B+C)+(C+A)-(A+B)=(2,14)\).

Teacher review: This uses the usual order D on AB, E on BC, and F on CA. If your textbook figure assigns those labels differently, reassign the midpoint pairs before solving.

Answer: \(A=(-1,-1),B=(11,3),C=(1,7)\). Their side midpoints are the three given points under that labeling.

Use ordered pairs to name two street intersections and explain what each coordinate represents.

An ordered pair names one north–south street and one east–west street. That pair crosses at exactly one point.

Answer: One intersection is named \((4,3)\), and one is named \((3,4)\).

Question 15 · Circles on a pixel screen

Use the pixel bounds of two circles to decide whether they appear on-screen and how many intersections they have.

The screen covers \(0\le x\le800,0\le y\le600\). Circle A has bounds \(20\le x\le180,70\le y\le230\); circle B has bounds \(150\le x\le350,130\le y\le330\). Both remain on-screen.

The centre distance is \(AB=\sqrt{(250-100)^2+(230-150)^2}=\sqrt{28{,}900}=170\). Since \(20=|100-80|<170<180=100+80\), the circles meet at two points.

Answer: Neither circle extends beyond the screen; the circles intersect in two points.

Question 16 · Is the quadrilateral a square?

Determine whether the quadrilateral with the given vertices is a square and find its area.

For \(A(2,1),B(-1,2),C(-2,-1),D(1,-2)\), consecutive side vectors are \((-3,1),(-1,-3),(3,-1),(1,3)\). Each has squared length 10. The first two have dot product \((-3)(-1)+(1)(-3)=0\), so adjacent sides are perpendicular.

Four equal sides and one right angle prove it is a square. Its area is \((\sqrt{10})^2=10\).

Answer: Yes, \(ABCD\) is a square; its area is 10 square units.

Common mistakes and examination tips

  • Reversing an ordered pair: read x first, then y.
  • Dropping a negative sign: keep coordinate differences in parentheses before squaring.
  • Calling axis points quadrants: a zero coordinate places a point on an axis.
  • Using a diagonal as a horizontal distance: apply the distance formula unless one coordinate is shared.
  • Testing collinearity from a sketch alone: compare slopes, vectors or distances.
  • Using differences for a midpoint: average x-values and y-values separately.
  • Confusing a floor plan with a 3-D model: a 2-D diagram gives no height.
  • Overlooking open-ended choices: state why your chosen coordinates satisfy the conditions.
Before submitting: label axes, preserve coordinate order, show substitutions, state units, and check that points lie in the intended quadrant or region.
