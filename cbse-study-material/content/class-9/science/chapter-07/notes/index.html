Learning objectives

By studying this chapter, you should be able to calculate work done by a constant force, connect work with changes in kinetic energy, compare common forms of energy, apply the conservation of mechanical energy in ideal situations, calculate power, and describe how pulleys, levers and inclined planes provide mechanical advantage.

Core idea

Work transfers energy when a force causes displacement. Energy may change form, and simple machines can change the size or direction of an effort while trading force for distance.

1. Work done by a constant force

In physics, work is done when a force causes displacement. For a constant force in the direction of displacement:

Formula and unit

Work = force × displacement in the force direction; when force is parallel to displacement, (W = F s).

SI unit: joule (J), where (1 J=1 N m). Work is a scalar quantity. Its sign depends on the direction of force relative to displacement.

If a constant force (F) makes angle θ with displacement (s), (W=Fscosθ). This means only the force component along the displacement contributes to work.

  • Positive work: force component and displacement point in the same direction; kinetic energy may increase.
  • Negative work: force component opposes displacement, as friction or braking force often does.
  • Zero work: displacement is zero, force is zero, or force is perpendicular to displacement.
Worked example

A student pushes a trolley with a constant horizontal force of 40 N through 5 m in the same direction.

(W=Fs=(40)(5)=200 J).

2. Work–energy theorem

The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy:

Work–energy theorem

Net work (Wnet=ΔK=Kf-Ki).

Kinetic energy (K=1/2mv2), where (m) is mass and (v) is speed. SI unit: joule.

Positive net work increases kinetic energy; negative net work decreases it. If net work is zero, kinetic energy does not change, although other forms of energy can change or forces may balance.

Worked example

A 2 kg cart speeds up from 3 m s−1 to 5 m s−1.

ΔK = (1/2(2)(52-32)=25-9=16 J).

Therefore the net work done on the cart is +16 J.

3. Forms of energy and mechanical energy

Energy is the capacity for causing change or doing work. It can appear as kinetic, potential, chemical, thermal, electrical, sound, light or other forms. Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy in a system.

Key expressions near Earth’s surface

Kinetic energy: (K=1/2mv2).

Gravitational potential energy relative to a chosen zero level: (U=mgh), where (h) is vertical height and (g) is gravitational field strength (near Earth, use the value provided by the question).

Mechanical energy: (Emech=K+U).

The zero level for gravitational potential energy is a choice. Only changes in potential energy matter in many problems; keep the same reference level through a calculation.

For an object falling without significant air resistance, gravitational potential energy changes into kinetic energy. The total mechanical energy stays constant. With friction or air resistance, mechanical energy can decrease as some energy transfers to thermal energy and sound; total energy is still conserved when all forms are counted.

4. Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

Formula and unit

Average power (P=(W / t)), where (W) is work and (t) is time.

SI unit: watt (W); (1 W=1 J s−1).

Worked example

Two motors each transfer 600 J of energy. Motor A does this in 10 s; Motor B takes 20 s.

(PA=600/10=60 W); (PB=600/20=30 W). Motor A has the greater power because it transfers the same energy in less time.

5. Simple machines and mechanical advantage

A simple machine makes a task more convenient by changing the magnitude or direction of an effort, or by changing how far it acts. It does not create energy. In an ideal machine, input work equals output work; in a real machine, some energy is transferred to heat and sound.

Mechanical advantage

Mechanical advantage (MA=(load / effort)).

For an ideal machine, a larger mechanical advantage means a smaller effort can balance a given load, usually over a longer distance.

Pulleys

A fixed pulley is attached to a support and mainly changes the direction of effort. A single movable pulley is attached to the load; ideally two rope segments support the pulley and load, so its mechanical advantage is 2. Real friction and pulley/rope mass reduce the actual advantage.

Inclined plane

An inclined plane raises a load over a longer path. For an ideal plane with slope length (L) and vertical rise (h), (MA=L/h). The effort is smaller than the load when (L) is greater than (h), but it acts through a longer distance. With friction, more input work is needed.

Levers

A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fulcrum. Its class depends on which part lies between the other two: the fulcrum, load or effort. For an ideal balanced lever, (effort× effort arm=load× load arm), so the ideal (MA=effort arm/load arm).

6. Worked examples and common mistakes

Kinetic energy

Find the kinetic energy of a 4 kg object moving at 3 m s−1.

(K=1/2mv2=1/2(4)(32)=18 J).

Gravitational potential energy

A 2 kg bag is lifted vertically by 5 m. Use (g=10 m s−2).

(ΔU=mgh=(2)(10)(5)=100 J).

Inclined plane advantage

A 2 m ramp raises a load through 0.5 m. For an ideal ramp, (MA=L/h=2/0.5=4). A 400 N load would require an effort of (400/4=100 N), ignoring friction.

Common mistakes
  • Calling every force × distance product work without checking the force direction.
  • Using mass in grams in an energy formula without converting to kilograms.
  • Confusing energy (J) with power (W).
  • Using total path length where vertical height (h) is required for gravitational potential energy.
  • Counting a pulley rope tail as a support strand even though it does not directly hold the moving load.
  • Assuming a real machine has ideal mechanical advantage or creates energy.

7. Original practice question bank

These are original revision questions, not copied textbook exercise prompts. Answers are included for self-check.

Multiple-choice questions — 10

1. A force of 20 N moves a box 3 m in the same direction. What work is done?
  1. 6 J
  2. 17 J
  3. 60 J
  4. 0 J

Answer: C. (W=Fs=20×3=60 J).

2. A person carries a bag horizontally at constant height. The upward supporting force does what work on the bag?
  1. Positive work
  2. Negative work
  3. Zero work
  4. Cannot be determined because mass is missing

Answer: C. Support force is vertical while displacement is horizontal, so the force is perpendicular to displacement.

3. What is the SI unit of power?
  1. Joule
  2. Watt
  3. Newton
  4. Metre

Answer: B. One watt is one joule per second.

4. If the speed of an object doubles while its mass stays the same, its kinetic energy becomes:
  1. Twice as large
  2. Four times as large
  3. Half as large
  4. Unchanged

Answer: B. (Kpropto v2), so doubling speed multiplies K by (22=4).

5. In an ideal single movable pulley, how many rope segments directly support the load?
  1. One
  2. Two
  3. Three
  4. Four

Answer: B. Two tension strands support the movable pulley in the ideal arrangement shown.

6. Which element lies between the other two in a second-class lever?
  1. Effort
  2. Fulcrum
  3. Load
  4. None

Answer: C. A second-class lever has the load between fulcrum and effort.

7. A force opposite to displacement does:
  1. Positive work
  2. Negative work
  3. Zero work always
  4. No work-energy transfer

Answer: B. The force component is opposite to the displacement.

8. Which formula gives gravitational potential energy change near Earth for a vertical height change?
  1. ΔU = mgΔh
  2. ΔU = mv
  3. ΔU = F/t
  4. ΔU = m/Δh

Answer: A. For constant (g), change in gravitational potential energy is (mgΔ h).

9. What does an inclined plane ideally exchange for a smaller effort?
  1. Greater effort distance
  2. Less load
  3. More energy created
  4. Zero work

Answer: A. The effort acts through a longer distance along the ramp.

10. A fixed pulley ideally provides mechanical advantage close to:
  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. Depends only on load mass

Answer: B. It mainly changes effort direction; it does not multiply the force ideally.

Numerical practice

  1. A 5 kg object moves at 4 m s⁻¹. Find its kinetic energy.

    (K=1/2(5)(42)=40 J).

  2. Find the work done when a 30 N force acts through 8 m in its direction.

    (W=30×8=240 J).

  3. A machine does 900 J of work in 15 s. Find its average power.

    (P=W/t=900/15=60 W).

  4. A 3 kg object is raised 4 m. Use (g=10 m s−2). Find the increase in gravitational potential energy.

    (ΔU=mgh=(3)(10)(4)=120 J).

  5. A 600 N load is balanced by a 150 N effort. Find the mechanical advantage.

    (MA=load/effort=600/150=4).

  6. An ideal lever has a 0.8 m effort arm and a 0.2 m load arm. Find its mechanical advantage.

    (MA=effort arm/load arm=0.8/0.2=4).

Concept questions

  1. State the work–energy theorem in words and symbols.

    The net work on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: (Wnet=Kf-Ki).

  2. Why does a fixed pulley help even if its ideal mechanical advantage is one?

    It changes the direction of the effort, so a person can pull downward to raise a load. This may make the task more convenient.

  3. Does a real frictional machine conserve mechanical energy?

    Not all input work remains useful mechanical energy; some transfers to thermal energy and sound. Total energy is conserved when all forms are included.

8. Quick revision and concept map

When the force has a component in the direction of displacement.

Net work equals change in kinetic energy.

Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of speed.

How quickly work is done or energy is transferred.

It reduces ideal effort force by increasing the distance over which effort acts.

Which of the fulcrum, load or effort lies between the other two.

Concept map

Force causing displacement → work → change in kinetic energy. Energy appears in many forms; kinetic + potential = mechanical energy. In ideal motion without dissipative forces, mechanical energy is conserved. Work or energy transferred per time → power. Pulleys, inclined planes and levers change force and distance; they do not create energy.
