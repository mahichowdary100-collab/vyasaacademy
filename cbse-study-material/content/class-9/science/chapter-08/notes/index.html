Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to describe how experimental evidence changed atomic models; identify electrons, protons and neutrons; use atomic and mass numbers to calculate particle counts; write element symbols and simple nuclide notation; distribute electrons in shells for the first 18 elements; find common valencies; and compare isotopes, isobars and weighted average atomic mass.

The central idea

Scientific models are explanations that are revised when observations do not fit. An atom has a tiny nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons outside it. The proton count identifies the element; proton plus neutron count gives the mass number.

1. Rediscovering the roots of atomic theory

Ancient Indian thinker Acharya Kanada described matter as being built from tiny particles called parmanus. Greek thinkers Leucippus and Democritus proposed indivisible particles called atomos. These were philosophical ideas rather than conclusions from controlled experiments. In 1808, John Dalton developed an atomic theory based on the experimental evidence available at the time and treated atoms as indivisible building blocks.

Later discoveries showed that atoms contain smaller particles. The model of the atom therefore developed as scientists gathered new evidence.

2. A short historical journey through atomic models

Thomson’s model

J. J. Thomson’s cathode-ray experiments showed that atoms contain negatively charged electrons. To account for the atom’s overall neutrality, he proposed a spread-out positive sphere with electrons embedded in it (the “plum pudding” model).

Rutherford’s gold-foil experiment and nuclear model

Geiger and Marsden directed positively charged alpha particles at very thin gold foil. Most passed through, some changed direction sharply, and a very small number bounced back. Rutherford inferred that most of an atom is empty space and that nearly all positive charge and most mass are concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus.

Why Rutherford’s model was incomplete

Classical reasoning suggested that a charged electron moving on a curved path would radiate energy. If it continuously lost energy, it should spiral into the nucleus. Since atoms are stable, Rutherford’s model alone did not explain atomic stability.

Bohr’s model

Bohr proposed that electrons occupy specific allowed energy levels (shells) and do not lose energy while remaining in one of these stationary states. An electron changes level by absorbing or releasing the corresponding energy difference. This model explained atomic stability better at this level, though modern quantum theory replaces fixed electron paths with probability clouds.

3. What components contribute to an atom’s mass?

Rutherford identified the positively charged nucleus; protons account for its positive charge. In 1932, James Chadwick discovered the neutron, a neutral particle with mass close to a proton. Electrons have a much smaller mass, so nearly all an atom’s mass is in its nucleus.

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so total positive and negative charge cancel. Neutrons contribute to mass but not electrical charge.

4. Element symbols, atomic number and mass number

An element symbol has one or two letters. The first letter is uppercase and a second letter, if present, is lowercase: for example, Co is cobalt while CO represents one carbon and one oxygen symbol written together. Some symbols come from Latin names, such as Fe for iron and Na for sodium.

Two numbers identify an atom

Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. It defines which element the atom is. For a neutral atom, electrons = protons.

Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. Therefore, neutrons (= A-Z).

AZX   →   p = Z;   n = A − Z;   e = Z for a neutral atom

For example, 2311Na has 11 protons, (23-11=12) neutrons and, when neutral, 11 electrons. The superscript is the mass number; the subscript is the atomic number.

Keep the counts distinct

Atomic number counts protons only. Mass number counts protons and neutrons. Do not use the electron count to calculate mass number at this level.

5. How are electrons distributed in different energy levels?

In the Bohr–Bury shell model, electrons fill energy levels starting nearest the nucleus. The shells are named K, L, M, N, corresponding to (n=1,2,3,4).

Shell rules for this chapter
  • Maximum capacity of shell (n): (2n^2) (K: 2, L: 8, M: 18).
  • The outermost shell holds at most 8 electrons (the first shell holds at most 2).
  • Fill inner shells before outer shells. For the first 18 elements, the familiar distributions are K then L then M, with M reaching 8 before N begins.

A neutral atom’s electron count equals its atomic number. For example, sodium has (Z=11), so its shell distribution is (2,8,1). The outermost electrons are called valence electrons.

Combining capacity of an atom: valency

Valency is an atom’s combining capacity. A simple way to find it is to count how many electrons the atom would lose, gain, or share to reach a stable outer shell. For 1–4 valence electrons, common valency often equals that count; for 5–7, it is often (8 -) that count; a full shell has valency 0. Carbon commonly shares four electrons.

6. A deeper look into atomic structure

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers. They have the same proton number and, when neutral, the same electron configuration; their neutron counts differ.

For isotopes found in nature, the average atomic mass reflects the relative abundance of each isotope. It is a weighted average, not the simple mean unless the isotopes are equally abundant.

Average atomic mass = Σ (isotope mass × fractional abundance)

Worked example: chlorine

Suppose chlorine has isotopes of mass 35 u (75%) and 37 u (25%).

Weighted average = (35 × 0.75) + (37 × 0.25) = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5 u.

This average describes a large natural sample; it does not mean each chlorine atom has a fractional mass of 35.5 u.

Isobars

Isobars are atoms of different elements that have the same mass number but different atomic numbers. For example, 4018Ar, 4019K, and 4020Ca each have mass number 40, but different proton numbers and therefore different element identities.

Compare carefully: isotopes have the same (Z) and different (A); isobars have the same (A) and different (Z).

8. Worked examples and common mistakes

Example 1: count particles in sodium-23

For neutral 2311Na: protons (=Z=11); neutrons (=A-Z=23-11=12); electrons (=11), because the atom is neutral.

Example 2: find an unknown mass number

An atom has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. Its atomic number is 17 and its mass number is (17+18=35). Element 17 is chlorine, so the nuclide is chlorine-35.

Example 3: electron arrangement in magnesium

Magnesium has (Z=12), therefore a neutral Mg atom has 12 electrons. Fill K with 2, then L with 8; the remaining 2 go to M. Configuration: (2,8,2). Its two outer electrons give common valency 2.

Common mistakes
  • Swapping the positions of (A) and (Z) in nuclide notation.
  • Calling (A) the number of protons; it is protons plus neutrons.
  • Assuming neutron count identifies the element; proton count does.
  • Drawing electrons inside the nucleus in a Bohr diagram.
  • Treating isotope diagrams as if they were size-accurate pictures of atoms.
  • Taking a simple average of isotope masses without using their abundances.
  • Confusing element symbols: the second letter is lowercase (Co, not CO).
Exam method

Write the given values (A) and (Z) first. Then use (p=Z), (n=A-Z), and for a neutral atom (e=Z). State when you use neutrality; ions have a different electron count.

9. Original practice questions

These are original revision prompts, not reproduced textbook questions.

  1. A neutral atom has (Z=15) and (A=31). Find its proton, neutron and electron counts.
  2. Write the shell distribution for a neutral atom with 16 electrons. State its valence electron count and common valency.
  3. Explain why the gold-foil experiment challenged Thomson’s model.
  4. Two atoms have the same atomic number but mass numbers 35 and 37. What relationship do they have? Which particle count differs?
  5. Calculate average atomic mass when isotope masses 10 u and 11 u occur in 20% and 80% abundances, respectively.
  6. Why are argon-40 and calcium-40 called isobars?
Show answers
  1. 15 protons, (31-15=16) neutrons, 15 electrons.
  2. For (Z=16), sulfur: (2,8,6); six valence electrons; common valency 2.
  3. Most alpha particles passed through, but a few underwent large deflections or reversed direction, implying a tiny concentrated positive nucleus rather than spread-out positive charge.
  4. They are isotopes; neutron number differs by 2.
  5. 10 × 0.20 + 11 × 0.80 = 10.8 u.
  6. Both have A = 40, while their atomic numbers (18 and 20) differ.

10. Quick revision

Remember these relationships
  • Neutral atom: protons = electrons; the nucleus contains protons and neutrons.
  • Atomic number (Z=p); mass number (A=p+n); hence (n=A-Z).
  • Isotopes: same (Z), different (A). Isobars: same (A), different (Z).
  • Shell capacity follows (2n^2); valency describes combining capacity based on reaching a stable outer shell.
  • Weighted average atomic mass uses each isotope’s abundance.
  • Models changed as experimental evidence improved; the Bohr atom is a useful simplified representation, not the modern complete description.

Concept map: experimental observations → atomic models refined → electrons, protons and neutrons identified → (Z) identifies the element and (A) counts nucleons → shell arrangement informs valency → neutron variation creates isotopes; equal (A) across different elements gives isobars.
