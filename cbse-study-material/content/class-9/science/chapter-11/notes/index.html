Learning objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to compare asexual and sexual reproduction; describe vegetative propagation, budding and spore formation; explain how meiosis makes gametes and contributes to variation; label the main parts of a flower; trace pollination through fertilisation to seed and fruit formation; compare reproductive strategies in animals; and describe, in age-appropriate scientific language, human reproductive organs, fertilisation, pregnancy, menstrual health and ways to reduce pregnancy and infection risks.

Big picture

Reproduction produces new individuals and passes genetic information to the next generation. Asexual reproduction usually makes near-identical offspring from one parent; sexual reproduction combines genetic material from gametes and creates variation.

11.1 Asexual Reproduction

Asexual reproduction forms a new individual from one parent without the fusion of male and female gametes. In many examples, cell division by mitosis copies the parent cell’s genetic material. The offspring are therefore genetically very similar to the parent and to one another; such copies are called clones. Small differences can still arise through mutations.

Vegetative Propagation in Plants

Vegetative propagation is asexual reproduction in which a new plant grows from a root, stem or leaf rather than from a seed. A potato tuber, ginger or turmeric rhizome, onion bulb, sugarcane or money-plant cutting, and plantlets along a Bryophyllum leaf are familiar examples.

Why use it?

Vegetative propagation can multiply selected plants quickly and preserve desirable features. A trade-off is that genetically similar plants may share the same vulnerability to a disease or environmental change.

Other Asexual Methods: Budding and Spores

Budding begins as a small outgrowth on the parent. It grows by cell division and may detach to live independently. Yeast and Hydra are examples. In spore formation, a parent organism produces spores that can grow when conditions are suitable; bread moulds such as Rhizopus are familiar examples. Spores are small and can be dispersed, but they need suitable moisture, temperature and nutrients to grow.

Louis Pasteur’s experiments challenged the idea that life appears spontaneously from non-living matter. They supported the conclusion that new life arises from pre-existing life and helped strengthen cell theory.

11.2 Sexual Reproduction and Meiosis

Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of male and female gametes. Each gamete carries one set of chromosomes. Meiosis is a special cell division that halves the chromosome number when gametes form. At fertilisation, the two sets combine in the zygote, restoring the species’ usual chromosome number.

Meiosis also reshuffles which member of each chromosome pair enters a gamete. With n pairs and independent assortment, there are (2^n) possible combinations from this process alone. For 23 pairs, (2^{23}=8{,}388{,}608) combinations. This is a simplified model; further processes can add variation.

Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants: Flower Structure

A flower contains reproductive structures. The stamen is the male part: its filament supports the anther, where pollen grains form. The pistil is the female part: stigma, style and ovary. The ovary contains ovules, each with an egg cell. Petals and sepals also support and protect the flower.

Pollination, Fertilisation, Seeds and Fruits

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma. In self-pollination, pollen reaches a stigma on the same flower or another flower on the same plant. In cross-pollination, pollen moves to a flower on another plant of the same species. Wind, water and animals such as insects or birds can act as pollinating agents.

After compatible pollen lands on a stigma, it can germinate and grow a pollen tube through the style towards an ovule. A male gamete travels through the tube and fuses with the egg cell: this is fertilisation, which forms a zygote. After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary develops into a fruit. A seed can germinate when conditions such as water, air and suitable temperature are available.

Pollination Strategies and Seed Dispersal

Flowers have features that can make pollen transfer more likely. Wind-pollinated plants often release abundant, light pollen; insect-pollinated flowers often attract visitors and pollen can stick to them. These are broad patterns, not rules for every species. Textbook sample estimates illustrate that wind pollination may release many more pollen grains, while the example insect-pollinated flowers form more seeds per flower; actual success varies by species and conditions.

Seeds also disperse away from the parent plant. Light seeds with hairs or wings, such as dandelion or madar, can travel by wind. Other seeds may be carried by water, animals or by the plant’s own mechanisms. Dispersal can reduce crowding and allow plants to reach new places.

11.3 Sexual Reproduction in Animals

In sexual reproduction, a male gamete and a female gamete meet and fuse. The resulting zygote divides and develops into a new organism. The details differ among animal groups. Reproduction may be sexual, asexual or include different methods at different stages in some organisms.

11.4 Reproductive Strategies and Variation in Animals

Reproductive strategies reflect where fertilisation and development occur and how much protection and care the young receive. In external fertilisation, gametes meet outside the body, commonly in water. Many eggs may be released because some are not fertilised or do not survive. In internal fertilisation, gametes meet inside the body; eggs or developing young receive more protection. These are general patterns, and species vary.

A larva is an early life stage that can look and live differently from the adult. A tadpole changing into a frog or a caterpillar becoming a butterfly illustrates metamorphosis. Sexual reproduction can create new combinations of inherited features. Variation within a population may help some individuals cope when conditions change.

11.5 Reproduction in Human Beings

Human reproductive systems mature gradually during puberty. The biological information below uses standard scientific terms and is provided for learning. Puberty is a physical process; it does not by itself mean a person is emotionally or socially ready for adult responsibilities.

11.5.1–11.5.3 Main reproductive organs

The male reproductive system includes testes, where sperm form; the scrotum, which holds the testes; ducts including the vas deferens; glands that add fluid; and the urethra and penis. The female reproductive system includes ovaries, which contain egg cells and release hormones; oviducts (fallopian tubes); the uterus; cervix; and vagina.

The urethra carries urine out of the body and also carries semen during ejaculation; the two are not passed at the same time. Sperm are tiny and motile, while an egg is larger, non-motile and contains stored nutrients. The exact organ structures are more detailed than these learning diagrams.

Gametes, Fertilisation and Early Development

In humans, meiosis forms gametes with 23 chromosomes each; typical body cells have 46. An egg is released from an ovary in ovulation. If sperm and egg fuse in an oviduct, a zygote forms. It divides repeatedly as it travels towards the uterus. When it attaches to the uterine lining, this is called implantation and marks the beginning of pregnancy in the chapter’s description.

Menstrual Cycle and Menstrual Hygiene

If no pregnancy begins, the thickened uterine lining is shed with some blood through the vagina; this is menstruation (a period). The menstrual cycle is often about 28 days, but cycles of roughly 21–35 days can occur. Day 1 is the first day of bleeding. Ovulation does not always occur on day 14; it is commonly about 14 days before the next period, and cycle timing varies.

Menstruation is a normal body process. Use clean menstrual products, change them regularly, wash hands before and after changing, clean the external genital area gently with water, and dispose of used products safely. Reusable products need thorough cleaning and complete drying. Severe pain, very heavy bleeding or other concerning symptoms are reasons to speak with a trusted adult and a qualified health professional.

Pregnancy, Care and Reproductive Health

11.5.7–11.5.8 Pregnancy, childbirth and maternal health

Pregnancy usually lasts about nine months and is often described in three trimesters. During this time, the embryo and later foetus develop in the uterus. Pregnancy and childbirth involve health needs for both the pregnant person and baby. The chapter highlights balanced nutrition, appropriate rest, regular check-ups and advice from qualified health workers. Avoid alcohol, tobacco and taking medicines without medical advice during pregnancy.

11.5.9–11.5.10 Maturity, contraception and infection prevention

Physical maturity and emotional readiness are not the same. Informed, responsible decisions and respect for consent are important. Some infections, called sexually transmitted infections (STIs), can pass through sexual contact. Condoms reduce the risk of many STIs and can also reduce pregnancy risk. Other contraceptive methods include prescribed hormonal medicines and intrauterine devices (IUDs), such as a Copper-T; suitability and side effects differ, so a qualified health professional should guide personal choices. Pills do not protect against STIs.

Health note

These are school-level study notes, not personal medical advice. Questions about puberty, periods, pregnancy prevention, symptoms or infection deserve confidential guidance from a qualified health professional or trusted adult.

The chapter also discusses IVF (in-vitro fertilisation), where fertilisation takes place outside the body in a laboratory setting, and recognises Indian scientist Subhash Mukhopadhyay’s early IVF work. In flowering plants, Professor P. Maheshwari contributed to plant embryology and in-vitro fertilisation research.

Worked Examples and Common Mistakes

Example 1: chromosome counts

A species has 12 chromosomes in a body cell. Its gametes normally have half that number: (12\div2=6) chromosomes. When two gametes fuse, the zygote has (6+6=12).

Example 2: possible assortment combinations

For 3 chromosome pairs, each pair can contribute either of its two chromosomes. Number of combinations (=2^3=8). This counts assortment combinations only, not every possible genetic difference.

Example 3: cycle timing

A student has a 32-day cycle. Ovulation is often roughly 14 days before the next period, so a rough textbook estimate is near day (32-14=18), not automatically day 14. This is not a reliable way to predict or prevent pregnancy.

Common mistakes
  • Calling pollination and fertilisation the same event: pollen transfer comes first; gamete fusion is fertilisation.
  • Mixing up ovule and ovary: ovule becomes seed; ovary becomes fruit.
  • Saying all vegetative propagation uses seeds; it uses vegetative plant parts.
  • Assuming every animal lays eggs or that fertilisation is always external.
  • Assuming ovulation always happens on cycle day 14.
  • Calling a zygote an embryo before it begins its divisions and development.
  • Assuming birth-control pills protect against STIs; condoms reduce STI risk.
  • Treating physical puberty as proof of emotional readiness.

Original Practice Questions

These original revision questions are written for these notes and are not copied textbook exercise questions.

  1. State two differences between asexual and sexual reproduction.
  2. Give one example each of vegetative propagation, budding and spore formation.
  3. Why does meiosis reduce chromosome number in gametes?
  4. How many assortment combinations are possible from 4 chromosome pairs?
  5. Distinguish self-pollination from cross-pollination.
  6. Put these in order: seed formation, pollination, fertilisation, pollen tube growth.
  7. Which flower structure becomes the seed, and which becomes the fruit?
  8. What is one difference between external and internal fertilisation?
  9. Why is it inaccurate to state that ovulation always occurs on day 14?
  10. Name one way to reduce STI risk and one source for personal reproductive-health guidance.
Show answers
  1. Asexual reproduction uses one parent and no gamete fusion; sexual reproduction joins gametes and usually creates more variation.
  2. Examples: ginger rhizome; yeast or Hydra budding; Rhizopus spore formation.
  3. It makes each gamete haploid, so fertilisation restores the usual chromosome number rather than doubling it each generation.
  4. (2^4=16) possible assortment combinations.
  5. Self-pollination transfers pollen within the same flower or plant; cross-pollination transfers it between plants of the same species.
  6. Pollination → pollen tube growth → fertilisation → seed formation.
  7. The ovule develops into the seed; the ovary develops into the fruit.
  8. External fertilisation occurs outside the body, commonly in water; internal fertilisation occurs inside the body.
  9. Cycle lengths vary; ovulation is often roughly 14 days before the next period, so its calendar day changes.
  10. Condoms reduce STI risk; speak to a qualified health professional or trusted adult for personal guidance.

Quick Revision

Remember
  • Asexual reproduction: one parent, no gamete fusion, offspring usually very similar.
  • Vegetative propagation uses roots, stems or leaves; budding and spores are other asexual methods.
  • Meiosis halves chromosome number to form gametes; fertilisation restores it in the zygote.
  • Stamen = anther + filament; pistil = stigma + style + ovary.
  • Pollination transfers pollen; fertilisation fuses gametes.
  • Ovule → seed; ovary → fruit.
  • Animal strategies differ in fertilisation, eggs, development and care.
  • In humans, sperm and egg each have 23 chromosomes; a typical body cell has 46.
  • Cycle length and ovulation timing vary; day 14 is only a 28-day example.
  • Condoms reduce STI and pregnancy risk; other methods require suitable professional guidance.
