Sound Waves: Characteristics and Applications

Learning objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to explain how vibration produces sound; describe how sound travels through a material medium as a longitudinal mechanical wave; identify compressions and rarefactions; interpret a density-versus-distance graph; relate wavelength, frequency, time period and speed; distinguish pitch, loudness and intensity; explain reflection, echo and reverberation; and describe uses of ultrasound, infrasound, echolocation and SONAR.

One key idea

A sound source vibrates, causing nearby particles of a medium to vibrate back and forth. The disturbance and its energy travel through the medium; the particles do not travel along with the sound from source to listener.

10.1 Production of Sound

Sound is produced by a vibrating object. Plucking a string, striking a drum skin, blowing across a flute’s air column, or striking a tuning fork sets a part of the source vibrating. Once the vibration stops, the sound fades away. The vibrating object is the source of sound.

A tuning fork is a U-shaped metal bar with two prongs. Striking it gently on a soft pad makes its prongs vibrate. When a vibrating prong lightly touches water, ripples appear, giving visible evidence of vibration. Vocal cords also vibrate when we speak or sing.

C. V. Raman

Sir C. V. Raman studied the acoustics of Indian percussion instruments, including the tabla and mridangam. The tabla’s central syaahi patch affects how its membrane vibrates and contributes to its characteristic range of tones.

10.2 Propagation of Sound

Sound can travel through gases, liquids and solids. The material that carries a sound wave is its medium. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles to pass on the disturbance.

When sound passes through a medium, each particle oscillates around its mean position and interacts with nearby particles. The disturbance moves onward, transferring energy. In air, the particle motion is back and forth along the same line as the direction in which the wave travels.

Longitudinal wave

A wave in which the particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave travel is called a longitudinal wave. Sound in air is longitudinal.

10.3 Sound Waves

As a source vibrates, it creates alternating regions in the medium:

  • Compression (C): particles are closer together than average; pressure and density are higher.
  • Rarefaction (R): particles are farther apart than average; pressure and density are lower.

A sound wave is a mechanical wave because it requires matter as a medium. It carries energy, while the particles of the medium oscillate locally rather than travelling with the wave.

Slinky is a model

A slinky helps us visualize compressions and rarefactions. It is an analogy for a medium, not a literal picture of air molecules at their true size or spacing.

10.4 Energy of Sound Waves

Sound waves transfer energy from the source to the surroundings. A sound can make a stretched membrane or sheet vibrate, causing light grains placed on it to jump. A microphone converts sound energy into an electrical signal; a speaker uses an electrical signal to vibrate a diaphragm and produce sound.

The medium’s particles do not travel all the way from the source to the listener. They pass the disturbance to neighbouring particles, while energy travels through the medium.

10.5 Graphical Representation of a Sound Wave

At a particular instant, the density of a medium varies from place to place as a sound wave passes. We can plot density against distance to represent that variation. Above-average density corresponds to a compression; below-average density corresponds to a rarefaction.

Do not misread the graph

The up-and-down curve shows how density changes with distance at one instant. It does not mean sound particles travel up and down; in air, they move back and forth parallel to propagation.

10.6 Characteristics of a Sound Wave

QuantityMeaningSymbol and SI unit
WavelengthDistance between successive compressions or successive rarefactionsλ, metre (m)
FrequencyNumber of complete oscillations per second at a pointf or ν, hertz (Hz)
Time periodTime for one complete oscillationT, second (s)
AmplitudeMaximum change in the medium’s density (or pressure) from its average valueA; depends on chosen graph scale
Wave speedDistance travelled by the disturbance per unit timev, metre per second (m/s)
Key relationships

Frequency: f = 1/T    ·    Wave speed: v = fλ = λ/T

Use consistent units. If wavelength is in metres and frequency in hertz (s⁻¹), speed is in metres per second.

Wave Speed in Different Media

Sound speed depends on the medium. Under comparable conditions it is generally greatest in solids, lower in liquids and lowest in gases. In dry air, speed also increases with temperature; the values below are approximate.

Medium (about 15 °C)Approximate sound speed
Air340 m/s
Water1,500 m/s
Steel5,000 m/s

In dry air, sound travels at about 331 m/s at 0 °C and 344 m/s at 22 °C. These are reference values; exact speed depends on the conditions and material.

Pitch, Loudness and Human Hearing

  • Pitch is how high or low a sound is perceived. In general, higher frequency is heard as higher pitch; lower frequency as lower pitch.
  • Loudness is how strong a sound seems to a listener. It generally increases with wave amplitude, but perception varies between listeners and with distance.
  • Intensity is a measurable physical quantity describing sound power passing through a unit area; loudness is a subjective perception. Decibels (dB) are commonly used to express sound level.

The approximate human audible range is 20 Hz to 20 kHz, and it varies with age and individual hearing. Sounds below 20 Hz are called infrasonic; sounds above 20 kHz are ultrasonic. People generally cannot hear either range.

10.7 Reflection of Sound

Sound can reflect from a surface. At a reflecting boundary, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection when both are measured from the normal to the surface.

Echo

An echo is a reflected sound heard separately from the original. At about 340 m/s, a time gap of at least 0.1 s means the sound travels 34 m for the round trip. The reflector therefore needs to be roughly 17 m or farther away for a distinct echo in these conditions.

Distance to reflector = (sound speed × echo round-trip time) ÷ 2

Reverberation

Reverberation is the persistence of sound caused by multiple reflections that arrive too close together to be heard as separate echoes. It can help a hall sound full, but excessive reverberation makes speech unclear. Curtains, upholstered seats and sound-absorbing panels can reduce unwanted reflections.

Gol Gumbaz in Bijapur is known for its Whispering Gallery, where repeated reflections make faint sounds audible across the dome.

10.8 Ultrasound, Infrasound and Applications

Ultrasound is sound above 20 kHz. It is used in medical imaging (ultrasonography), cleaning delicate parts, detecting flaws inside materials and some medical procedures. Infrasound is below 20 Hz and can help monitor some natural events, such as earthquakes or severe storms.

Echolocation

Bats emit short ultrasonic calls and use returning echoes to locate prey and obstacles. Dolphins and some other animals also use echolocation.

SONAR

SONAR (sound navigation and ranging) sends an ultrasonic pulse through water and measures the time for its echo to return. Because the measured time covers the outward and return path, distance is half the total travel distance.

Worked example: SONAR distance

A pulse returns after 0.80 s in water where sound speed is 1,500 m/s.

Distance = (1,500 × 0.80) ÷ 2 = 600 m.

Worked Examples and Common Mistakes

Example 1: frequency and time period

A source makes 12 complete vibrations in 3 seconds. Frequency = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Hz. Time period = 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25 s.

Example 2: wavelength

A sound has frequency 400 Hz and travels through air at 344 m/s. Wavelength = v ÷ f = 344 ÷ 400 = 0.86 m.

Example 3: echo distance

An echo returns 0.60 s after a clap. Use v = 340 m/s. Distance to reflector = (340 × 0.60) ÷ 2 = 102 m.

Common mistakes
  • Drawing sound travelling in air as a transverse wave; air sound is longitudinal.
  • Thinking air particles travel from the source to the ear; they oscillate locally.
  • Calling the peaks and troughs in a density graph the up-and-down path of particles.
  • Mixing up frequency and time period: f = 1/T.
  • Using v = fλ without matching units.
  • For echo or SONAR, forgetting that the measured time includes the outward and return trip.
  • Equating loudness and intensity; intensity is measurable, loudness is perceived.
  • Assuming sound can travel through empty space.

Original Practice Questions

These original revision prompts are written for these notes; they are not copied textbook exercise questions.

  1. A source completes 30 vibrations in 6 seconds. Find its frequency and time period.
  2. A sound wave has a wavelength of 0.50 m and frequency of 680 Hz. Calculate its speed.
  3. Why can a person hear a sound through a desk even if the air path is partly blocked?
  4. A reflected sound returns 0.40 s after it is made. Use a sound speed of 340 m/s to estimate the distance to the reflecting surface.
  5. Classify 15 Hz, 500 Hz and 25 kHz as infrasonic, audible-range or ultrasonic frequencies.
  6. A SONAR signal returns after 1.20 s. If sound travels through the water at 1,500 m/s, estimate the object’s distance.
Show answers
  1. f = 30 ÷ 6 = 5 Hz; T = 1 ÷ 5 = 0.20 s.
  2. v = fλ = 680 × 0.50 = 340 m/s.
  3. Sound propagates through solids as well as gases; the desk carries the vibration to the listener.
  4. d = vt/2 = 340 × 0.40 ÷ 2 = 68 m.
  5. 15 Hz: infrasonic; 500 Hz: within the approximate audible range; 25 kHz: ultrasonic.
  6. d = vt/2 = 1,500 × 1.20 ÷ 2 = 900 m.

Quick Revision

Remember
  • Vibrations produce sound; sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave.
  • Sound needs a material medium and carries energy, not a stream of travelling particles.
  • Compression = higher density; rarefaction = lower density.
  • Wavelength λ is compression-to-compression (or rarefaction-to-rarefaction) spacing; f = 1/T; v = fλ.
  • Higher frequency usually means higher pitch; greater amplitude is generally heard as louder.
  • Echo is a distinct reflected sound; reverberation is a blend of rapid reflections.
  • SONAR and echolocation use reflected sound; ultrasonic waves have many medical and industrial applications.

Concept map: vibrating source → wave transfers energy through a medium → compressions/rarefactions → wavelength, frequency, period, amplitude and speed → human perception (pitch/loudness) → reflection (echo/reverberation) and applications (ultrasound, echolocation, SONAR).
