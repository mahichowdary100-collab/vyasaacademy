Learning objectives

By the end of Chapter 9, you should be able to explain why mass is conserved in physical and chemical changes; apply the fixed mass ratio in a pure compound; describe Dalton’s atomic theory and its modern limits; distinguish molecules, covalent compounds and ionic compounds; draw simple electron-dot structures; write formulas from valencies or ion charges; compare common properties of ionic and covalent substances; and calculate molecular mass or formula-unit mass from atomic masses.

Big picture

Atoms combine in definite whole-number ratios. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms, so a closed system conserves total mass. The type of chemical bond and the structure of a substance help explain its formula and properties.

9.1 Law of Conservation of Mass

During a physical or chemical change, matter is not created or destroyed. In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged into new substances, so the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products when all substances are included.

Law of conservation of mass

Total mass before reaction = Total mass after reaction

A closed system keeps matter from entering or escaping. If a gas escapes from an open vessel, the measured mass of the vessel and its contents may fall even though mass has not vanished.

For example, vinegar (acetic acid) reacts with baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate) and produces carbon dioxide gas. A balloon fixed to the flask can capture the gas. If the gas is allowed to escape, a balance measuring only the flask and remaining contents shows a lower reading.

Worked example

A sealed reaction starts with 2.40 g of substance P and 1.60 g of substance Q. The products include 2.75 g of R and one other product. Find the mass of the other product.

Total reactant mass = 2.40 + 1.60 = 4.00 g.

Other product mass = 4.00 − 2.75 = 1.25 g.

Check: 2.75 + 1.25 = 4.00 g, matching the reactants.

Exam tip

In a mass calculation, list every reactant and product. If the experiment is open and produces a gas, explain whether the gas was included in the measured system.

9.2 Law of Constant Proportions

A pure compound always contains the same elements in a fixed ratio by mass, regardless of where the sample came from or how much of it is present. This is also called the law of definite proportions or Proust’s law.

Water always has hydrogen and oxygen in a mass ratio of 1 : 8. Thus, 9 g of water contains 1 g hydrogen and 8 g oxygen; 18 g of water contains 2 g hydrogen and 16 g oxygen.

Scale the ratio

A 45 g sample of pure water contains 5 g hydrogen and 40 g oxygen, because 1 + 8 = 9 parts and 45 ÷ 9 = 5 g per part.

9.3 Dalton’s Atomic Theory

Dalton used the laws of chemical combination to explain matter in terms of atoms. In school-level form, his main ideas are:

  • Matter is made of tiny particles called atoms.
  • Atoms of each element have characteristic properties and combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
  • In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged; they are not created or destroyed.

These ideas helped explain why mass is conserved and why compounds have a fixed composition. Modern discoveries refined some of Dalton’s proposals: atoms are divisible into subatomic particles, and atoms of the same element can have different masses because they may contain different numbers of neutrons (isotopes). In ordinary chemical reactions, however, nuclei are not changed.

9.4 How Atoms Combine?

Atoms can join to form molecules or extended ionic solids. A molecule is an electrically neutral group of more than one atom that can exist independently and retain the substance’s characteristic properties. Molecules may contain atoms of one element (H₂, O₂) or more than one element (H₂O, HCl). Some elements, such as helium, exist as separate atoms.

Atoms may achieve a more stable outer electron arrangement by sharing electrons or by transferring electrons. The attraction that holds the particles together is a chemical bond.

9.4.1 Bonding by sharing electrons: covalent bond

A covalent bond forms when atoms share one or more pairs of valence electrons. The shared electrons count for both atoms: hydrogen can complete a duplet, while many other atoms in these examples complete an octet.

One shared pair is a single bond (H—H or H—Cl). Two shared pairs form a double bond (as in O₂ or CO₂), and three shared pairs form a triple bond (as in N₂). Do not confuse the number of bonds with the total number of atoms.

Naming simple covalent compounds

For compounds made of non-metals, prefixes can show how many atoms are present: mono (1), di (2), tri (3), tetra (4), penta (5), and hexa (6). The second element’s name typically ends in “-ide”: CO is carbon monoxide, CO₂ is carbon dioxide, and SF₆ is sulfur hexafluoride. The prefix mono is often omitted for the first element.

9.4.2 Bonding by electron transfer: ionic bond

An ionic bond forms when electrons transfer from one atom to another. The atom that loses electron(s) becomes a positively charged cation; the atom that gains electron(s) becomes a negatively charged anion. Opposite charges attract. Ionic substances form extended crystal lattices; a formula such as NaCl gives the simplest whole-number ratio of ions, not a separate NaCl molecule.

Examples of common ions include Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻ and NH₄⁺. The magnitude of an ion’s charge tells how many electrons were lost or gained, but the nucleus’s proton number is unchanged by ordinary ion formation.

Ion notation

Write the charge as a superscript after the symbol: Mg²⁺, O²⁻, NH₄⁺. The numeral 1 is not written in a single charge (Na⁺, Cl⁻).

9.5 Writing Chemical Formulae

Covalent compounds

A covalent molecular formula states which atoms are present and how many of each molecule contains. Subscripts come from the actual atom ratio, for example H₂O, CO₂, H₂S and CCl₄.

Ionic compounds: balance the total charge

Write the cation first, then the anion. Choose the smallest whole-number ratio that makes total positive charge equal total negative charge. The charges are not written in the final neutral formula. Criss-crossing charge magnitudes is a shortcut; reduce any common factor and use brackets around a polyatomic ion when more than one is needed.

IonsCharge balanceFormulaCompound name
Na⁺ and Cl⁻1(+1) + 1(−1) = 0NaClSodium chloride
Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻1(+2) + 2(−1) = 0MgCl₂Magnesium chloride
Al³⁺ and O²⁻2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0Al₂O₃Aluminium oxide
Ca²⁺ and OH⁻1(+2) + 2(−1) = 0Ca(OH)₂Calcium hydroxide
Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0Al₂(SO₄)₃Aluminium sulfate

Naming ionic compounds

Name the positive ion first, then the negative ion. A single-element negative ion typically ends in “-ide” (sodium chloride, magnesium oxide). If a metal can form ions with different charges, specify its charge where required, such as iron(III) chloride for FeCl₃.

Formula-writing checks
  • Make the total charge zero for a neutral ionic compound.
  • Reduce subscripts to the simplest whole-number ratio: Mg₂O₂ becomes MgO.
  • Do not change a polyatomic ion’s internal formula; use brackets when repeating it, e.g. Ca(OH)₂.
  • Do not write the ion charges in the final neutral formula.
  • For covalent compounds, follow the named molecule’s atom counts; do not apply ionic charge balancing blindly.

9.6 Properties of Ionic and Covalent Compounds

Bonding and particle arrangement help explain how substances behave. The table describes common patterns, not a rule without exceptions.

PropertyIonic compoundsMany simple covalent compounds
Particles and structurePositive and negative ions in a repeating crystal latticeSeparate neutral molecules
Melting and boiling pointsUsually high because ions attract stronglyOften lower for small molecules
Electrical conductivity as a solidUsually do not conduct; ions are fixed in placeUsually do not conduct; no mobile ions
When molten or dissolved in waterConduct when ions are free to move (if soluble and present as ions)Most do not conduct; sugar dissolves but remains molecular
SolubilityMany dissolve in water; solubility variesVaries; some, such as sugar, dissolve in water

For electrical conduction, mobile charged particles are needed. In a solid ionic crystal the ions occupy fixed positions. When melted or dissolved (where the compound dissolves and ions are present), ions can move and carry charge. Sugar molecules in water remain neutral, so sugar solution does not conduct electricity appreciably.

Use careful wording

Use “usually,” “often,” and “many” for general property comparisons. Solubility and melting point can vary across compounds; avoid saying every ionic compound dissolves in water or every covalent substance is insoluble.

9.7 Molecular Mass of Covalent Compounds

The molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms shown in one molecule’s formula. Use the subscript as a multiplier; if there is no subscript, that element contributes one atom.

Molecular mass = sum of (number of each atom × its atomic mass)

Worked example: water

Given H = 1 u and O = 16 u:

H₂O = (2 × 1 u) + (1 × 16 u) = 18 u.

Worked example: nitric acid

Given H = 1 u, N = 14 u and O = 16 u:

HNO₃ = (1 × 1) + (1 × 14) + (3 × 16) = 1 + 14 + 48 = 63 u.

9.8 Formula Unit Mass of Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds form extended crystal structures rather than separate molecules. A formula unit shows the simplest whole-number ratio of ions. Its formula unit mass is the sum of the atomic masses represented by that ratio.

Worked example: magnesium chloride

Given Mg = 24 u and Cl = 35.5 u:

MgCl₂ = (1 × 24) + (2 × 35.5) = 24 + 71 = 95 u.

This is called formula unit mass; the ionic solid is a lattice, not a collection of MgCl₂ molecules.

Worked example: aluminium oxide

Given Al = 27 u and O = 16 u:

Al₂O₃ = (2 × 27) + (3 × 16) = 54 + 48 = 102 u.

Worked-example checklist and common mistakes

  1. Mass conservation: compare the complete mass of reactants with products; account for gases and the system boundary.
  2. Fixed proportion: reduce the element masses to a ratio, then scale all parts by the same factor.
  3. Bond type: sharing gives a covalent bond; electron transfer creates ions whose electrostatic attraction gives an ionic bond.
  4. Ionic formula: use ion charges and make the total positive and negative charge equal.
  5. Mass calculation: multiply each element’s atomic mass by its subscript, then add.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a lower balance reading in an open flask disproves conservation of mass when a gas escaped.
  • Confusing a mass ratio with an atom ratio; water’s mass ratio H:O is 1:8, while its atom ratio is 2:1.
  • Calling NaCl a molecule; its formula gives the simplest ion ratio in a lattice.
  • Leaving charges unbalanced, failing to reduce subscripts, or omitting brackets around repeated polyatomic ions.
  • Calling electrons themselves different because a dot and cross use different symbols; they only track origin in a diagram.
  • Adding atomic masses without using subscripts as multipliers.
  • Using “molecular mass” for an ionic solid instead of “formula unit mass.”

Original practice questions

These are new revision prompts written for these notes, not copied textbook exercise questions.

  1. A sealed reaction has 5.2 g of reactants. Two products have masses 1.7 g and 2.1 g. Find the mass of the third product.
  2. A 36 g sample of pure water is decomposed. Find the masses of hydrogen and oxygen produced.
  3. Explain why sodium chloride solution conducts electricity but sugar solution does not.
  4. Write the neutral formula made by Mg²⁺ and N³⁻. Show the charge check.
  5. Calculate the molecular mass of CO₂ using C = 12 u and O = 16 u.
  6. Calculate the formula unit mass of Ca(OH)₂ using Ca = 40 u, O = 16 u and H = 1 u.
Show answers
  1. Total product mass = 5.2 g, so the third product = 5.2 − 1.7 − 2.1 = 1.4 g.
  2. Water’s H:O mass ratio is 1:8, nine parts total; 36 ÷ 9 = 4 g per part. Hydrogen = 4 g; oxygen = 32 g.
  3. NaCl forms mobile ions in water; dissolved sugar remains neutral molecules and provides no mobile ions.
  4. Mg₃N₂: 3(+2) + 2(−3) = 0.
  5. 12 + 2(16) = 44 u.
  6. 40 + 2(16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74 u.

Quick revision

Key ideas at a glance
  • Closed-system chemical change: total mass of reactants equals total mass of products.
  • A pure compound has a constant mass ratio of its elements.
  • Atoms combine in whole-number ratios; covalent bonds share electrons and ionic bonds form through electron transfer and charge attraction.
  • Cations are positive; anions are negative. Neutral ionic formulae balance total charge and show the simplest ratio.
  • Molecular mass sums atoms in a molecule; formula unit mass sums atoms in an ionic formula unit.

Concept map: conservation of mass + fixed composition → Dalton’s atomic explanation → atoms combine → sharing gives covalent molecules; transfer gives ions and ionic lattices → formulas show atom counts or ion ratios → structure helps explain properties and masses.
