Exploration: Entering the World of Secondary Science

This opening chapter introduces the habits scientists use: asking questions, observing, measuring, building models, making predictions and revising ideas when evidence requires it.

Learning objectives

After studying this introduction, you should be able to:

  • distinguish observations from inferences and explanations;
  • describe how questions, evidence, models, predictions and tests connect;
  • explain what a model includes and what it leaves out;
  • record measurements with suitable units and sensible precision;
  • use a simple mathematical relationship to describe a pattern;
  • distinguish scientific laws, theories and principles without ranking them as guesses;
  • state assumptions and limits when making a prediction or estimate;
  • explain how scientific ideas can be checked and improved.
Starting idea

Science is a systematic way of building and improving explanations about the natural world. It uses observations, measurements, reasoning, models and testing.

1. How scientific inquiry works

Scientific inquiry often starts with curiosity: something happens, a pattern is noticed or an explanation needs checking. A scientist makes the question precise, decides what evidence could help, gathers observations or measurements and looks for patterns. An explanation or model can then be used to make a prediction. A test may support the model, reveal a limit or show that it needs revision.

This is not a rigid recipe. Scientists may revisit a question, use existing data, improve an instrument or compare explanations. Good scientific work makes its evidence and reasoning open to examination.

2. Observation, evidence and inference

An observation is what we notice or measure. An inference is a conclusion drawn from observations. An explanation connects evidence to a proposed reason. Keeping these ideas separate makes reports clearer.

StatementTypeReason
“The thermometer reads 24 °C.”ObservationA measurement was recorded.
“The liquid is warmer than before.”Comparison from observationsIt compares readings at two times.
“The dark surface absorbed more energy.”InferenceIt proposes a reason for a measured change.
Record first, explain second

Write what you observed before interpreting why it happened. Include units and conditions; keep unexpected results instead of silently removing them.

3. Models: keeping what matters

A scientific model is a simplified representation of an object, system, process or relationship. A map is a model of a place; a diagram can represent parts of a cell; a mathematical rule can represent how distance changes with time. A model keeps features useful for a question and leaves out others.

What models help with

They make complex or unseen systems easier to describe, compare, communicate and use for predictions.

What to check

Ask what the model includes, what it omits, which assumptions it makes and where it may stop being useful.

Models can change

New observations or better tools can show that a model needs revision.

Example: A road map keeps roads and landmarks useful for planning a route. It does not show every tree, slope or building interior. It is useful for a task, not a complete copy of the ground.

Remember

A model is not automatically wrong because it is simplified. Judge it by the question and understand its limits.

4. Scientific language and units

Scientific communication uses precise terms, agreed symbols and standard units so others can understand or repeat a measurement. “Long”, “hot” and “fast” can be useful in conversation, but a scientific comparison usually needs a number, unit and stated conditions.

QuantityCommon unitExample
Lengthmetre (m)2.4 m
Timesecond (s)18 s
Masskilogram (kg)0.5 kg
Temperaturekelvin (K) in SI; °C widely used in school measurements22 °C
Worked example: convert before comparing

A board measures 250 cm. Since 100 cm = 1 m:

250 cm ÷ 100 = 2.5 m

250 cm and 2.5 m describe the same length. A number without a unit can be ambiguous.

Kilo- means 1000 times a base unit; centi- means one hundredth; milli- means one thousandth. Match units before comparing or calculating.

5. Mathematics: a language for relationships

Mathematics helps science describe patterns compactly. A formula is a model connecting quantities; its assumptions must fit the situation.

Average speed

average speed = total distance ÷ total time

This gives an average for an interval. It does not mean an object moved at one steady speed at every moment.

Worked example: check units

A cyclist covers 150 m in 30 s. Average speed = 150 m ÷ 30 s = 5 m/s. Metres divided by seconds gives metres per second.

A pattern in a small data set is a clue, not proof that the same rule always applies. Look for missing conditions and measurements that do not fit.

6. Laws, theories and principles

Scientific laws, theories and principles are different kinds of well-supported knowledge. They are not steps on a ladder where a theory becomes a law.

TermGeneral roleStudy reminder
LawDescribes a regular pattern in nature, often in words or mathematics.Check the conditions where it applies.
TheoryProvides a broad, evidence-supported explanation that connects observations and laws.A theory is not an unsupported guess.
PrincipleStates a central idea used to reason about a class of situations.Use the meaning given in that topic.

Reliable evidence or better measuring methods can reveal limits and refine scientific knowledge. This does not mean all claims have equal support.

7. Predictions, tests and variables

A prediction says what should happen if a model is useful under stated conditions. It should be clear enough that evidence could support or challenge it.

Plan a fair comparison

  1. State the question and prediction.
  2. Choose what to change (independent variable).
  3. Decide what to observe or measure (dependent variable).
  4. Keep other relevant conditions similar.
  5. Use suitable tools, repeat measurements when practical and record results honestly.
  6. Compare results with the prediction, including values that do not fit.
Original investigation example

Question: Does release height affect a paper helicopter’s fall time? Change the height, measure time consistently, keep the helicopter design and release method the same, and repeat each height. One dramatic fall is not enough evidence for a broad conclusion.

Science also uses field observations, astronomy, historical evidence, computer models and existing data when a laboratory test is not possible.

8. Limits, failures and self-correction

Every measurement has limits. Instruments have finite resolution; observers may make mistakes; conditions may vary; and a model may work only over a certain range. A careful report states these limits instead of making a conclusion broader than the evidence allows.

When a prediction fails, scientists check the measurement, method, assumptions and model. A repeat test may reveal an error; sometimes the model needs revision. Repeating, comparing and sharing evidence help science correct itself over time.

Careful conclusion

“These results suggest…” may fit a small investigation better than “This proves…”.

9. Estimation

An estimate is a reasoned approximate value. It is useful when an exact measurement is unavailable, unnecessary or too time-consuming. Show assumptions and check that the result is sensible.

Worked estimate: classroom length
  1. A student takes 8 steps across the room.
  2. Estimate one step as about 0.65 m.
  3. Length ≈ 8 × 0.65 m = 5.2 m.
  4. If steps are 0.6–0.7 m, the estimate is about 4.8–5.6 m.

“Roughly 5 m” is more honest than reporting 5.200 m. A tape measure can check it.

Worked example: map scale

Scale: 1 cm represents 100 m. A route measures 3.5 cm on the map.

Estimated length = 3.5 × 100 m = 350 m. Curves and slopes can make the walked route differ.

  • Break large quantities into manageable parts.
  • Use a familiar reference and state it.
  • Round sensibly and avoid false precision.
  • Check whether the result is plausible.

10. One world, many branches

Nature does not divide itself into school subjects. Scientists combine physics, chemistry, biology, Earth and environmental science, mathematics and technology. A river question may involve flow, dissolved substances, organisms, weather, maps and statistics.

Physics

Matter, energy, forces, motion and interactions.

Chemistry

Substances, properties and changes.

Biology

Living organisms and life processes.

Earth and environment

Earth systems, resources, climate and interactions.

Mathematics helps scientists measure, represent data, compare patterns and communicate results.

11. Try an investigation

Observation or inference?

Watch an ice cube on a plate. Record three observations and one possible explanation. Label each statement so the evidence and explanation stay separate.

Make and check an estimate

Estimate a desk width using hand spans. State the reference length, calculate, measure with a scale, then describe one source of uncertainty.

Design a fair test

Choose one question about a paper helicopter. Write a prediction, identify changed and measured variables, and name two conditions to keep the same. Follow teacher safety instructions.

13. Original practice question bank

All practice items are original revision questions, not NCERT exercise prompts or previous-year questions.

Multiple-choice questions — 12

Very short answer — 8

  1. What is an observation?

    A statement based on something noticed or measured directly.

  2. What is an inference?

    A conclusion drawn from observations and evidence.

  3. Name the SI unit of length.

    The metre (m).

  4. How many metres are in 300 cm?

    3 m.

  5. What is a model?

    A simplified representation for a question.

  6. What is a prediction?

    A statement about what should happen under stated conditions.

  7. Is a scientific theory an unsupported guess?

    No; it is a broad explanation supported by evidence.

  8. Give one way to check a measurement.

    Repeat it consistently and compare readings.

Short answer — 2 marks — 6

  1. Give one observation and one inference about droplets outside a cold glass.

    Observation: droplets appear. Inference: water vapour in air condensed on the cold surface.

  2. State two features of a useful model.

    It represents features relevant to a question and has understandable limits.

  3. Why include units with measurements?

    Units identify the quantity’s scale and make comparisons meaningful.

  4. Convert 1.8 km to metres.

    1.8 × 1000 = 1800 m.

  5. Why might a prediction not match an observation?

    The model may omit a factor, conditions may differ or measurement uncertainty may matter.

  6. Why is “a theory becomes a law” inaccurate?

    Theory and law have different roles: theories explain; laws describe regular patterns.

Short answer — 3 marks — 5

  1. How can an observation lead to a scientific test?

    Ask a focused question, make a prediction and gather evidence under stated conditions.

  2. Why is a map a model? Name one detail it omits.

    It represents route features but omits details such as each tree or building interior.

  3. Estimate a corridor length if it takes 12 steps of about 0.7 m each. State one limit.

    12 × 0.7 m ≈ 8.4 m. Step length varies, so check with a measuring tape.

  4. How keep a paper-helicopter comparison fair?

    Change one factor, keep design and release method the same, measure consistently and repeat.

  5. How can science correct an earlier explanation?

    Test predictions against reliable evidence and revise if results reveal a limitation.

Case-based questions — 2 sets

Case 1: Map scale. A map uses 1 cm = 250 m and a route measures 4 cm.

  1. Estimate the route length.
  2. Name one detail the map may omit.
  3. Why may the walking route differ?
Show model answers

4 × 250 m = 1000 m (1 km); slope may be omitted; a walked route can curve or detour.

Case 2: Paper helicopter. A student changes blade length and release height between trials.

  1. Why is the result hard to interpret?
  2. What should change in the next test?
  3. Name one measurement.
Show model answers

Two factors changed; change one while controlling the other; measure fall time.

Diagram questions — 3

  1. Draw an inquiry cycle and label evidence, model, prediction and test.

    Use arrows and show that evidence may lead to revision or a new question.

  2. Draw a thermometer and record a reading with its unit.

    Show a readable scale and include °C or K.

  3. Draw a graph of a quantity changing over time with labelled axes and units.

    Put time on the horizontal axis and the measured quantity on the vertical axis; use sensible scales.

HOTS and competency-based — 4

  1. A model leaves details out. Is it necessarily wrong?

    No; judge whether omitted details matter to the question and whether the model works within its limits.

  2. One observation matches a prediction. Is the model proved?

    No; repeat tests and compare alternatives because one result may fit several models.

  3. Two groups measure a desk as 2.1 m and 2.4 m. Name checks before deciding one is wrong.

    Check units, instrument zero, endpoints, resolution, method and repeat readings.

  4. Why can “about 5 m” be better than “5.200 m” for a step estimate?

    The inputs are approximate; extra decimals suggest unsupported precision.

One-minute recap

Science builds explanations through questions, observations, measurements, models, predictions and tests. Models simplify. Mathematics describes relationships. Estimates make assumptions explicit. Laws describe patterns; theories explain them. Conclusions have limits, and explanations improve through checking.

Checklist

  • I can separate an observation from an inference.
  • I can explain a model’s limits.
  • I can record measurements with units.
  • I can identify variables in a fair test.
  • I can state a testable prediction.
  • I can give a sensible estimate.

1. Which is the strongest basis for a scientific explanation?
  1. Repeated observations with a clear record
  2. A popular opinion
  3. A single unrecorded guess
  4. A conclusion chosen before testing

Answer: A. Evidence can be examined and checked by others.

2. Which statement is an observation?
  1. The plant is sad
  2. The plant received 12 mL of water
  3. The plant grew because it was sad
  4. The soil must be unhealthy

Answer: B. The amount of water is measured directly.

3. A map is a model because it:
  1. contains every detail of a place
  2. selects features useful for navigation
  3. is always drawn full size
  4. predicts every weather change

Answer: B. It keeps selected spatial information and omits other details.

4. What should normally accompany a measured number?
  1. A unit
  2. An opinion
  3. An unrelated picture
  4. A guess only

Answer: A. The unit identifies the quantity and scale.

5. The SI unit of length is the:
  1. second
  2. metre
  3. kilogram
  4. litre

Answer: B. The metre (m) is the SI base unit of length.

6. A 2.4 m board is how long in centimetres?
  1. 24 cm
  2. 240 cm
  3. 2400 cm
  4. 0.24 cm

Answer: B. 1 m = 100 cm, so 2.4 m = 240 cm.

7. What makes a prediction useful for testing?
  1. It states what should happen
  2. It cannot be checked
  3. It changes after every observation
  4. It leaves out conditions

Answer: A. A testable prediction states an expected outcome under conditions.

8. In a fair test, a student should generally:
  1. change many variables at once
  2. change one chosen factor and control others
  3. omit units
  4. discard unexpected results

Answer: B. Changing one factor helps interpret a result.

9. A scientific theory is best described as:
  1. an unsupported hunch
  2. a broad explanation supported by evidence
  3. a law renamed
  4. an unchangeable opinion

Answer: B. Theories explain patterns and have evidence.

10. Which statement about laws and theories is correct?
  1. A theory turns into a law
  2. A law is always a guess
  3. They have different roles
  4. A law explains every mechanism

Answer: C. Laws describe patterns; theories explain them.

11. A student estimates 10 steps of about 0.6 m each. Estimate the length.
  1. 0.6 m
  2. 6 m
  3. 16.7 m
  4. 60 m

Answer: B. 10 × 0.6 m ≈ 6 m.

12. Why keep an unexpected measurement in the record?
  1. It may reveal an effect or method issue
  2. It is always wrong
  3. It makes the report longer
  4. It must replace the average

Answer: A. Check unexpected values; do not silently erase them.
