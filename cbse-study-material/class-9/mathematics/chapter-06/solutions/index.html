Exercise Set 6.1

Unless a question specifies another value, use π ≈ 22/7.

Question 1 · Radius from circumference

Concept: Circumference of a circle is C = 2πr.

Given C = 44 cm:

r = C ÷ (2π) = 44 ÷ (2 × 22/7) = 44 × 7/44 = 7 cm.

Answer: The radius is 7 cm.

Check: 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 44 cm.

Question 2 · Circumferences to 3 significant figures

Use C = 2πr with π = 22/7.

  • (i) r = 7 cm: C = 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 44 cm = 44.0 cm to 3 significant figures.
  • (ii) r = 10 cm: C = 440/7 cm ≈ 62.9 cm.
  • (iii) r = 12 cm: C = 528/7 cm ≈ 75.4 cm.

Answers: (i) 44.0 cm; (ii) 62.9 cm; (iii) 75.4 cm.

Check: Circumference increases in direct proportion to the radius, so the 12 cm circle must have a larger circumference than the 10 cm circle.

Question 3 · Arc length

Concept: An arc subtending θ° is θ/360 of the full circumference, so L = (θ/360) × 2πr.

  • (i) r = 3.5 cm, θ = 60°: L = (60/360) × 2 × 22/7 × 3.5 = (1/6) × 22 = 11/3 cm ≈ 3.67 cm.
  • (ii) r = 6.3 m, θ = 120°: L = (120/360) × 2 × 22/7 × 6.3 = (1/3) × 39.6 = 13.2 m.

Answers: (i) 11/3 cm (about 3.67 cm); (ii) 13.2 m.

Question 4 · Perimeter of a sector

Concept: Sector perimeter includes the curved arc and both radii.

First find the arc length:

L = (75/360) × 2 × 22/7 × 14 = (5/24) × 88 = 55/3 cm.

Perimeter = 2r + L = 28 + 55/3 = 139/3 cm ≈ 46.33 cm.

Answer: 139/3 cm, or about 46.33 cm.

Question 5 · Perimeters of nine composite boundaries

Method: Add only the outer straight and curved boundary. Internal construction lines do not count. For each semicircle, arc length is πr; for each quarter-circle, it is πr/2.

  1. (i) Two straight lengths of 80 m and two semicircles that make one circle of diameter 60 m: P = 2(80) + π(60) = 160 + 1320/7 = 2440/7 m ≈ 348.57 m.
  2. (ii) Semicircles of radii 6 cm and 4 cm plus two straight 2 cm pieces: P = 6π + 4π + 4 = 10π + 4 = 248/7 cm ≈ 35.43 cm.
  3. (iii) Four semicircular arcs of diameter 10 cm (r = 5 cm): P = 4πr = 20π = 440/7 cm ≈ 62.86 cm.
  4. (iv) Three semicircles of diameter 12 cm (r = 6 cm): P = 3πr = 18π = 396/7 cm ≈ 56.57 cm.
  5. (v) Four semicircles with r = 7 cm and four quarter-circles with r = 14 cm: P = 4π(7) + 4(π(14)/2) = 28π + 28π = 56π = 176 cm.
  6. (vi) One semicircle of diameter 28 cm and four semicircles of diameter 7 cm: P = π(14) + 4π(3.5) = 28π = 88 cm.
  7. (vii) Semicircles on the sides of a right triangle with legs 6 cm and 8 cm. The hypotenuse is 10 cm, so P = π(3 + 4 + 5) = 12π = 264/7 cm ≈ 37.71 cm.
  8. (viii) One semicircle of diameter 12 cm and three of diameter 4 cm: P = π(6) + 3π(2) = 12π = 264/7 cm ≈ 37.71 cm.
  9. (ix) One semicircle of diameter 20 cm and two of diameter 10 cm: P = π(10) + 2π(5) = 20π = 440/7 cm ≈ 62.86 cm.

Answers: (i) 2440/7 m; (ii) 248/7 cm; (iii) 440/7 cm; (iv) 396/7 cm; (v) 176 cm; (vi) 88 cm; (vii) 264/7 cm; (viii) 264/7 cm; (ix) 440/7 cm.

Question 6 · Tyre revolutions

Concept: In one complete revolution without slipping, a tyre travels one circumference.

(i) With diameter 56 cm, distance per revolution = πd = (22/7) × 56 = 176 cm = 1.76 m.

(ii) Convert 10 km to centimetres: 10 km = 1,000,000 cm. Number of revolutions = 1,000,000 ÷ 176 = 62,500/11 ≈ 5,681.82 revolutions, about 5,682 revolutions.

Answers: (i) 176 cm per revolution; (ii) about 5,681.82 revolutions (approximately 5,682).

Check: 5,681 complete turns cover 999,856 cm, leaving 144 cm (about 0.82 of a turn). This distinguishes complete rotations from the total fractional rotation.

Rounding note: A tyre can rotate fractionally during travel; “about 5,682” is the nearest whole turn.

Question 7 · Perimeter of the flower arcs

For each flower, add the arc lengths around every petal. The square or hexagon is a construction guide, not part of the flower perimeter.

(i) Square, side 14 cm. Each of four petals is bounded by two quarter-circle arcs of radius 7 cm. Thus the total is eight quarter-circles, or two full circumferences:

P = 2 × 2π(7) = 28π = 88 cm (using π = 22/7).

(ii) Regular hexagon, side 42 cm. Each of six petals contributes two 60° arcs of radius 42 cm. Together the arcs span 6 × 120° = 720°, equivalent to two complete circles:

P = 2 × 2π(42) = 168π = 528 cm (using π = 22/7).

Answers: (i) 88 cm; (ii) 528 cm.

Check: The construction polygon is not counted; only the curved petal boundary is measured.

Question 8 · Ratio of radii

Circumference C = 2πr uses the same constant 2π for both circles. Therefore the ratio of their circumferences equals the ratio of their radii:

r₁ : r₂ = C₁ : C₂ = 5 : 4.

Answer: The radii are in the ratio 5 : 4.

Check: Multiplying both radii by the same factor 2π preserves their ratio.

Exercise Set 6.2

Use base × perpendicular height for parallelograms and triangles. Use Heron’s formula when only side lengths are available: Area = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)], where s=(a+b+c)/2.

Question 1 · Triangle ADE in the textbook figure

Area(△ADE) = ½ × base × perpendicular height = ½ × 8 × 10 = 40 cm².

Answer: 40 cm².

Question 2 · Isosceles trapezium

The parallel sides differ by 40−20=20 cm. Because the non-parallel sides are equal, the extra 20 cm splits evenly: each right triangle at an end has horizontal leg 10 cm and hypotenuse 26 cm.

Height h = √(26²−10²)=√576=24 cm. Area = ½(40+20)×24 = 720 cm².

Answer: 720 cm².

Check: A height of 24 cm with a 10 cm offset gives a 26 cm sloping side by 10–24–26.

Question 3 · Heron’s formula

The third side is 32−8−11=13 cm, so the side lengths are 8, 11 and 13 cm. The semiperimeter is s=16 cm.

Area = √[16(16−8)(16−11)(16−13)] = √(16×8×5×3) = √1920 = 8√30 cm² ≈ 43.82 cm².

Answer: 8√30 cm² (about 43.82 cm²).

Question 4 · Triangular plot with sides in a ratio

Let the sides be 3k, 5k and 7k. Then 15k=300, so k=20 and the sides are 60 m, 100 m and 140 m. The semiperimeter is 150 m.

Heron’s formula gives Area = √[150×90×50×10] = 1500√3 m² ≈ 2,598.08 m².

Answer: 1500√3 m² (about 2,598.08 m²).

Question 5 · Shorter diagonal of a rhombus

Let the shorter diagonal be d. The longer diagonal is 2d. The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular, so Area = ½d(2d)=d².

d²=128, hence d=√128=8√2 cm ≈ 11.31 cm (take the positive root for a length).

Answer: The shorter diagonal is 8√2 cm.

Question 6 · Triangles on the same parallel lines

Triangles PCD and QCD share base CD. Since P and Q lie on a line parallel to CD, their perpendicular heights to CD are equal. Their areas are equal.

Answer: Area(△PCD) : Area(△QCD) = 1 : 1.

Question 7 · Point on a parallelogram diagonal

Use PR as a common line. Triangles PSO and PQO have common base PO on PR. In a parallelogram, diagonal PR splits it into equal-area triangles PQR and PSR; equivalently, vertices Q and S have equal perpendicular distances from PR. Therefore triangles with the same base PO and equal heights have equal areas.

Answer: Area(△PSO) = Area(△PQO).

Question 8 · Midpoint parallelogram inside a 4-gon

Let P,Q,R,S be the midpoints of AB,BC,CD,DA. The four corner triangles APS, BPQ, CQR and DRS are each one-quarter of the corresponding triangle formed with diagonal AC or BD: each has half of each adjacent side, so its area scale is 1/4. The two triangles on diagonal AC together have the whole 4-gon’s area; the two on diagonal BD do too. Therefore the four corner pieces total 1/4×(2×area of the 4-gon)=half its area. The central midpoint parallelogram is the remaining half.

By the midpoint theorem, PQ and SR are both parallel to AC; PS and QR are both parallel to BD. Thus the midpoint figure is a parallelogram.

Answer: The midpoint parallelogram has half the area of the original 4-gon.

Proof note: The area statement is Varignon’s theorem; the midpoint theorem supplies the parallel-side fact.

Question 9 · A point on a median

D is the midpoint of BC, so triangles ABD and ACD have equal areas. Triangles PBD and PCD also have equal areas: they share the same altitude from P to BC and have equal bases BD=DC.

Subtract the second equality from the first: Area(ABD)−Area(PBD)=Area(ACD)−Area(PCD). The left and right differences are Area(ABP) and Area(ACP), respectively.

Answer: Area(△ABP) = Area(△ACP).

Question 10 · Red and green regions in a square

Let the square’s side be s, and let P’s perpendicular distances from AB and CD be h and s−h. Then the red pair has area ½sh + ½s(s−h)=½s². Likewise, distances from BC and AD add to s, so the green pair also has area ½s².

Answer: Red area : Green area = 1 : 1.

Question 11 · Triangle area from a midpoint construction

Set B=(0,0), C=(1,0), A=(0,1) for a convenient unit right triangle; a general triangle follows by an affine scaling. Then D, the midpoint of AB, is (0,½). Let P=(t,0), where 0<t≤1. The line through P and D has slope −1/(2t). A parallel through C=(1,0) meets line AB (x=0) at Q=(0,1/(2t)).

Area(△BPQ)=½·t·(1/(2t))=¼, while Area(△ABC)=½. Thus the first is exactly half the second. This ratio is unchanged by scaling the original triangle.

Answer: Area(△BPQ) = ½ Area(△ABC).

Figure note: The constructed Q may lie on the extension of AB depending on P’s position; follow the textbook figure’s placement and parallel-line condition.

Exercise Set 6.3

Use sector area = (θ/360)πr². For segment questions, subtract or add the triangle between the two radii and the chord.

Question 1 · 60° sector, radius 7 cm

Area = (60/360)π(7²) = 49π/6. With π=22/7, this is 77/3 cm² ≈ 25.67 cm².

Answer: 77/3 cm².

Question 2 · Quadrant from circumference

With C=44 cm and π=22/7, r=C/(2π)=7 cm. A quadrant is one-fourth of the circle: Area = ¼πr²=¼×22/7×49=38.5 cm².

Answer: 38.5 cm².

Question 3 · Minute-hand sweep

In 10 minutes a minute hand turns (10/60)×360°=60°. With radius 7 cm, swept area = (60/360)×π×49 = 77/3 cm² ≈ 25.67 cm².

Answer: 77/3 cm².

Question 4 · Minor and major sectors

For r=10 cm and π=3.14, the circle area is 3.14×100=314 cm². The 90° sector is one-quarter: 78.5 cm². The 270° sector is three-quarters: 235.5 cm².

Answers: minor sector 78.5 cm²; major sector 235.5 cm².

Question 5 · Minor and major segments

The 60° central angle with equal radii forms an equilateral triangle. Minor sector = (1/6)×3.14×15²=117.75 cm². Triangle = (√3/4)×15² ≈ (1.73/4)×225=97.3125 cm².

Minor segment =117.75−97.3125≈20.44 cm². Major segment = circle area 706.5−20.4375≈686.06 cm².

Answers: minor segment ≈20.44 cm²; major segment ≈686.06 cm².

Question 6 · Two non-overlapping wipers

Each wiper sweeps a 120° sector of radius 28 cm. Total area =2×(120/360)×(22/7)×28² = 4928/3 cm² ≈ 1,642.67 cm².

Answer: 4928/3 cm² (about 1,642.67 cm²).

Question 7 · General 60° minor segment

The sector area is πr²/6. The two radii and chord make an equilateral triangle of area √3r²/4. Subtract:

Segment = πr²/6−√3r²/4 = r²(π/6−√3/4).

Proved: minor segment area = r²(π/6−√3/4).

Question 8 · Inscribed equilateral triangle

For an equilateral triangle with circumradius r, its side is √3r. Its area is (√3/4)(√3r)²=(3√3/4)r². Divide by the circle’s area πr²:

Answer: Area ratio = 3√3/(4π) ≈ 0.413.

Question 9 · Inscribed square

The square’s diagonal is the circle’s diameter 2r. Thus its area is diagonal²/2=(2r)²/2=2r². Dividing by πr² gives:

Answer: Area ratio = 2/π ≈ 0.637.

Question 10 · Inscribed regular hexagon

A regular hexagon inscribed in a circle is made of six equilateral triangles of side r. Its area =6×(√3/4)r²=(3√3/2)r². The ratio to πr² is:

Answer: Area ratio = 3√3/(2π) ≈ 0.827, twice the ratio in Q8.

Check: 3√3/(2π)=2×[3√3/(4π)].

End-of-Chapter Exercises

Use π≈22/7 unless a question gives another value. Question references below are brief; consult the textbook figures for the full layouts.

Question 1 · Area models for identities

For (a+b)², draw a square of side a+b and split each side into lengths a and b. Its four regions have areas a², ab, ab and b², so the total is a²+2ab+b².

For (a+b)(a−b)=a²−b², start with an a×a square and remove a b×b square. Rearrange the remaining L-shaped pieces into a rectangle whose sides are a+b and a−b.

For (a+b+c)², divide each side of a square into lengths a,b,c. The nine regions are three squares a²,b²,c² and two each of ab,ac,bc.

Results: (a+b)²=a²+2ab+b²; (a+b)(a−b)=a²−b²; (a+b+c)²=a²+b²+c²+2ab+2bc+2ca.

Question 2 · Isosceles triangle area

The base is 40−15−15=10 cm. The altitude bisects it, giving a right triangle with legs 5 and h and hypotenuse 15. Thus h=√(15²−5²)=10√2 cm. Area=½×10×10√2=50√2 cm².

Answer: 50√2 cm² (about 70.71 cm²).

Question 3 · Equal sides from base and area

From 60=½×10×h, the altitude is 12 cm. It bisects the 10 cm base, leaving 5 cm on each side. Each equal side is √(12²+5²)=13 cm.

Answer: 13 cm each.

Question 4 · Perimeter of a right triangle

Let the other leg be x. Since 54=½×12×x, x=9 cm. The hypotenuse is √(12²+9²)=15 cm, so perimeter=12+9+15=36 cm.

Answer: 36 cm.

Question 5 · Heron’s formula with side ratio 2:3:4

The scale factor is 45/(2+3+4)=5, giving sides 10,15,20 cm and semiperimeter 22.5 cm.

Area=√[22.5×12.5×7.5×2.5]=75√15/4≈72.62 cm².

Answer: 75√15/4 cm² (about 72.62 cm²).

Question 6 · Two methods for a 7–24–25 triangle

Right-triangle method: 7²+24²=25², so Area=½×7×24=84 cm².

Heron method: s=(7+24+25)/2=28. Area=√[28×21×4×3]=√7056=84 cm².

Answer: 84 cm² by both methods.

Question 7 · Bicycle wheel distance

One turn covers the circumference πd=60π cm. After 100 turns, distance=6000π cm. With π=22/7 this is 132000/7 cm≈18,857.14 cm=188.57 m.

Answer: 6000π cm, approximately 188.57 m.

Question 8 · Quadrant from circumference

C=66 cm gives r=C/(2π)=66/(44/7)=10.5 cm. Quadrant area=¼πr²=¼×22/7×10.5²=86.625 cm².

Answer: 86.625 cm².

Question 9 · Car wheel turns

With radius 28 cm, one turn covers 2πr=56π=176 cm. In 1 km=100,000 cm, the number of turns is 100,000/176=6250/11≈568.18 turns (568 complete turns plus part of another).

Answer: 176 cm per turn; about 568.18 turns in 1 km.

Question 10 · Same area and perimeter rectangles

Let a rectangle have sides x and y. Its perimeter fixes x+y and its area fixes xy. Therefore x and y are the two roots of the same quadratic t²−(x+y)t+xy=0. Any other rectangle with the same perimeter and area has the same pair of side lengths, possibly in the opposite order.

Answer: Yes. The rectangles must be congruent.

Question 11 · Trapezium area from a parallelogram

Copy the trapezium and turn the copy to form a parallelogram with base a+b and height h. Its area is (a+b)h, twice the trapezium’s area.

Answer: Area of trapezium = ½(a+b)h.

Question 12 · Trapezium split into triangles

Draw a diagonal. The two triangles have bases a and b along the parallel sides and the same altitude h. Their areas add to ½ah+½bh=½(a+b)h.

Answer: Area = ½(a+b)h.

Question 13 · Two identical trapezia

Turn one copy and fit it against the other along a non-parallel side. The pair makes a parallelogram with base a+b and height h. Its area is (a+b)h, so one trapezium has half that area.

Answer: Area of one trapezium = ½(a+b)h.

Question 14 · Kite area from diagonals

Let diagonal AC=p and BD=q, meeting perpendicularly at O. If AC is split into lengths x and y, and BD into u and v, then the four triangle areas sum to ½xu+½xv+½yu+½yv=½(x+y)(u+v).

Answer: Kite area = ½pq, by both algebraic grouping and adding the four right-triangle areas.

Question 15 · Scaling and area

  • (i) Rectangle, scale 2: Area changes from ab to (2a)(2b)=4ab. Tile it with two copies along each direction: four copies fit exactly.
  • (ii) Triangle, scale 2: Similarity multiplies area by 2²=4. Joining edge midpoints partitions the larger triangle into four congruent copies.
  • (iii) Triangle, scale 3: Similarity multiplies area by 3²=9. A triangular grid dividing each side into three equal parts partitions it into nine congruent copies.

Answers: area factors 4, 4 and 9; in each case the stated number of congruent smaller shapes tiles the larger one.

Question 16 · Shaded fractions in two figures

Fig. 6.43: The shaded quadrilateral is made of two triangles that each match an unshaded companion triangle in area: each pair has equal bases and the same height. Thus the unshaded and shaded parts together contribute twice the shaded area.

Answer for Fig. 6.43: 1/2 of the large triangle.

Fig. 6.44: Take the square side as 1. The four corner right triangles have area 1/4 each. Comparing their overlaps with the central unshaded region shows that the central square has area 1/5 of the whole square.

Answer for Fig. 6.44: 1/5 of the square.

Figure check: The second fraction relies on the exact overlapping construction in Fig. 6.44; recheck its labels and shading against the official PDF.

Question 17 · Circle-covered fraction in two rectangles

For n equal circles of radius r arranged in one row, the enclosing rectangle is 2nr by 2r, with area 4nr². The circles have combined area nπr².

Fraction covered = nπr²/(4nr²)=π/4 in either figure.

Answer: π/4 (or 11/14 using π≈22/7).

Question 18 · General row of circles

For 10, 20 or 50 circles the covered fraction stays π/4, because n cancels from (nπr²)/(4nr²). This proves the conjecture for any positive whole number n.

Answer: The fraction is π/4 for every such row, including 10, 20 and 50 circles.

Question 19 · Nine identical rectangles

Let each small rectangle have long side l and short side b. From the arrangement, four long sides span the same width as five short sides: 4l=5b, so b=4l/5. The large rectangle is 4l by (l+b), and its area is 72:

4l(l+4l/5)=72 ⇒ 36l²/5=72 ⇒ l=√10 cm and b=4√10/5 cm.

Perimeter of one small rectangle =2(l+b)=18√10/5 cm ≈ 11.38 cm.

Answer: 18√10/5 cm (about 11.38 cm).

Question 20 · Equal shaded triangles at the trisected side

Since BD = DE = EC, the bases BD and EC are equal. The perpendicular distance from A to line BC is the same altitude h for both triangles.

Area(ABD) = ½(BD)h and Area(AEC) = ½(EC)h. Because BD = EC, these areas are equal. In fact, △ADE has the same base length and altitude too, so all three smaller triangles have equal area.

One finite-piece construction: For the cut plan, temporarily slide a copy of the red triangle until its base overlaps the blue base on the same line. Draw both outlines and their crossings. This gives a common central pentagon and three triangular flaps. Cut the blue triangle along those boundaries and label the flaps 1, 2, and 3 to match their positions in the red outline. Keep the common pentagon in place; turn pieces 2 and 3 through 180° around the marked midpoints on their sloping sides, then translate piece 1 by one base length along the baseline. The four pieces fill the red triangle using only rigid moves. The temporary overlay is only a guide for placing the cuts.

This general four-piece method is described in Rowland, “Morphing equal-area triangles,” Tangenten 2/2015, Figures 9–10 (construction reference).

Answer: Area(ABD) = Area(AEC) = ⅓ Area(ABC); the equal-area triangles can also be related by a finite four-piece dissection.

Teacher review needed: Confirm the auxiliary overlay cut lines and piece labels against the cited Figures 9–10 before treating the dissection as fully checked. A continuous slide of infinitely many strips is not a finite cut-and-paste construction.

Question 21 · Two shaded regions in the quarter-circle figure

Let the square side be a. The quarter-circle has radius a, so its area is ¼πa². Each inward semicircle has radius a/2 and area ½π(a/2)² = πa²/8. Together, their areas add to πa²/4, exactly the quarter-circle's area.

Let S₁ and S₂ be the two semicircular regions. Their common part is A. By inclusion–exclusion, Area(S₁ ∪ S₂) = Area(S₁) + Area(S₂) − Area(A) = Area(quarter-circle) − Area(A). Because both semicircles lie inside the quarter-circle, the part of it outside their union is B = Area(quarter-circle) − Area(S₁ ∪ S₂) = Area(A).

Answer: Area(A) = Area(B).

Question 22 · Four-petal flower in a unit square

The square side is 2, so each semicircle radius is 1. The flower boundary consists of eight quarter-circle arcs: perimeter=8×(¼×2π×1)=4π units.

Each of the eight curved sections is a quarter-sector of area π/4 minus a right triangle of area 1/2. Total flower area=8(π/4−1/2)=2π−4=16/7 square units using π=22/7.

Answers: perimeter 4π units (88/7 using 22/7); area 2π−4 square units (16/7 using 22/7).

Question 23 · Annulus with a tangent chord

Let the outer and inner radii be R and r. The chord of length ℓ touches the inner circle, so the perpendicular from the centre bisects it; each half is ℓ/2. By Pythagoras, R²=r²+(ℓ/2)².

Annulus area=π(R²−r²)=πℓ²/4.

Answer: The green annulus area is πℓ²/4.

Question 24 · Semicircles on a right triangle

A semicircle with diameter d has area πd²/8. If the right triangle legs are a,b and hypotenuse c, then a²+b²=c². Multiplying by π/8 gives the sum of the two leg-semicircle areas equal to the hypotenuse-semicircle area.

Subtract the common pieces shown in the figure from both sides; the remaining lunes are A, B and C.

Answer: Area(A)+Area(B)=Area(C).

Question 25 · Lens formed by equal intersecting circles

The centre-to-centre distance equals the radius r. Joining the two centres to either intersection forms an equilateral triangle, so each circle contributes a 120° sector to the overlap. One circular segment has area πr²/3−√3r²/4.

The lens is two such segments: Area=2(πr²/3−√3r²/4)=r²(2π/3−√3/2).

Answer: r²(2π/3−√3/2).

Question 26 · Rectangle from three triangle areas

Let the perpendicular legs of the triangle marked C be p and q, so pq=2C. Let x and y be the remaining rectangle dimensions. The A and B triangles give px=2A and qy=2B. Thus xy=(px·qy)/(pq)=2AB/C.

Rectangle area=(x+q)(y+p)=xy+xp+yq+pq=2AB/C+2A+2B+2C.

Answer: Rectangle area = 2(A+C)(B+C)/C.

Question 27 · Equal shaded regions from a quarter-circle and semicircle

Let OA=OB=r, so triangle AOB is right-angled and AB=√(r²+r²)=r√2. The semicircle on AB therefore has radius r/√2 and area ½π(r/√2)²=πr²/4, equal to the quarter-circle area of radius r.

If x is the common unshaded portion removed from these equal areas, the two remaining shaded regions each have area πr²/4−x.

Answer: The two shaded regions have equal area.
