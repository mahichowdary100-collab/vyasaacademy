Exercise Set 5.1 — Circumcircles

Question 1 · Circumcircle of an acute triangle

Concept: The perpendicular bisectors of a triangle’s sides meet at the circumcentre. For an acute triangle, that point lies inside the triangle.

  1. Draw (AB=5 cm).
  2. At (A), construct a (70°) ray. At (B), construct a (60°) ray on the same side of (AB); their intersection is (C).
  3. Then (angle C=180°-70°-60°=50°). All three angles are acute.
  4. Construct perpendicular bisectors of two sides, such as (AB) and (AC). Let them meet at (O).
  5. With centre (O) and radius (OA), draw the circle. Since (O) is equidistant from all three vertices, it passes through (A,B,C).

Answer: The circumcentre (O) is inside ( Δ ABC).

Question 2 · Circumcircle of an obtuse triangle

Concept: The circumcentre of an obtuse triangle lies outside the triangle.

  1. Draw (AB=5 cm).
  2. At (A), construct a (100°) angle with (AB), and mark (AC=4 cm) on its ray. Join (BC).
  3. Construct perpendicular bisectors of two sides. Their intersection (O) is the circumcentre.
  4. Draw the circle centred at (O) through (A); it also passes through (B) and (C).
  5. Because (angle A=100°) is obtuse, the perpendicular bisectors meet outside the triangle.

Answer: The circumcentre lies outside ( Δ ABC).

Question 3 · Measuring the circumradius

Concept: A circumcentre is equidistant from the triangle’s three vertices. The construction is specified by side lengths (6,7,7) cm.

  1. Draw (AB=6 cm). With centres (A) and (B) and radius (7 cm), draw arcs meeting at (C).
  2. Join (AC) and (BC). Construct perpendicular bisectors of two sides; their intersection is (O).
  3. Measure (OA,OB,OC) using the same ruler and construction scale. They should agree, since each is a radius of the circumcircle.
  4. For a numerical check, the triangle has sides (7,7,6). Its altitude to the 6 cm base is (√(7^2-3^2)=2√(10)). Its area is (6√(10)). The circumradius is (R=(abc)/(4K)=(7×7×6)/(4×6√(10))=(49)/(4√(10))≈ 3.87 cm).

Answer: (OA=OB=OC), approximately (3.87 cm); ruler measurements may differ slightly.

Question 4 · Least radius through two points

Concept: For a circle through (A) and (B), the centre must be equidistant from (A) and (B), so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of (AB).

  1. Let (M) be the midpoint of (AB), so (AM=BM=(AB)/(2)).
  2. For any possible centre (O), triangle (OMA) is right-angled at (M) (unless (O=M) in the diameter case).
  3. By the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem, (OA^2=OM^2+AM^2 ≥ AM^2), hence (OA ≥ AM).
  4. Equality occurs when (OM=0), that is, the centre is (M), and (AB) is a diameter.

Answer: The least possible radius is ((AB)/(2)), attained by the circle with (AB) as diameter.

Think, Draw and Infer

Question 1 · Three collinear points

Concept: A circle can intersect a line in at most two distinct points; a perpendicular bisector is the locus equidistant from two points.

  1. Put the three distinct collinear points in order (A,B,C) on one line. The perpendicular bisectors of (AB) and (BC) are both perpendicular to that same line, so they are parallel (and distinct).
  2. If (P) had (PA=PB=PC), then (P) would lie on both perpendicular bisectors. Distinct parallel lines do not meet, so no such (P) exists.
  3. A circle cannot pass through all three distinct collinear points: the line through them would intersect the circle in three points, but a line meets a circle at no more than two points.
  4. For a sketch, mark (A,B,C) on a horizontal line, then draw the two parallel perpendicular bisectors at the midpoints of (AB) and (BC).

Answer: No such (P) exists; the perpendicular bisectors are parallel; no circle passes through three distinct collinear points; a line cannot cut a circle at three distinct points.

Question 2 · Congruent triangles on one circumcircle

Concept: A rotation about the circumcentre preserves the circle and all distances.

Rotate triangle ABC about its circumcentre O through any angle that is not a symmetry angle of the triangle. Each vertex remains on the same circumcircle, and rotation preserves all side lengths and angles. There are infinitely many choices of such angles, so this creates other congruent triangles sharing the same circumcircle.

Answer: Yes. Rotating the triangle about the centre gives infinitely many congruent placements on the same circumcircle.

Exercise Set 5.2 — Equal Chords and Central Angles

Question 1 · The triangle formed by a chord and the centre

Concept: Any two radii of one circle have equal lengths.

Let (AB) be a chord and (O) the centre. Join (OA) and (OB). Since (OA=OB=r), triangle (OAB) has two equal sides.

Answer: ( Δ OAB) is isosceles, with base (AB).

Exercise contentsNext question ↓

Question 2 · Equal bases in the isosceles triangles

Concept: SSS congruence.

Let chords (AB) and (DE) of the same circle be equal. Join their endpoints to centre (O). We have (OA=OD) and (OB=OE) (all are radii), and (AB=DE) (given). Thus ( Δ OABcong Δ ODE) by SSS.

Answer: The two isosceles triangles are congruent by SSS.

Exercise Set 5.3 — Perpendiculars to Chords

Question 1 · Converse of the midpoint theorem

Concept: RHS congruence.

Let O be the centre and OM ⟂ AB at M. In right triangles OMA and OMB, OA = OB (radii), OM is common, and both have a right angle at M. By RHS, the triangles are congruent, so AM = BM.

Answer: The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.

Exercise contentsNext question ↓

Question 2 · Altitude in an isosceles inscribed triangle

Concept: The perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the circle’s centre.

Given (AB=AC), equal chords (AB) and (AC) subtend equal central angles. Equivalently, the equal radii (OB=OC) make (O) lie on the perpendicular bisector of (BC). In isosceles triangle (ABC), the altitude from (A) to base (BC) is also its perpendicular bisector. Therefore that altitude and the perpendicular bisector of (BC) are the same line, so it passes through (O).

Answer: The altitude from (A) to (BC) passes through the circumcentre.

Question 3 · Distance between opposite-side chords

Concept: The perpendicular from the centre bisects each chord; use the right triangles it creates.

  1. The 6 cm chord has half-length 3 cm, so its distance from the centre is (√(5^2-3^2)=4) cm.
  2. The 8 cm chord has half-length 4 cm, so its distance from the centre is (√(5^2-4^2)=3) cm.
  3. The chords lie on opposite sides of the centre, so their separation is (4+3=7) cm.

Answer: The distance between the chords is (7 cm).

Exercise Set 5.4 — Equal Chords and Their Distances

Question 1 · Proving equal chords are equidistant

Concept: Apply Pythagoras to the right triangles formed by perpendiculars from the centre.

Let equal chords (AB) and (FG) have midpoints (E) and (H), respectively. By the centre-to-chord theorem, (CE⊥ AB), (CH⊥ FG), and each perpendicular bisects its chord. Thus (AE=FH). Also (CA=CF=r). In right triangles (CEA) and (CHF):

(CE^2=r^2-AE^2), while (CH^2=r^2-FH^2). Since (AE=FH), (CE^2=CH^2). Distances are nonnegative, so (CE=CH).

Answer: Equal chords of the same circle are equidistant from its centre.

Question 2 · Equal perpendicular distances

Concept: Chords at the same distance from the centre have the same half-length.

The searchable question text reads CH perpendicular to GH, while its referenced figure places H on chord GF and shows CH perpendicular to GF. Using the figure’s intended relationship: the perpendicular from the centre bisects each chord, so AE = EB and GH = HF. The radii CA and CF are equal, and CE = CH is given. Right triangles CEA and CHF are congruent by RHS. Hence AE = HF. Doubling gives AB = 2AE = 2HF = GF.

Answer (using the figure’s intended perpendicular): AB = GF.

Teacher review needed: Confirm whether the text’s “GH” is a printing error for “GF”.

Question 3 · Alternative proof using Pythagoras

Concept: Equal radii and equal perpendicular distances produce equal chord halves.

Let the radius be (r), and let the common distance of the chords from the centre be (d). If half-chord lengths are (x) and (y), then (x^2+d^2=r^2) and (y^2+d^2=r^2). Therefore (x^2=y^2). Since lengths are nonnegative, (x=y), so the full chord lengths (2x) and (2y) are equal.

Answer: (AB=GF).

Exercise Set 5.5 — Chord Lengths and Distance

Question 1 · Radius 7 cm, distance 6 cm

Concept: The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord.

  1. Let half the chord be (x). The radius, perpendicular distance, and half-chord form a right triangle.
  2. (x^2+6^2=7^2), so (x^2=49-36=13), hence (x=√(13)) cm.
  3. The full chord is (2x).

Answer: (2√13 cm ≈ 7.21 cm).

Exercise contentsNext question ↓

Question 2 · Deriving the chord formula

Concept: Pythagoras in the right triangle from the centre to the midpoint of the chord.

Let the radius be (r), the perpendicular distance to the chord be (d), and half the chord be (x). Then (x^2+d^2=r^2), so (x^2=r^2-d^2). Taking the nonnegative root gives (x=√(r^2-d^2)). The complete chord is (2x).

Answer: (chord length=2√(r^2-d^2)), for (0≤ d≤ r).

Question 3 · Comparing doubled distances

Concept: A chord’s length depends on its distance (d) from the centre through (2√(r^2-d^2)).

Let the distance of (AB) be (d), so the distance of (CD) is (2d). If both are chords of the same circle of radius (r), their lengths are (2√(r^2-d^2)) and (2√(r^2-4d^2)), respectively. These are not generally in the ratio (2:1). For example, with (r=5,d=1), the lengths are (4√6) and (2√(21)), not in a (2:1) ratio. A valid second chord also requires (2dle r).

Answer: No. Doubling the distance from the centre does not imply halving or doubling the chord length; use the formula, and require (2d≤ r).

Exercise Set 5.6 — Angles Subtended by Arcs

Question 1 · Chord with a 60° central angle

Concept: The radii to the chord endpoints form an isosceles triangle.

In ΔAOB, (OA=OB=12) cm and the included angle ∠AOB is (60°). The two base angles are equal and sum to (120°), so each is (60°). Therefore ΔAOB is equilateral.

Answer: (AB = 12 cm).

Question 2 · Points subtending equal angles

(i) No. If (X) and (Y) are on the same side of chord (AB) and on the circle, they lie on the same segment of that chord. Angles standing on the same chord in the same segment are equal, so ∠AXB=∠AYB.

(ii) No. Equal angles do not force (X) and (Y) to be on the same side. For example, when (AB) is a diameter, every point on the circle on either side of (AB) subtends a right angle; choose (X) and (Y) on opposite semicircles.

(iii) Not without a side condition. The converse concyclicity test applies when (X) and (Y) lie on the same side of (AB): equal angles then imply that (A,B,X,Y) lie on a circle. If they are on opposite sides, equality alone is insufficient; opposite-side angles can be equal in the diameter/right-angle case without forcing the four points to be concyclic.

Teacher review needed: Confirm the exact wording of part (iii) against the official print, which may refer to points not lying on the original circle, rather than points outside the circle through (A,B).

Answer: (i) No; (ii) No; (iii) yes if X and Y lie on the same side of AB, but the side condition matters.

Question 3 · Finding the marked angle

Concept: Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to (180°).

In Fig. 5.26, the quadrilateral is cyclic and the angle opposite the unknown (x) is (100°). Therefore (x+100°=180°), so (x=80°).

Answer: (x=80°).

End-of-Chapter Exercises

Question 1 · Chord 5 cm from centre; radius 13 cm

The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord. If half its length is (x), then (x^2+5^2=13^2), so (x^2=144), (x=12) cm. The full chord is (2x).

Answer: (24 cm).

Exercise contentsNext question ↓

Question 2 · Angle at the circle from a 70° arc

The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is twice the angle at a point on the remaining circle. Thus the required angle is (70°÷2=35°).

Answer: (35°).

Question 3 · Distance to a 24 cm chord in a 26 cm diameter circle

The radius is (13) cm and half the chord is (12) cm. If the distance is (d), then (d^2+12^2=13^2), so (d^2=25).

Answer: (5 cm).

Question 4 · Radius 15 cm; chord 9 cm from centre

Half the chord (x) satisfies (x^2+9^2=15^2). Hence (x^2=225-81=144), so (x=12) cm and the full chord is (24) cm.

Answer: (24 cm).

Question 5 · Perpendicular bisector of a chord

Let (AB) be a chord with midpoint (M), and (O) the centre. Since (OA=OB) (radii), (AM=BM) (midpoint), and (OM) is common, triangles (OMA) and (OMB) are congruent by SSS. Thus ∠OMA=∠OMB. These adjacent angles form a straight angle, so each is (90°). Hence (OM⊥ AB). The perpendicular bisector of (AB) is the line through (M) perpendicular to (AB), so it passes through (O).

Answer: The centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of every chord.

Question 6 · Angle subtended by a diameter

The arc subtended by diameter (AB) at centre (O) is a semicircle, so ∠AOB=180°. The angle at the circumference is half the central angle.

Answer: ∠ACB=(90°).

Question 7 · Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral

Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary. Thus ∠C=(180°-75°=105°), and ∠D=(180°-110°=70°).

Answer: ∠C=(105°), ∠D=(70°).

Question 8 · Algebraic opposite angles

((2x+10)+(3x-20)=180). Thus (5x-10=180), (5x=190), and (x=38). Therefore ∠P=(2(38)+10=86°) and ∠R=(3(38)-20=94°).

Answer: (x=38), ∠P=(86°), ∠R=(94°).

Question 9 · Radius from chord and distance

Half of the 16 cm chord is (8) cm. The radius is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs (8) cm and (6) cm: (r^2=8^2+6^2=100).

Answer: (r=10 cm).

Question 10 · Cyclic quadrilateral with sides 5, 5, 12, 12

For a cyclic quadrilateral with side lengths (a,b,c,d) and semiperimeter (s), Brahmagupta’s formula gives (K=√((s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d))). Here (s=(5+5+12+12)/2=17). Therefore (K=√((12)(12)(5)(5))=√(3600)=60).

Answer: (60 square units).

Teacher review: Brahmagupta’s formula may be beyond the chapter’s stated theorems. A classroom solution can instead derive the result by splitting the cyclic quadrilateral into triangles and using the cosine rule.

Question 11 · Locate the circumcentre without drawing the circle

Choose three non-collinear vertices of the cyclic quadrilateral. Construct the perpendicular bisectors of two sides of that triangle. Their intersection is the circumcentre because it is equidistant from the three vertices. The fourth vertex lies on the same circumcircle, so this is also the quadrilateral’s circumcentre. Check whether the intersection lies inside the quadrilateral’s boundary or outside it; no circle needs to be drawn.

Answer: Construct two perpendicular bisectors and inspect where their intersection lies relative to the quadrilateral.

Question 12 · Equal intersecting chords

Let equal chords (AB) and (CD) intersect at (P). By the intersecting chords theorem, (PA× PB=PC× PD). Also (PA+PB=AB=CD=PC+PD). Two positive numbers with the same sum and product form the same unordered pair, so ({PA,PB}={PC,PD}). Therefore the segments match in length; the exact endpoint correspondence depends on how the endpoints are named in the figure.

Answer: The two segment lengths on one chord equal the two segment lengths on the other (as an unordered pair).

Teacher review needed: The wording “corresponding line segments” may depend on the textbook’s labelled figure; use that figure to state whether (PA=PC, PB=PD) or (PA=PD, PB=PC).

Question 13 · Constructing a 6 cm chord 3 cm from centre

Use the perpendicular from the centre to bisect the chord. Its half-length is (3) cm and its distance from the centre is (3) cm.

  1. Draw a line segment (AB=6) cm and mark its midpoint (M), so (AM=3) cm.
  2. At (M), construct a perpendicular to (AB), and mark (O) on it with (OM=3) cm.
  3. With centre (O) and radius (OA=√(3^2+3^2)=3√2) cm, draw the circle through (A). Since (O) lies on the perpendicular bisector of (AB), it also passes through (B).

Answer: A valid construction has radius (3√2 cm).

Question 14 · Rectangle is the only cyclic parallelogram

In any parallelogram, opposite angles are equal. In any cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles sum to (180°). Therefore a cyclic parallelogram has a pair of equal opposite angles whose sum is (180°): each is (90°). All its angles are right angles, so it is a rectangle. Conversely, every rectangle is cyclic because its opposite angles are (90°+90°=180°).

Answer: A parallelogram is cyclic exactly when it is a rectangle.

Question 15 · Diagonals of an inscribed rectangle

Each angle of a rectangle is (90°), so each diagonal subtends a right angle at the opposite vertex. A chord that subtends a right angle is a diameter: its central angle is (180°). Thus both diagonals of the inscribed rectangle are diameters of the same circle. They meet at the circle’s centre.

Answer: The diagonals intersect at the centre of the circumcircle.

Question 16 · Locus of midpoints of fixed-length chords

Let the fixed chord length be (2a) in a circle of radius (r), with (0 < 2a ≤ 2r). The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it. If (M) is a chord midpoint, the right triangle gives (OM^2+a^2=r^2), so (OM=√(r^2-a^2)), constant for every such chord. Therefore all midpoints lie on a circle centred at (O) with that radius.

Answer: A concentric circle of radius (√(r^2-a^2)) (a single point if the chord is a diameter).

Question 17 · Centre on the angle bisector

Given chords (AB=AC), equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre: ∠AOB=∠COA. TΔs (AOB) and (AOC) have (OA) common, (OB=OC) (radii), and included angles equal; hence they are congruent by SAS. Therefore ∠BAO=∠OAC).

Answer: (AO) bisects ∠BAC, so the centre lies on its angle bisector.

Question 18 · Two parallel chords on the same side

The half-chord lengths are (5) cm and (12) cm. Let (d_1) be the distance of the 10 cm chord and (d_2) of the 24 cm chord from the centre. Since they are on the same side and 7 cm apart, (d_1-d_2=7). Also (r^2=d_1^2+5^2=d_2^2+12^2). Thus (d_1^2-d_2^2=119), so ((d_1-d_2)(d_1+d_2)=119). Hence (d_1+d_2=17), giving (d_1=12,d_2=5). Therefore (r^2=12^2+5^2=169).

Answer: (r=13 cm).

Question 19 · Regular hexagon in a circle

Joining the centre to consecutive vertices divides the full (360°) into six equal central angles of (60°). Each triangle formed by two radii and one side is isosceles with vertex angle (60°), hence equilateral. The side of the hexagon is (r). The perpendicular distance from the centre to a side is the altitude of an equilateral triangle of side (r), equal to (√(r^2-(r/2)^2)=√3r/2).

Answer: Side length (r); distance from centre to each side (√3r/2).

Question 20 · Angles in a cyclic quadrilateral with MN as a diameter

Angles MOP and MNP both stand on chord MP. The points O and N lie on the same segment of that chord, so the angles are equal. Also, since MN is a diameter, angle MPN = 90°.

Answer: Angle MOP = angle MNP; angle MPN = 90°.

Question 21 · Exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral

The searchable textbook text names angle CDE while also placing E on the extension of CD; those three points would be collinear. The intended exterior angle is likely angle ADE, formed by AD and extension DE. Under that interpretation, angle ADE = 180° − angle CDA. In cyclic ABCD, angle ABC + angle CDA = 180°, so angle ADE = angle ABC.

Answer (intended): The exterior angle at D equals the interior opposite angle at B.

Teacher review needed: Confirm the angle label in the official diagram/print before publication; the searchable text appears internally inconsistent.

Question 22 · No chord e1ceeds the diameter

For a chord at distance (d) from the centre in a circle of radius (r), its length is (2√(r^2-d^2) ≤ 2r), because (d^2 ≥ 0). The diameter has length (2r) and passes through the centre, where (d=0).

Answer: Every chord is at most as long as the diameter; equality holds only for a diameter.

Question 23 · Shortest chord through an interior point

Let the point (A) be at distance (d_0=OA<r) from the centre. For any chord through (A), let (p) be its perpendicular distance from (O). Its length is (2√(r^2-p^2)), which decreases as (p) increases. Among lines through (A), the largest possible distance from (O) occurs when the line is perpendicular to (OA): then (p=OA). (If (A=O), every diameter through (O) has the same length.)

Answer: The shortest chord through (A) is perpendicular to (OA).

Question 24 · Angle in a semicircle from the figure

Let the diameter endpoints be (A,B), centre (O), and point (C) on the semicircle. Since (OA=OC), triangle (AOC) is isosceles; call each base angle (a). Since (OB=OC), triangle (BOC) is isosceles; call each base angle (b). The straight angle at (O) gives (a+b=90°). At (C), ∠ACB=a+b).

Answer: ∠ACB=(90°).

Question 25 · Midpoints of two pairs of endpoints

Place diameter (AB) on a horizontal axis with centre (O=(0,0)) and radius (r). Since chords (CC') and (DD') are perpendicular to (AB), write their endpoints as (C=(u,v), C'=(u,-v)) and (D=(w,z), D'=(w,-z)). The circle equation gives (u^2+v^2=w^2+z^2=r^2).

Let (M) be the midpoint of chord (CD), and (M') the midpoint of (C'D'). Then (M=((u+w)/2,(v+z)/2)) and (M'=((u+w)/2,-(v+z)/2)). Their horizontal coordinates are equal, so (MM') is vertical while (AB) is horizontal.

Answer: (MM'⊥ AB).

Teacher review: The coordinate argument assumes the paired endpoints (C,D) are the upper endpoints and (C',D') the matching lower endpoints as indicated in the textbook diagram.

Question 26 · Opposite angles from the figure

Draw radii from O to the vertices. Each angle at the circumference is half the measure of its intercepted arc. The arcs intercepted by a pair of opposite angles together cover the full circle, whose measure is 360°. Therefore the opposite angles add to one-half of 360°.

Answer: The opposite angles add to 180°.

Teacher review needed: Match the labels p, q, u and v to the textbook diagram before describing each individual angle in terms of those labels.

Common mistakes and examination tips

  • Draw the perpendicular from the centre to a chord before using its half-length in a right triangle.
  • Use the central-angle theorem only with the same arc and a point on the remaining part of the circle.
  • State whether two chords lie on the same side or opposite sides of the centre before adding/subtracting their distances.
  • In construction questions, mark the perpendicular bisectors and the centre clearly; an approximate sketch is not a proof of equal radii.
  • For cyclic quadrilaterals, pair opposite angles (not adjacent ones) when using the (180°) result.
  • Keep exact radicals until the final step; round only if measurement is requested.
