Exploring Mixtures and their Separation

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • distinguish pure substances from mixtures, and homogeneous from heterogeneous mixtures;
  • compare solutions, suspensions and colloids using observable properties;
  • calculate mass-by-mass, mass-by-volume and volume-by-volume percentage concentrations;
  • choose a separation method based on physical properties of the components;
  • explain crystallisation, distillation, chromatography, sublimation, centrifugation and coagulation;
  • interpret solubility information and work safely with mixtures and laboratory equipment.
Core idea

A mixture contains two or more substances physically combined in variable proportions. Its components retain their identities and can often be separated using differences in physical properties.

1. Pure substances and mixtures

A pure substance has a fixed composition and characteristic properties. An element contains one kind of atom; a compound contains elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion. A mixture contains substances together without forming a single new substance by chemical combination. Mixture composition can vary.

A homogeneous mixture has uniform composition throughout at the scale considered (for example, salt solution). A heterogeneous mixture has visibly or measurably different regions (for example, sand in water). Some mixtures appear uniform to the eye but are colloids, whose dispersed particles are larger than those in a true solution.

In a solution, the dissolved substance is the solute and the dissolving medium is the solvent. A solution can be made from solids, liquids or gases. A concentrated solution contains relatively more solute than a dilute one, but the terms alone do not give an exact numerical concentration.

Common misconception

“Looks clear” does not by itself prove that a sample is a pure substance. A solution may be clear and still contain more than one substance. Likewise, a colloid may look uniform but scatter light.

3. Expressing concentration as a percentage

Percentage concentration compares the amount of solute with the amount of the final solution. Match the numerator and denominator units in the named form.

Formulas

Mass by mass % (m/m) = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 100

Mass by volume % (m/v) = (mass of solute in g ÷ volume of solution in mL) × 100

Volume by volume % (v/v) = (volume of liquid solute ÷ volume of solution) × 100

For mass-by-mass concentration, mass of solution = mass of solute + mass of solvent. For the volume-based forms, use the final solution volume given or measured; do not automatically add starting volumes because volumes may not be additive.

Worked example 1 · m/m

12 g of salt is dissolved to make 150 g of solution.

Mass percent = (12 ÷ 150) × 100 = 8% (m/m).

Check: the salt is part of the 150 g total solution; it is not compared only with the solvent mass.

Worked example 2 · m/v

A solution contains 5 g of glucose in 100 mL of final solution.

Mass-by-volume percent = (5 g ÷ 100 mL) × 100 = 5% (m/v).

Worked example 3 · v/v

20 mL of ethanol is present in 80 mL of final solution.

Volume-by-volume percent = (20 mL ÷ 80 mL) × 100 = 25% (v/v).

Check before calculating

Identify the solute; identify whether the question uses mass or volume; use the final solution amount in the denominator; preserve the form label (m/m, m/v or v/v) in the answer.

4. Solubility and saturated solutions

Solubility describes how much solute can dissolve in a stated amount of solvent under specified conditions, especially temperature. A saturated solution contains as much dissolved solute as it can hold under those conditions; extra solute remains undissolved. An unsaturated solution can dissolve more solute at that temperature.

For many solid solutes in water, solubility increases as temperature rises, but the amount of change differs by substance. Gas solubility in liquids often decreases as temperature rises. Always read the substance and conditions from the data or graph rather than assuming one universal trend.

When a hot saturated solution cools, the solubility of many solids decreases and crystals may form. This is the basis of crystallisation. Slow cooling can produce larger crystals; rapid cooling often gives smaller crystals. Evaporation and crystallisation must be done safely, with suitable lab supervision.

5. Choosing a separation method

First describe the mixture and the property that differs between its components. Then choose a method that uses that difference. A mixture with more than two components may need a sequence of steps.

MethodProperty usedSuitable example / result
FiltrationInsoluble solid particles are larger than filter poresSeparate sand from water
Evaporation / crystallisationSolvent evaporates; solute crystallises as solution becomes concentrated/coolsRecover dissolved solid; crystallisation can yield purer crystals
Simple distillationDifferent boiling/condensing behaviour; one component is collected as vapour then liquidRecover water from a solution of a non-volatile solute
Fractional distillationDifferent boiling points of miscible liquids, especially when relatively closeSeparate liquid components with a fractionating column
Paper chromatographyComponents move differently between stationary paper and moving solventSeparate dyes/pigments in ink
SublimationOne solid changes directly to vapour on heating and deposits on coolingSeparate a sublimable solid from a non-sublimable solid (e.g. camphor and sand)
CentrifugationParticles of different density respond differently to rapid spinningSeparate suspended solids from liquid; cream separation in milk processing
CoagulationFine colloidal particles clump into larger aggregatesHelp remove suspended/colloidal impurities during water treatment

Example: sand, salt and camphor

  1. Heat the mixture carefully under supervised lab conditions: camphor sublimes and can be collected after vapour cools.
  2. Add water to the remaining sand and salt. Salt dissolves; sand does not.
  3. Filter: sand stays as residue; salt solution passes through.
  4. Evaporate some water and cool the concentrated solution to crystallise salt, or evaporate to dryness when appropriate.

Each step uses a different physical property: sublimability, solubility and particle size, then solvent volatility/solubility change.

6. Separation methods in detail

Crystallisation

Crystallisation obtains solid crystals from a solution. A solution is concentrated, often by gentle heating, and then allowed to cool. As solubility falls, crystals form. The crystals can be separated and dried. Crystallisation is useful when a purer solid is required than simple evaporation may provide.

Distillation

In distillation, a liquid component is vaporised and then condensed. In simple distillation, a solution containing a dissolved non-volatile solid can yield the solvent as distillate. Fractional distillation uses a fractionating column to improve separation of miscible liquids with different boiling points. Do not heat a closed apparatus; use the setup and safety instructions provided by a teacher.

Paper chromatography

A small spot of sample is placed near the bottom of paper. The paper's lower edge is dipped in solvent, keeping the sample spot above the solvent level. As solvent rises, different components travel different distances because they differ in solubility in the solvent and attraction to the paper. A pure single dye may give one spot; a mixture can give several.

Sublimation

Some solids change directly from solid to vapour on heating and return to solid on a cooler surface. This can separate a sublimable substance from a non-sublimable solid. Examples often used in school demonstrations include camphor or ammonium chloride mixed with sand; follow teacher guidance because vapours may be harmful.

Centrifugation

Rapid spinning helps suspended particles separate from a liquid. Denser particles tend to move outward and collect as a pellet or sediment while the liquid remains above. In dairy processing, centrifugation can separate cream from milk. A paperfuge is a low-cost hand-spun device inspired by the same separation principle.

Coagulation

Very small dispersed particles in a colloid may remain suspended for a long time. Coagulation destabilises them so they clump into larger flocs that settle or can be filtered. Water treatment commonly combines coagulation with settling and filtration. Use chemicals only as directed by a trained adult.

7. Original practice question bank

These original revision questions are written for practice and are not copied NCERT exercise prompts. Answers are included for self-check.

Multiple-choice questions — 10

1. Which mixture is homogeneous?
  1. Sand and water
  2. Oil and water
  3. Salt dissolved completely in water
  4. Iron filings and sulphur

Answer: C. A completely dissolved salt solution has uniform composition at the scale considered.

2. Which mixture shows the Tyndall effect?
  1. Pure water
  2. Sugar solution
  3. Colloid
  4. Distilled water

Answer: C. Colloidal particles scatter a beam of light.

3. Which method is best suited to recover dissolved salt as crystals?
  1. Filtration alone
  2. Crystallisation
  3. Magnetic separation
  4. Sieving

Answer: B. Concentrating and cooling the solution can form salt crystals.

4. Which method separates dyes in ink based on their movement through paper?
  1. Chromatography
  2. Centrifugation
  3. Decantation
  4. Sublimation

Answer: A. Paper chromatography separates components with different attractions to paper and solvent.

5. In 10% (m/m) solution, the denominator is:
  1. Mass of solvent
  2. Mass of solute
  3. Mass of solution
  4. Volume of solution

Answer: C. Mass-by-mass percentage uses the total solution mass.

6. Which technique uses rapid spinning to help separate suspended particles?
  1. Distillation
  2. Centrifugation
  3. Crystallisation
  4. Chromatography

Answer: B. Centrifugation separates based partly on density under spinning.

7. What should the sample spot do in paper chromatography?
  1. Start below the solvent surface
  2. Start above the solvent surface
  3. Be placed at the very top
  4. Be dissolved in the paper

Answer: B. Keeping the spot above the solvent lets the rising solvent carry it up the paper.

8. What happens during coagulation?
  1. Colloidal particles clump into larger aggregates
  2. Solute becomes a gas only
  3. Liquid boils at a lower temperature
  4. All particles dissolve

Answer: A. Clumping makes the particles easier to settle or filter.

9. Which pair of methods can separate sand and salt from a mixture of the two?
  1. Dissolve in water, filter, then crystallise
  2. Chromatography then centrifugation
  3. Sublimation only
  4. Distillation only

Answer: A. Salt dissolves, sand does not; filtration removes sand, and crystallisation recovers salt.

10. A suspension left undisturbed will often:
  1. Remain permanently uniform
  2. Settle over time
  3. Become an element
  4. Show no particles

Answer: B. Suspension particles are relatively large and often settle.

Numerical practice

  1. Find the mass-by-mass percentage when 15 g of solute is present in 200 g of solution.

    (15 ÷ 200) × 100 = 7.5% (m/m).

  2. What is the mass-by-volume percentage of 8 g solute in 250 mL solution?

    (8 ÷ 250) × 100 = 3.2% (m/v).

  3. A mixture has 30 mL of a liquid component in 120 mL final solution. Find its volume-by-volume percentage.

    (30 ÷ 120) × 100 = 25% (v/v).

  4. A 12% (m/m) solution has a total mass of 250 g. Find the solute mass.

    Solute mass = (12/100) × 250 g = 30 g.

Short answer

  1. State two differences between a true solution and a suspension.

    A solution is homogeneous and does not settle on standing; a suspension is heterogeneous and often settles. Solution particles pass through ordinary filter paper, while suspension particles may be retained.

  2. Why is crystallisation often preferred to evaporating a solution completely?

    Crystallisation can form purer crystals as the solution cools, leaving some impurities in the remaining liquid. Complete evaporation may leave dissolved impurities mixed with the solid.

  3. How does simple distillation separate water from salt solution?

    Water is heated to form vapour. The vapour is cooled in a condenser and collected as liquid distillate, while non-volatile salt remains in the flask.

  4. Why can a colloid look uniform but still be heterogeneous?

    Its dispersed particles are small enough not to be seen individually but remain a separate dispersed phase. They scatter light and do not form a true molecular solution.

8. Practical work and safety

  • Wear eye protection when heating or handling solutions; tie back long hair and keep the bench clear.
  • Never taste laboratory materials; avoid direct inhalation of vapours and never heat a closed system.
  • Use a small, teacher-approved amount for crystallisation or sublimation demonstrations.
  • For chromatography, use the stated solvent and keep the baseline above its level.
  • For a centrifuge, balance opposing tubes and use only after instruction; never open while the rotor is moving.
  • Dispose of solutions and solids as instructed; do not pour unknown chemicals into a sink.
Plan a fair investigation

State the question, make a prediction, change one factor at a time, keep other conditions as similar as possible, record observations with units, and write a conclusion supported by the observations.

9. Quick revision and concept map

Two or more substances physically combined in variable proportions.

Scattering of light by dispersed particles, characteristic of colloids.

Mass of solute ÷ mass of solution × 100.

Paper chromatography separates components by differing movement with solvent and attraction to paper.

Rapid spinning and differences in particle density.

Identify the component property that differs, then select the method that uses it.

Concept map

Matter → pure substances or mixtures. Mixtures → homogeneous or heterogeneous → solutions, colloids and suspensions. Mixture properties guide separation: particle size → filtration; volatility/boiling point → distillation; solubility and temperature → crystallisation; sublimability → sublimation; density under spinning → centrifugation; movement/affinity → chromatography; colloid destabilisation → coagulation. Concentration expresses solute amount relative to final solution amount.
