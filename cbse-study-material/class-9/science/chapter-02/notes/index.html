Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • explain why microscopes are needed to observe most cells, and use magnification and size units carefully;
  • describe the cell as the structural and functional unit of living organisms;
  • compare plant and animal cells, and prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells;
  • connect the structure of the nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, endoplasmic reticulum, vacuoles, membrane and wall to their functions;
  • distinguish diffusion from osmosis and predict water movement across a selectively permeable membrane;
  • describe, at an introductory level, how mitosis and meiosis support growth, repair, reproduction and variation;
  • explain how loss of control over cell division can lead to cancer, without treating all growths as cancer;
  • plan and report a simple microscope or osmosis investigation safely.

What you should already know

You have already studied that living things need materials and energy, and that plants and animals are made of organs and tissues. A cell is a smaller level of organisation: many cells together can form a tissue, tissues form organs, and organs work together in organ systems.

Why this matters

Cells explain how a cut heals, how a plant leaf makes food, why a raisin swells in water, and why muscle cells need energy during exercise. Cell biology also helps scientists understand inherited conditions, infections and some diseases.

1. Cells: the working units of life

A cell is the smallest unit that can perform the basic activities of life. Some organisms, such as many bacteria, consist of one cell. Plants, animals and many fungi consist of many specialised cells that cooperate.

Cell theory — core idea

Living organisms are made of one or more cells; the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life; and new cells arise from pre-existing cells through cell division.

In 1665, Robert Hooke looked at thin cork under a microscope. He saw many small box-like compartments and used the word cell for them. The cork cells he observed were dead cell walls. Later, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek used improved lenses to observe living microscopic organisms. Better microscopes have since revealed details too small for the unaided eye.

Size, magnification and resolution

Cells are usually measured in micrometres (µm). One micrometre is one-millionth of a metre: (1,mathrm{µm}=10^{-6},mathrm{m}). A nanometre (nm) is smaller: (1,mathrm{µm}=1000,mathrm{nm}). Cell sizes vary widely, so a stated value should be treated as an example, not a rule for every cell.

Magnification tells how many times larger the image is than the actual object. Resolution is the ability to distinguish two close points as separate. Making an image larger does not necessarily make it clearer; useful detail depends on resolution and focus.

Reading a microscope scale

If an object is 20 µm long and its image is 8 mm long, first convert units: (8,mathrm{mm}=8000,mathrm{µm}). Magnification (=8000/20=400). The image is 400 times the object's actual length.

2. The cell membrane: a boundary that controls exchange

The plasma membrane (cell membrane) surrounds the cell contents. It separates the inside from the surroundings while allowing the cell to exchange selected materials. It is selectively permeable: some substances pass more readily than others. It is not an open sieve and does not let every substance pass freely.

The membrane is flexible. In the fluid-mosaic description, it is made mainly of a double layer of phospholipids with proteins embedded in or associated with it. The parts can move within the layer, while membrane proteins help with particular jobs such as transport or receiving signals. This is a model of the membrane's organisation, not a rigid wall.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region where they are more concentrated to a region where they are less concentrated, due to their random motion. Particles continue moving in both directions; at equilibrium, movement continues but there is no net change in concentration. For example, oxygen can diffuse from the blood into a body cell when its concentration is higher in the blood.

Osmosis

Osmosis is the net movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from a more dilute solution (higher water concentration) towards a more concentrated solution (lower water concentration). Water molecules move both ways, but the overall movement is towards the side with more dissolved solute until conditions balance.

Surrounding solutionNet water movementTypical cell response
More dilute than the cell contents (hypotonic)Water tends to enter the cellAn animal cell may swell; a plant cell becomes firm as the wall resists excessive expansion
Similar concentration (isotonic)No net movement; water still moves both waysCell volume stays approximately steady
More concentrated than the cell contents (hypertonic)Water tends to leave the cellAn animal cell may shrink; plant cell contents may pull away from the wall (plasmolysis)

In a potato osmosis activity, a potato cup containing concentrated sugar solution and placed in water may collect water. The membrane of living potato cells allows water to move, while the difference in concentration drives a net movement into the cup. A fair investigation includes a comparison setup and records the starting and final liquid levels.

Do not confuse

Diffusion describes the net movement of particles down a concentration difference. Osmosis is specifically the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Osmosis is not the movement of sugar through the membrane.

3. The cell wall: support outside the membrane

A plant cell has a strong cell wall outside its plasma membrane. It is mainly made of cellulose. The wall gives shape and support and helps protect the cell. It is generally porous to water and many small substances; selective exchange is mainly controlled by the membrane inside it.

When water enters a plant cell, the contents press against the wall and the cell becomes turgid. The wall limits further expansion and helps the plant remain firm. Animal cells do not have a cell wall, so their volume can change more freely when water moves in or out.

4. Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

Cells are grouped by how their genetic material is organised and whether they contain membrane-bound organelles.

FeatureProkaryotic cellEukaryotic cell
NucleusNo membrane-bound nucleus; DNA lies in a nucleoid regionDNA is enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus
Membrane-bound organellesAbsentPresent, such as mitochondria; plant cells also have chloroplasts
Typical organisationUsually smaller and structurally simplerUsually larger and internally compartmentalised
ExamplesBacteriaPlant, animal and fungal cells

Both kinds have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, genetic material and ribosomes. “Prokaryotic” does not mean “without DNA”; it means the DNA is not enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus.

5. The cell interior: parts working together

The cytoplasm is the material inside the membrane and outside the nucleus. It contains structures and is the setting for many cell reactions. Eukaryotic cells use organelles to organise particular jobs. No organelle works alone: materials move among cell parts as the cell grows and responds.

Cell partMain structure or locationJob to remember
NucleusMembrane-bound; contains most of the cell's DNAStores genetic instructions and helps coordinate cell activities
MitochondrionMembrane-bound organelleReleases usable energy from food during cellular respiration
ChloroplastFound in photosynthetic plant cells; contains chlorophyllCaptures light energy for photosynthesis
Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)Membrane network; rough ER has ribosomes, smooth ER does notHelps make and move proteins (rough ER) and lipids (smooth ER)
RibosomeTiny structures in cytoplasm or attached to rough ERBuilds proteins
Golgi apparatusStacked membrane sacsModifies, sorts and packages materials for use or transport
LysosomeMembrane-bound sac with digestive enzymesBreaks down some waste and worn-out cell parts

A useful cell-factory analogy

The nucleus is like an instruction store, ribosomes are protein-building sites, ER is a transport and production network, Golgi sorts and packs, and mitochondria supply usable energy. Analogies help you remember roles, but a cell is not literally a factory and these parts are not independent machines.

6. Plant and animal cells

Plant and animal cells share a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, ER, ribosomes and Golgi apparatus. A typical plant cell also has a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic cells, and a large central vacuole. Animal cells lack a cell wall and chloroplasts, and often have smaller vacuoles.

FeatureTypical plant cellTypical animal cell
Cell wallPresent outside the membraneAbsent
ChloroplastsPresent in many photosynthetic cells, such as leaf cellsAbsent
VacuoleOften one large central vacuoleUsually smaller temporary vacuoles, if present
ShapeOften more regular because of the wallOften more flexible in outline
Shared partsBoth have a membrane, cytoplasm, genetic material, ribosomes, mitochondria, ER and Golgi apparatus

These are typical patterns, not rules that every cell follows. For example, not every plant cell contains chloroplasts: cells in underground roots usually do not photosynthesise.

7. Cell growth, division and cancer

Cells do not simply grow larger without limit. Many cells grow, copy their genetic material and divide. Cell division supports growth, replacement and repair. In a multicellular organism, cell division must be coordinated with signals from the body.

Mitosis and meiosis: introductory comparison

FeatureMitosisMeiosis
Main roleGrowth, repair and replacement in many body cellsFormation of reproductive cells in organisms that reproduce sexually
Number of divisionsOne divisionTwo successive divisions
ProductsUsually two daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cellUsually four cells with half the parent chromosome number
Genetic resultDaughter cells are usually genetically very similar, barring changesProduces genetic variation through the processes of meiosis

This is a high-level comparison. Do not memorise detailed stage names unless your teacher or textbook specifically asks for them. Mitosis and meiosis are different processes with different outcomes.

How uncontrolled division can cause cancer

Normally, cells respond to signals that regulate when to divide, repair damage or die. If changes in genetic instructions disrupt these controls, a group of cells may divide excessively and form a growth. A benign tumour stays local; a malignant tumour can invade nearby tissue and may spread. Cancer is not one single disease, and risk and treatment depend on its type. It is not contagious. Only qualified health professionals can diagnose or advise on treatment.

Accuracy and care

Do not describe every lump or tumour as cancer. Do not blame a person for having cancer. This section explains a biological idea, not medical diagnosis.

8. Biomolecules, cells and current research

Cells contain biomolecules—molecules used to build cell structures and carry out life processes. At this level, remember their broad roles:

Biomolecule groupExamples of role in cells
CarbohydratesProvide energy and can contribute to cell structures
LipidsForm much of the cell membrane and store energy
ProteinsBuild structures and perform jobs such as transport and catalysis
Nucleic acidsDNA stores inherited instructions; RNA helps use those instructions

Research methods continue to improve. Better microscopes, cell cultures and imaging methods help scientists observe cell structures and study how cells behave. Researchers also investigate stem cells and organoids—small lab-grown models that can reproduce some features of tissues. These models are useful but do not reproduce an entire human body.

Mitochondria help cells release usable energy. Some inherited conditions, such as Leigh syndrome, can involve impaired mitochondrial function and affect tissues that need much energy. A case study links cell biology with health; it does not mean every mitochondrial change causes the same condition.

The CBSE syllabus highlights Indian cytologist Professor Arun Kumar Sharma and his work on chromosomes and methods for studying them. Studying chromosomes helps researchers observe how genetic material is organised and distributed when cells divide.

Structure and function

When explaining a cell part, connect what it is like structurally to what it does. For example: “The cell membrane is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances can enter or leave.” This is stronger than giving a name without its role.

9. Practical work: observe and investigate cells

Temporary slide of onion epidermis

  1. Use a clean slide and coverslip; follow the teacher's instructions and use a thin inner onion peel.
  2. Add a suitable stain if directed by the lab protocol, then lower the coverslip gently to reduce air bubbles.
  3. Begin with the low-power objective, focus carefully, then move to higher power only when the image is centred.
  4. Record what you can actually see, such as cell outlines, walls and stained nuclei. Do not label organelles that are not visible.

Osmosis investigation

Compare equal potato strips or potato cups in water and in a more concentrated sugar or salt solution. Keep time, size and temperature as similar as possible. Measure mass or length before and after. A result supports the idea that water can move across cell membranes; differences in cut size or solution concentration can affect the result.

Fair-test checklist

Change one condition at a time, keep other conditions similar, repeat measurements when possible, record units, and report unexpected results rather than hiding them.

Safety: follow your school's laboratory procedure. Use glass slides and stains only under teacher supervision; handle coverslips carefully, never taste laboratory materials, and wash hands after practical work.

10. Formula and microscope calculations

Magnification = image size ÷ actual size

Magnification has no unit. Image size and actual size must use the same unit before dividing. Rearranged forms are: image size = magnification × actual size; actual size = image size ÷ magnification.

Unit relationshipEquivalent value
1 mm1000 µm
1 µm1000 nm
1 cm10 mm
Worked example

Question: A cell is 30 µm long. Its drawing is 12 mm long. Find the magnification.

Given: actual size = 30 µm; image size = 12 mm = 12,000 µm.

Formula: magnification = image size ÷ actual size.

Substitution: 12,000 µm ÷ 30 µm = 400.

Answer: magnification = 400×.

Avoid: dividing 12 mm by 30 µm without converting to the same unit.

Basic problems

1. An object is 20 µm long and its image is 10 mm. Find magnification.

10 mm = 10,000 µm. (M=10,000/20=500). Answer: 500×. Keep units the same before division.

2. An image is 20 mm at 400×. Find actual size.

20 mm = 20,000 µm. Actual size = 20,000/400 = 50 µm.

3. Convert 1.2 mm into micrometres.

1.2 × 1000 = 1200 µm. Do not multiply by 100.

4. Convert 45 µm into millimetres.

45 ÷ 1000 = 0.045 mm.

5. A 3 mm image is made at 150×. Find actual size.

3 mm = 3000 µm. Actual size = 3000/150 = 20 µm.

Intermediate problems

6. A cell 80 µm long forms an image 24 mm long. Find magnification.

24 mm = 24,000 µm. (M=24,000/80=mathbf{300×}).

7. A 30 µm cell is viewed at 500×. Find image size in mm.

Image size = 500 × 30 µm = 15,000 µm = 15 mm.

8. An image is 4.2 mm at 350×. Estimate actual size in µm.

4.2 mm = 4200 µm. Actual size = 4200/350 = 12 µm.

9. Thirty cells fit across a 1.2 mm field of view. Estimate one cell's average width.

1.2 mm = 1200 µm. Average width = 1200/30 = 40 µm, assuming they fit edge-to-edge.

10. A microscope uses a 10× eyepiece and a 40× objective. What is total magnification?

Total magnification = 10 × 40 = 400×.

Challenging problems

11. A 25 µm cell appears 12.5 mm long. Find magnification.

12.5 mm = 12,500 µm. (M=12,500/25=mathbf{500×}).

12. An organism is 120 µm long. How long is its image at 250×, in mm?

Image size = 120 × 250 = 30,000 µm = 30 mm.

13. Six similar cells span 0.24 mm. Find the mean length of one cell in µm.

0.24 mm = 240 µm. Mean = 240/6 = 40 µm.

14. Convert 0.036 mm to nanometres.

0.036 mm = 36 µm. Since 1 µm = 1000 nm, 36 × 1000 = 36,000 nm.

15. An 80 µm object is viewed at 200×, then the image is enlarged by 1.5× in a print. Find printed image length and effective magnification.

Microscope image = 80 × 200 = 16,000 µm = 16 mm. Printed length = 16 × 1.5 = 24 mm. Effective magnification = 200 × 1.5 = 300×. State whether magnification refers to the original image or enlarged print.

Multiple-choice questions — 20

1. Which instrument is usually needed to observe most individual cells clearly?
  1. Unaided eye
  2. Microscope
  3. Telescope
  4. Thermometer

Answer: B — microscope. Many cells are too small to resolve with the unaided eye.

2. What does resolution describe in microscopy?
  1. Ability to separate two nearby points
  2. How many times larger an image is
  3. The colour of the image
  4. The width of the microscope stage

Answer: A. The ability to distinguish two nearby points as separate. It is different from magnification.

3. Which statement best defines a selectively permeable membrane?
  1. Every substance passes freely
  2. Only water can ever pass
  3. Some substances pass more readily than others
  4. The membrane is a rigid wall

Answer: C. It permits some substances to cross more readily than others, helping regulate exchange.

4. Diffusion is the net movement of particles from:
  1. Lower to higher concentration
  2. Higher to lower concentration
  3. Water only through a membrane
  4. Particles stop moving

Answer: B. Higher concentration to lower concentration, due to random particle motion.

5. Osmosis is specifically the net movement of which substance across a selectively permeable membrane?
  1. Water
  2. Dissolved sugar
  3. Protein
  4. Oxygen only

Answer: A — water. Osmosis does not mean movement of dissolved sugar itself.

6. A plant cell placed in a more dilute solution tends to:
  1. Lose water and shrink
  2. Gain water and become firm
  3. Stop all molecular movement
  4. Always burst

Answer: B. Gain water and become turgid; the wall resists excessive expansion.

7. Which structure lies outside the plasma membrane in a typical plant cell?
  1. Cell wall
  2. Chloroplast
  3. Nucleus
  4. Vacuole

Answer: A — cell wall. It provides support and helps maintain shape.

8. In a prokaryotic cell, DNA is generally found:
  1. Inside a membrane-bound nucleus
  2. In a nucleoid region
  3. Inside a chloroplast
  4. Inside a vacuole

Answer: B. In a nucleoid region, not inside a membrane-bound nucleus.

9. Which organelle is most directly involved in releasing usable energy from food?
  1. Mitochondrion
  2. Ribosome
  3. Chloroplast
  4. Golgi apparatus

Answer: A — mitochondrion. Cellular respiration releases energy that the cell can use.

10. Which structure captures light energy for photosynthesis in a typical leaf cell?
  1. Mitochondrion
  2. Chloroplast
  3. Nucleus
  4. Ribosome

Answer: B — chloroplast. Chlorophyll in chloroplasts absorbs light.

11. Ribosomes are the main sites where cells:
  1. Build proteins
  2. Build cellulose walls
  3. Store water
  4. Copy chromosomes

Answer: A. Build proteins.

12. Rough endoplasmic reticulum looks “rough” mainly because:
  1. Ribosomes are attached to it
  2. It has a cell wall
  3. It contains chlorophyll
  4. It is outside the cell

Answer: A. Ribosomes are attached to its surface.

13. What is a major role of the Golgi apparatus?
  1. Modifying, sorting and packaging materials
  2. Storing most DNA
  3. Capturing light energy
  4. Controlling the whole cell

Answer: A. Modifying, sorting and packaging materials for transport.

14. Which feature is typical of a mature plant cell but not a typical animal cell?
  1. A large central vacuole
  2. A membrane-bound nucleus
  3. Mitochondria
  4. Ribosomes

Answer: A. A large central vacuole; plant cells also have a wall, and photosynthetic cells have chloroplasts.

15. Which process usually produces two daughter cells for growth and repair?
  1. Mitosis
  2. Meiosis
  3. Diffusion
  4. Osmosis

Answer: A — mitosis. At this level, remember its role and general outcome.

16. Which division process is associated with producing reproductive cells with half the chromosome number?
  1. Mitosis
  2. Meiosis
  3. Budding
  4. Diffusion

Answer: B — meiosis. It also contributes to genetic variation.

17. Which statement about an isotonic solution is correct?
  1. Water stops crossing
  2. Water moves both ways with no net movement
  3. The cell always bursts
  4. Solute cannot move at all

Answer: B. Water moves both ways, but there is no net movement and cell volume stays approximately stable.

18. A 5 mm image represents an object 25 µm long. What is the magnification?
  1. 20×
  2. 200×
  3. 2000×
  4. 0.2×

Answer: B. Convert 5 mm to 5000 µm, then divide by actual size: 5000/25 = 200×.

19. Which cell structure contains most of the genetic instructions in a eukaryotic cell?
  1. Nucleus
  2. Cell wall
  3. Vacuole
  4. Ribosome

Answer: A — nucleus. It encloses most of the cell's DNA.

20. Why does a plant cell not usually burst when water enters it?
  1. The nucleus prevents water entry
  2. The cell wall resists expansion
  3. The chloroplast pumps water out
  4. The membrane becomes rigid

Answer: B. Its rigid cell wall resists expansion as the cell becomes turgid.

Assertion–Reason — 10

For each item choose: A. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion; B. both true but the reason does not explain the assertion; C. assertion true, reason false; D. assertion false, reason true.

1. Assertion: A cell membrane is selectively permeable. Reason: It regulates the passage of substances into and out of the cell.

Answer: A. Selective passage is the basis of that regulatory role.

2. Assertion: Osmosis is the movement of dissolved sugar across a membrane. Reason: Osmosis concerns the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane.

Answer: D. The assertion is false; the reason correctly describes osmosis.

3. Assertion: Plant cells have a cell wall outside the plasma membrane. Reason: Cellulose contributes to the support of many plant cell walls.

Answer: B. Both statements are true, but cellulose does not explain the wall's location outside the membrane.

4. Assertion: Prokaryotic cells contain DNA. Reason: Their DNA is not enclosed by a membrane-bound nucleus.

Answer: B. Both are true. The reason describes organisation, not why DNA is present.

5. Assertion: Mitochondria help release usable energy from food. Reason: Many mitochondria have folded inner membranes.

Answer: B. Both are true, but the fold detail alone does not explain the organelle's full role.

6. Assertion: Mitosis is important for growth and repair. Reason: Mitosis usually produces two daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cell.

Answer: A. This outcome allows cells to be added while retaining the chromosome number.

7. Assertion: In an isotonic solution, water molecules stop crossing the membrane. Reason: At equilibrium, there is no net water movement.

Answer: D. Molecules continue moving; only the net movement is zero.

8. Assertion: Chloroplasts are found in every plant cell. Reason: Underground root cells usually do not photosynthesise.

Answer: D. The assertion is false; many root cells lack chloroplasts. The reason is true.

9. Assertion: A larger microscope image is always clearer. Reason: Resolution affects whether close details can be distinguished.

Answer: D. Larger does not always mean clearer; resolution matters.

10. Assertion: Meiosis can contribute to variation. Reason: Meiosis is the same process as mitosis and produces identical body cells.

Answer: C. The assertion is true; the reason is false.

Short answer — 2 marks — 10

  1. Distinguish magnification from resolution.

    Magnification is how many times larger the image is than the object. Resolution is the ability to distinguish two nearby points as separate.

  2. Give two differences between diffusion and osmosis.

    Diffusion is the net movement of particles down a concentration gradient. Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane toward the more concentrated solution.

  3. State two functions of the cell wall.

    It supports and protects the cell, and helps it maintain its shape while resisting excessive expansion.

  4. State two differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.

    Prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have both. Prokaryotic DNA lies in a nucleoid; eukaryotic DNA is enclosed in a nucleus.

  5. Give the role of the nucleus and mitochondrion.

    The nucleus stores most genetic instructions and helps coordinate activity. Mitochondria release usable energy during cellular respiration.

  6. Why are plant cells more likely to become turgid than animal cells?

    Water entering a plant cell pushes its contents against the rigid cell wall, creating firmness. Animal cells lack a wall.

  7. What is the role of rough ER and smooth ER?

    Rough ER has ribosomes and helps make/transport proteins. Smooth ER lacks ribosomes and helps make lipids.

  8. Give two roles of the Golgi apparatus.

    It modifies and sorts materials, then packages them for transport within or out of the cell.

  9. How do mitosis and meiosis differ in their general purpose?

    Mitosis supports growth, repair and replacement. Meiosis forms reproductive cells and contributes to variation.

  10. Why is the phrase “all plant cells have chloroplasts” incorrect?

    Only photosynthetic plant cells typically have chloroplasts; many root cells do not.

Short answer — 3 marks — 10

  1. Describe how to observe a temporary onion epidermis slide.

    Peel a thin inner epidermis, place it on a clean slide, add stain if directed, lower a coverslip gently, then focus from low to higher power. Record only visible structures and follow lab safety.

  2. Explain what happens to an animal cell in hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic surroundings.

    Hypotonic: net water enters and it may swell. Isotonic: no net water movement, so volume is roughly stable. Hypertonic: net water leaves and it may shrink.

  3. Compare plant and animal cells using three features.

    Plant cells have a cellulose wall, may have chloroplasts in photosynthetic tissues, and often have a large central vacuole. Animal cells lack a wall and chloroplasts and usually have smaller vacuoles. Both share membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus and mitochondria.

  4. Trace how a protein for export can move through a cell.

    Ribosomes on rough ER build the protein; ER helps move it; Golgi modifies, sorts and packages it; a vesicle carries it to the cell membrane for release.

  5. Explain the difference between no net movement and no movement in osmosis.

    Water molecules continue crossing the membrane in both directions. At equilibrium the rates balance, so the total amount on each side does not change: net movement is zero, not molecular movement.

  6. Give three differences between mitosis and meiosis.

    Mitosis involves one division and usually produces two similar cells with the same chromosome number for growth/repair. Meiosis involves two divisions and usually produces four cells with half the chromosome number for reproduction, while contributing to variation.

  7. Describe a fair test of osmosis using potato tissue.

    Use equal potato pieces in different solutions, measure a starting mass or length, keep time and temperature similar, then remeasure. Include a water control, repeat if possible and compare changes.

  8. Why are mitochondria especially numerous in some muscle cells?

    Contracting muscle cells need substantial usable energy. Mitochondria support energy release from food, so cells with high energy demand often contain many.

  9. Explain why an increase in magnification may not reveal new detail.

    Magnification enlarges the image, but resolution determines whether nearby details appear separately. If the optical system cannot resolve them, a larger image can remain blurred.

  10. Explain how uncontrolled cell division is related to cancer.

    Changes in genetic instructions can disrupt normal controls on division and cell death. Some cells then multiply excessively; malignant cells can invade or spread. Not every growth is malignant, and diagnosis is medical.

Long answer — 5 marks — 5

  1. Describe the structure and coordinated roles of five major parts in a plant cell.

    Marking points: cell wall supports/protects; plasma membrane regulates exchange; nucleus stores DNA and coordinates activity; chloroplast captures light for photosynthesis in photosynthetic cells; mitochondrion releases usable energy; vacuole stores cell sap and helps maintain firmness. Explain at least five with correct roles.

  2. Compare diffusion and osmosis, then predict what happens to a plant cell in a concentrated salt solution.

    Diffusion is net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration. Osmosis is net water movement through a selectively permeable membrane toward the more concentrated solution. In salt solution water leaves the cell; the contents shrink and may pull away from the wall (plasmolysis).

  3. Explain how a eukaryotic cell can make, process and release a protein.

    DNA in the nucleus provides instructions. Ribosomes build a protein; rough ER helps produce and transport it; Golgi modifies, sorts and packages it; vesicles carry it to the membrane. Cell energy supports these processes.

  4. Compare mitosis and meiosis and explain why both are important.

    Mitosis: one division; usually two daughter cells with the same chromosome number; supports growth, repair and replacement. Meiosis: two divisions; usually four cells with half the chromosome number; forms reproductive cells and contributes to variation. Both involve accurate distribution of genetic material.

  5. Plan an investigation to test whether solution concentration affects potato tissue mass.

    Prepare equal potato pieces; measure initial masses; place them for the same time in labelled solutions of different concentrations, including water as a comparison; keep temperature, volume and potato source similar; blot consistently and measure final masses; calculate change; repeat; tabulate/graph results; conclude only from evidence and note limitations.

Case-based questions — 3 sets

Case 1: Potato strips. Three equal potato strips are placed in pure water, a mild salt solution and a concentrated salt solution. The water-soaked strip feels firm; the concentrated-salt strip becomes softer.

  1. Which process explains the observations?
  2. In which setup is net water entry most likely?
  3. Name one condition that should be kept the same.
Show model answers

1. Osmosis. 2. The pure-water setup, which is most dilute relative to the cell contents. 3. Time, temperature, strip size, solution volume or potato source. Water enters cells in dilute surroundings and leaves in concentrated surroundings.

Case 2: Microscope observation. A student measures a cell image as 6 mm. The microscope magnification is 300×. The slide is clear at low power but blurry after switching to a higher power.

  1. Find the actual cell size in µm.
  2. Which property is limiting the clarity?
  3. Give one sensible focusing step.
Show model answers

1. 6 mm = 6000 µm; actual size = 6000/300 = 20 µm. 2. Resolution or focus. 3. Centre the specimen at low power and adjust fine focus carefully at higher power.

Case 3: Energy demand. A muscle cell used repeatedly during exercise needs energy. A laboratory observation shows many mitochondria in the cell.

  1. What is the main role of mitochondria?
  2. Why is this feature useful in a muscle cell?
  3. Name another organelle and its distinct role.
Show model answers

1. They release usable energy during cellular respiration. 2. Muscle contraction has a high energy demand. 3. Example: nucleus stores genetic instructions; ribosomes build proteins; ER helps make/transport proteins or lipids.

Diagram-based questions — 5

  1. On a plant-cell sketch, label wall, membrane, nucleus, chloroplast and vacuole.

    Marking: wall outermost; membrane just inside wall; nucleus within cytoplasm; chloroplasts in cytoplasm; large central vacuole. The diagram should be titled and labels should use clear leader lines.

  2. On a prokaryotic-cell sketch, label membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and nucleoid DNA. What key structure is absent?

    Label the four parts; state that a membrane-bound nucleus is absent.

  3. Draw a membrane separating dilute and concentrated solutions. Add an arrow for net water movement.

    Show a selectively permeable membrane and arrow from the dilute side toward the concentrated side. Label water and solute clearly.

  4. Draw a simple animal cell and label membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus and mitochondrion.

    All four labels should point to the correct structures; do not add a cell wall or chloroplast as typical animal-cell parts.

  5. Sketch the route of a protein that will leave a cell using arrows and labels.

    Nucleus instructions → ribosome/rough ER → Golgi apparatus → transport vesicle → cell membrane. Label the direction of transport.

HOTS and competency-based — 5

  1. A student says, “The cell became larger, so the solute entered it.” Give another explanation consistent with osmosis.

    Water may have entered through the membrane from the more dilute surroundings. Cell size change alone does not show that solute crossed; additional evidence is needed.

  2. Two microscope images are equally large, but one shows two nearby dots separately and the other shows one blurred spot. Which microscope has better resolution?

    The microscope showing two distinct dots has better resolution. Equal magnification does not imply equal resolving power.

  3. A plant kept in darkness still has mitochondria but its leaf cells are not photosynthesising. Explain why these facts do not conflict.

    Chloroplasts capture light for photosynthesis, which needs light. Mitochondria support energy release from food; cells need energy in both light and darkness.

  4. A cell has a membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA, but no membrane-bound nucleus. Classify it and justify.

    It is prokaryotic because its DNA is not enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus. The listed shared parts do not make it eukaryotic.

  5. A tissue contains cells dividing rapidly. Why is “rapid division always means cancer” an incorrect conclusion?

    Rapid division can be normal during growth, repair or other regulated processes. Cancer involves loss of normal control and requires clinical evidence and diagnosis.

13. Common Mistakes Students Should Avoid

Mistake: mixing up magnification and resolution

Correction: magnification is image enlargement; resolution is clarity of nearby detail. A bigger blurred image is still blurred.

Mistake: saying osmosis moves solute

Correction: osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane. Diffusion can describe movement of different particles.

Mistake: claiming water stops in an isotonic solution

Correction: molecules continue moving both ways; the net movement is zero.

Mistake: forgetting unit conversion

Correction: convert mm to µm or µm to nm before using the magnification formula. Show the conversion step.

Mistake: saying prokaryotes have no DNA

Correction: they have DNA, but no membrane-bound nucleus.

Mistake: labelling every green-looking shape a chloroplast

Correction: label only structures shown or observed; chloroplasts occur in photosynthetic cells, not every plant cell.

Mistake: treating mitosis and meiosis as synonyms

Correction: compare their purpose, number of divisions and general products.

Mistake: drawing a biological diagram without a title or clear labels

Correction: use a neat outline, horizontal leader lines, a title and only relevant labels. Do not shade heavily or crowd labels.

Mistake: diagnosing cancer from a diagram or one symptom

Correction: describe the biological principle only. Diagnosis belongs to qualified health professionals.

14. Quick revision

Cell

Smallest structural and functional unit of living organisms.

Membrane

Flexible, selectively permeable boundary that regulates exchange.

Osmosis

Net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane toward the more concentrated solution.

Prokaryote

DNA is not enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus; membrane-bound organelles are absent.

Eukaryote

Has a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

Cell division

Mitosis supports growth and repair; meiosis forms reproductive cells and contributes to variation.

Important definitions

  • Cell: smallest unit able to carry out basic life activities.
  • Organelle: a specialised structure within a cell that performs a particular role.
  • Diffusion: net particle movement from higher to lower concentration.
  • Osmosis: net water movement through a selectively permeable membrane.
  • Prokaryotic cell: cell without a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • Eukaryotic cell: cell with a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles.
  • Turgid: firm plant cell after water enters and presses its contents against the wall.
  • Plasmolysis: shrinkage of a plant cell's contents away from its wall after water loss.
  • Magnification: ratio of image size to actual size.
  • Resolution: ability to distinguish two close points as separate.

One-minute recap

All cells share a membrane, cytoplasm, genetic material and ribosomes. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotic cells do not. Membranes regulate exchange. Diffusion moves particles down a concentration difference; osmosis is water movement across a selectively permeable membrane. Plant cells have a supporting wall, photosynthetic cells have chloroplasts, and a large vacuole is common. Mitosis supports growth and repair; meiosis forms reproductive cells and contributes to variation. Magnification calculations require matching units.

Checklist

  • I can explain why resolution and magnification are different.
  • I can compare diffusion with osmosis.
  • I can predict a cell's response to dilute and concentrated surroundings.
  • I can compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • I can connect cell structures to their functions.
  • I can compare plant and animal cells without saying all cells are identical.
  • I can state the broad roles of mitosis and meiosis.
  • I can solve magnification questions with unit conversions.

Concept check

Why does a root hair cell need a membrane?

It separates the cell from soil solution and regulates exchange of water and dissolved substances.

Which two features help identify a typical photosynthetic plant cell?

A cell wall and chloroplasts, among other parts shared with animal cells.

If a cell's image is blurred, should you only increase magnification?

No. Check focus and resolution; increasing magnification alone may not reveal detail.
