Learning objectives

Big picture

Earth is a connected system. Energy from the Sun moves through air, water and land, while matter such as water, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen cycles between non-living environments and living things. A change in one part can affect many others.

13.1 Uneven Heating of the Earth

The Sun supplies most of the energy that drives weather and many Earth processes. Solar radiation is electromagnetic energy that travels through space; its speed in vacuum is about (3\times10^8\ \text{m/s}). The average incoming solar power on a square metre held perpendicular to the rays at the top of Earthâ€™s atmosphere is called the solar constant, about (1.4\ \text{kW/m}^2). Some energy is absorbed, scattered or reflected by the atmosphere, so clear-sky insolation at the surface can be lower (the chapter uses about (1\ \text{kW/m}^2) as a maximum example).

Insolation is solar radiation received at Earthâ€™s surface. It is not uniform. Differences in latitude, Earthâ€™s spherical shape, axial tilt, time of day, surface properties and atmospheric conditions change how much energy a place receives and absorbs.

Albedo and surface heating

Albedo is the fraction of incoming sunlight a surface reflects. High-albedo surfaces reflect more and absorb less; low-albedo surfaces absorb more. The chapter gives snow an approximate albedo of 0.80â€“0.90, ice 0.50â€“0.70 and crushed rock 0.25â€“0.30. Dark soil and ocean water generally have lower reflectivity than fresh snow.

Earthâ€™s shape and tilt

Because Earth is approximately spherical, sunlight arrives at different angles at different latitudes. Near the equator, rays are more direct on average; toward the poles, they are more oblique and the same incoming energy is spread over a larger area. Earthâ€™s axial tilt changes the angle and duration of sunlight through the year, producing seasons. Latitude, time of year and local weather all matter.

Role of the atmosphere

Clouds, gases and dust can absorb, scatter or reflect incoming solar radiation. Earthâ€™s warmed surface emits energy mainly as infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane absorb some outgoing infrared energy and re-emit it, helping keep the lower atmosphere and surface warm enough for life. Weather happens mainly in the troposphere, the lowest atmospheric layer. The chapter describes it as extending roughly 0â€“12 km; temperature generally decreases with height there. The stratosphere above it contains the ozone layer, which absorbs much harmful ultraviolet radiation.

How Land, Water and Air Exchange Heat

Land and water do not warm and cool at the same rate. This difference creates local temperature and pressure differences that move air. Soil, vegetation, snow, rock and water also reflect and store heat differently. In cities, dark roofs, roads and dense buildings can absorb and re-radiate heat, contributing to an urban heat islandâ€”urban areas that are warmer than nearby rural surroundings, especially at night.

The atmosphere also redistributes heat and moisture. Evaporation transfers water from surfaces to the air; condensation forms clouds; precipitation returns water. Mountains can change airflow and rainfall, while surface heating can drive local winds.

13.2 Uneven Heating Causes Winds and Ocean Currents

Uneven heating creates differences in air temperature and density. Warm air tends to rise, while cooler, denser air moves in to replace it. These movements, together with pressure differences and Earthâ€™s rotation, contribute to winds. Wind transfers heat and water vapour around the planet. Winds also exert friction on the ocean surface and help set surface currents in motion.

Local Winds: Seaâ€“Land and Mountainâ€“Valley Breezes

Local winds result from nearby differences in heating.

  • Daytime sea breeze: land warms faster than the sea. Air above land warms and rises; cooler air near the surface moves from sea toward land.
  • Night-time land breeze: land cools faster than the sea. Relatively warmer air over water rises; cooler surface air moves from land toward sea.
  • Daytime valley breeze: sunlit mountain slopes warm the air beside them; air flows upslope from the valley.
  • Night-time mountain breeze: mountain slopes cool; denser air flows downslope into the valley.

Planetary Winds and Ocean Currents

Solar heating is stronger on average near the equator than near the poles. Combined with rising and sinking air, pressure differences and Earthâ€™s rotation, this creates broad circulation patterns. The chapter introduces pressure belts near the equator, around 30Â° and 60Â° latitude, and near the poles. Earthâ€™s rotation deflects moving airâ€”the Coriolis effectâ€”to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern Hemisphere. These broad patterns help explain prevailing planetary winds; real weather is more complex.

Large surface winds, temperature differences and salinity differences move ocean water. Earthâ€™s rotation and the positions of continents also shape these currents. Large circular current systems are called gyres; in the simplified global pattern, major gyres rotate clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Southern Hemisphere. Warm surface water can move poleward while colder, denser water returns at depth, redistributing heat.

13.3 Biogeochemical Cycles

A biogeochemical cycle is the movement of matter between living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts of Earth. Water, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen cycle between the atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere, cryosphere and biosphere. These cycles keep essential materials available and connect ecosystems.

13.3.1 Water Cycle

Sunlight drives evaporation from oceans, lakes and rivers. Plants also release water vapour through transpiration. Water vapour cools and condenses into clouds; precipitation returns it to land and water as rain, snow or hail. Some water runs over land into streams and oceans; some infiltrates soil and becomes groundwater. Water dissolves and transports minerals, supporting organisms and ecosystems.

A warmer atmosphere can hold more water vapour and may intensify heavy rainfall in some places while increasing drought risk in others. Glacier melt changes freshwater flows and contributes to sea-level rise. Heavy runoff can erode soil, while reduced infiltration can limit groundwater recharge.

13.3.2 Carbon Cycle

Carbon occurs in living things, atmospheric carbon dioxide, ocean water, rocks and fossil fuels. In the fast carbon cycle, plants use carbon dioxide in photosynthesis; carbon passes through food webs and returns to the air through respiration and decomposition. Oceans also exchange carbon dioxide with the atmosphere.

The slow carbon cycle stores carbon in sediments, carbonate rocks and fossil fuels over geological time. Burning coal, oil and gas transfers some long-stored carbon rapidly back to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide. Deforestation can reduce carbon uptake by plants and release stored carbon.

13.3.3 Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is needed to build proteins and nucleic acids. Although the atmosphere contains abundant nitrogen gas ((N_2)), most plants cannot use it directly. Nitrogen fixation converts nitrogen gas into usable compounds. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Rhizobium in legume root nodules and Azotobacter in soil contribute; lightning also forms nitrogen compounds. Industrial fertiliser production (the Haberâ€“Bosch process) fixes nitrogen using energy.

Plants absorb usable nitrogen compounds from soil; animals get nitrogen by eating plants or other animals. Decomposers return nitrogen from wastes and dead organisms to soil through ammonification. Nitrification converts ammonia to nitrite and then nitrate. Denitrification returns some nitrate as nitrogen gas to the atmosphere.

13.3.4 Oxygen Cycle

Oxygen is present as (O_2) in the atmosphere and combined in water, carbon dioxide, rocks and living molecules. Photosynthesis by plants and other photosynthetic organisms releases oxygen. Respiration and combustion use oxygen and release carbon dioxide and water. These processes move oxygen between the atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and biosphere.

13.4 Human Impact on Earthâ€™s Processes

Human activities can disturb cycles and connections between Earthâ€™s spheres:

  • Burning fossil fuels transfers long-stored carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, strengthening the greenhouse effect and contributing to warming.
  • Deforestation reduces photosynthesis and transpiration, alters habitats and can increase soil erosion and runoff.
  • Excess fertiliser use can add nitrates to lakes and rivers. Nutrient enrichment may trigger algal blooms; decomposition can reduce dissolved oxygen and harm aquatic life. This process is called eutrophication.
  • Climate change affects rainfall, glaciers, sea level, ecosystems and the water cycle.
  • Urbanisation changes surface reflectivity, water infiltration and heat storage, contributing to urban heat islands and altered runoff.

Reducing avoidable emissions, protecting and restoring forests and wetlands, using fertilisers carefully, conserving water and expanding renewable energy can reduce pressure on interconnected Earth systems. Solutions work best when they consider effects across several spheres and communities.

Worked Examples and Common Mistakes

Example 1: solar energy received

A (1\ \text{m}^2) panel receives an insolation of (800\ \text{W/m}^2) for (15\) minutes. Convert time to (900\) seconds, then:

(E=P\times t=800\times900=720{,}000\ \text{J}=720\ \text{kJ})

Example 2: reading albedo

A surface has albedo (0.7). It reflects about 70% of incoming sunlight and, in a simplified estimate, absorbs about 30%, ignoring other losses. A lower-albedo surface generally absorbs a larger fraction.

Example 3: cycle reasoning

If fertiliser runoff raises nitrate levels in a lake, algae may grow rapidly. When algae die, decomposers break them down and use dissolved oxygen; oxygen levels can fall and harm fish. This chain is eutrophication.

Common mistakes
  • Confusing the solar constant at the top of the atmosphere with insolation at Earthâ€™s surface.
  • Thinking albedo is the amount absorbed; it measures the fraction reflected.
  • Attributing seasons only to Earthâ€“Sun distance; Earthâ€™s axial tilt changes sunlight angle and day length.
  • Drawing local breezes in the wrong direction: near the surface, air flows toward the warmer rising air.
  • Confusing matter cycling with energy flow: matter cycles; energy enters mainly as sunlight and ultimately leaves as heat.
  • Assuming plants can directly use atmospheric nitrogen gas; most require fixed nitrogen compounds.
  • Thinking every greenhouse effect is harmful; the natural greenhouse effect warms Earth, while its enhancement by added gases drives extra warming.
  • Explaining eutrophication as algae directly â€œusing upâ€ all oxygen; decomposition of excess biomass is a major oxygen-consuming step.
