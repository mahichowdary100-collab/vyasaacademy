How Forces Affect Motion

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • describe a force as a push or pull and identify its magnitude and direction;
  • calculate the resultant force when forces act along one straight line;
  • explain how balanced and unbalanced forces affect motion;
  • connect inertia and Newton’s three laws to familiar situations;
  • use F = ma and W = mg with correct units;
  • explain the roles of normal force and friction in everyday motion.
Central idea

A force can change an object’s motion or shape. The effect depends on the resultant (net) force on that object—not on any one force considered alone.

1. Force and its measurement

A force is a push or pull arising from an interaction. It has a magnitude (how large it is) and a direction. A force may start or stop motion, change speed or direction, or deform an object. A spring balance measures force; its SI unit is the newton (N).

The pointer shows 5 N. For the stationary object shown, the upward tension balances its downward weight; a balance measures force, not mass.

Vector reminder

Forces have direction. When forces lie on one line, choose a positive direction, assign signs, and add them algebraically.

2. Balanced and unbalanced forces

The net force is the vector sum of all forces on one object. If opposite forces are equal, they balance and the net force is zero. A zero net force means zero acceleration: the object stays at rest or continues with constant velocity. If the net force is non-zero, the object accelerates in the direction of that net force.

Arrow lengths use the same scale within the unbalanced panel: the 10 N arrow is 2.5 times the 4 N arrow. Resultant: 10 − 4 = 6 N to the right.

Quick calculation

A cart is pushed east with 12 N and west with 7 N. Its net force is 12 − 7 = 5 N east. The forces do not cancel because their magnitudes differ.

3. Newton’s first law: inertia

An object remains at rest, or continues moving with constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a non-zero net external force. This tendency to resist a change in motion is called inertia. Mass measures an object’s inertia: for the same net force, a larger mass is harder to accelerate.

When the bus slows suddenly, the passenger’s body tends to keep its earlier motion. A seat belt provides a force that changes that motion.

Common mistake

Zero net force does not mean “no forces” and does not mean “the object must be stationary.” Several forces can balance while an object moves at constant velocity.

4. Newton’s second law: force and acceleration

For an object of constant mass, acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to mass. In the form used here:

Fnet = ma

Here Fnet is in newtons (N), mass m is in kilograms (kg), and acceleration a is in metres per second squared (m/s²). One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s²: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

With equal masses, tripling the net force triples the acceleration. Within this diagram, the 6 N force arrow is three times the length of the 2 N arrow.

Use the net force

Substitute the resultant force into F = ma, not one push if other forces also act. The acceleration points in the direction of the resultant.

5. Weight and the normal force

Weight is the gravitational force exerted by Earth on an object. Near Earth’s surface, W = mg, where g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (equivalently 9.8 N/kg). Mass is measured in kilograms and does not depend on location; weight is a force measured in newtons and can change if gravitational field strength changes.

A surface exerts a support force perpendicular to itself, called the normal force. For a book resting on a level table with no other vertical forces, the upward normal force equals the downward weight. This balance explains why the book has no vertical acceleration.

Free-body diagram: draw only forces acting on the chosen object. The arrows are equal here because the book is not accelerating vertically.

Do not confuse these pairs

The book’s weight and the table’s normal force act on the same book and balance in this situation. They are not a Newton’s third-law pair. The normal-force partner is the book pushing down on the table; that force acts on the table.

6. Friction: the force that helps us walk

Friction acts between surfaces in contact and opposes their relative sliding or tendency to slide. Its direction depends on that relative tendency. Friction can slow a moving object, but it can also make motion possible: when walking, a foot pushes the ground backward and the ground’s friction on the foot points forward.

At the same contact patch, the shoe pushes the ground backward and the ground’s static friction pushes the shoe forward. The forces act on different objects.

Think about the contact

Ask “which object is this friction acting on?” A shoe can grip the ground without sliding; the forward static friction on the shoe propels the walker forward.

7. Newton’s third law: interaction pairs

When object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction on object A. The two forces are a third-law pair. They act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other in one object’s free-body diagram.

Each skater accelerates according to the net force on that skater; equal forces need not produce equal accelerations if their masses differ.

Walking

Your foot pushes the ground backward; the ground pushes you forward.

Swimming

A swimmer pushes water backward; water pushes the swimmer forward.

Recoil

When an object ejects material backward, the material exerts an opposite force on the object.

8. Worked examples

Example 1: Find acceleration from a net force

A 4 kg trolley experiences a net force of 12 N east. Find its acceleration.

Working: a = Fnet/m = 12/4 = 3 m/s². Answer: 3 m/s² east.

Example 2: Find the weight of a 5 kg object

Use W = mg, with g = 9.8 m/s²: W = 5 × 9.8 = 49 N. Answer: the weight is 49 N downward. The mass remains 5 kg.

Example 3: Balanced forces and motion

A puck slides in a straight line at constant speed on an ideal frictionless surface. The net horizontal force is zero. It does not need a forward net force to keep moving; a net force is needed to change its velocity.

9. Original practice question bank

Try these original questions before opening the answers.

1. A 9 N force east and a 9 N force west act on a stationary box. What is the net force?
0 N; the forces balance.
2. A 3 kg object has a net force of 15 N north. Find its acceleration.
a = 15/3 = 5 m/s² north.
3. A book rests on a table. Name the upward force on the book.
The normal contact force exerted by the table.
4. Why does a passenger tend to move forward when a bus stops suddenly?
Because of inertia: the passenger’s body tends to continue its previous motion.
5. During walking, which direction is friction on the planted shoe?
Forward, if the shoe is pushing the ground backward without slipping.
6. Are a swimmer’s push on water and water’s push on the swimmer acting on the same object?
No. They act on different objects and form a third-law pair.

10. Quick revision and concept map

The newton (N).

Zero acceleration; the object may be at rest or move with constant velocity.

For constant mass, Fnet = ma.

Mass measures inertia in kg; weight is gravitational force in N.

On the other interacting object.

The ground’s forward static friction on the foot helps propel the walker forward.

Concept map

Interactions produce forces → add all forces as vectors to find the net force → net force determines acceleration. Zero net force gives constant velocity (Newton’s first law); Fnet = ma relates force, mass and acceleration (second law); interaction forces are equal, opposite and act on different objects (third law). Weight is gravitational force mg; contact surfaces can exert normal force and friction.

  • I can draw and label forces acting on one chosen object.
  • I can distinguish balanced forces from “no forces.”
  • I can calculate net force, acceleration and weight with units.
  • I can identify the two objects in a third-law pair.
