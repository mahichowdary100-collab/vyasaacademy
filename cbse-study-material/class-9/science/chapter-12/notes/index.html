Learning objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to define biodiversity and endemic species; explain why organisms are classified; compare the five kingdoms using cell type, cell structure, organisation and nutrition; outline the main groups of plants and animals; explain adaptations and ecological roles; arrange classification from kingdom to species; write a binomial scientific name correctly; and describe how fossils, DNA evidence and conservation help us understand life’s diversity.

Big picture

Classification organises life’s diversity so we can compare organisms, communicate clearly, investigate relationships and support conservation. Scientific groupings can change when new evidence becomes available.

12.1 India as a Biodiversity Hotspot

Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms found on Earth. It includes diversity among species, within species, and across habitats and ecosystems. India has varied landscapes and climates—from mountains and deserts to forests, plateaus, coasts and islands—which support many forms of life.

An endemic species is naturally found in a particular geographic region and nowhere else. Examples highlighted in the chapter include the Nilgiri tahr, lion-tailed macaque, Indian pitcher plant (Nepenthes khasiana) and Neelakurinji. A biodiversity hotspot is a region rich in endemic species that has also experienced major habitat loss. The chapter mentions the Western Ghats, Indo-Burma, the Himalayas and Sundaland (including the Nicobar Islands).

Biodiversity supports food webs, pollination, soil fertility, clean water, medicine, livelihoods and ecosystem stability. Conserving habitats helps protect the many interactions among species.

12.2 How Has Biodiversity Evolved?

The diversity of life has changed over very long periods. Individuals within a species vary. Some inherited differences can affect survival and reproduction in a particular environment. Across many generations, populations change and new forms of life may arise. Classification gives scientists a systematic way to describe the diversity that exists and examine relationships among organisms.

The chapter also connects scientific understanding with local knowledge. For example, the Sangam Tinai tradition grouped landscapes by ecological setting, and sacred groves have helped conserve local habitats.

12.3 How to Classify Organisms?

Scientists group organisms by shared features and evidence of relationships. The chosen criterion matters: the same organisms can be grouped differently when we ask different questions. Useful classification criteria include:

  1. External features: shape, size and body organisation.
  2. Mode of nutrition: autotrophic or heterotrophic.
  3. Internal structures: tissues, organs and skeleton patterns.
  4. Cell structure: prokaryotic or eukaryotic, cell wall, and one cell or many.
  5. Ecological role: producer, consumer or decomposer.
  6. Reproduction: sexual or asexual methods.
  7. Genetic similarity: inherited features and DNA evidence.
How to classify thoughtfully

First state the feature you are using, then compare organisms consistently. One visible feature alone may not show how organisms are related; cell structure and genetic evidence can add important information.

12.4 The Need for Classification

Classification makes the study of a huge number of organisms manageable. It helps scientists organise information, compare similarities and differences, identify organisms, discuss them using shared names, study ecological relationships and recognise species that may need conservation.

The chapter’s hornbill example from Pakke Tiger Reserve illustrates these uses: comparing several related species can help researchers track biodiversity and understand the importance of large, old nesting trees. Classification connects identification with knowledge about habitat and conservation.

12.5 Biological Classification Systems Over Time

Classification systems have changed as scientific knowledge and tools improved. Early schemes relied mainly on visible features and habitat. The two-kingdom system grouped organisms as Plantae and Animalia. Microscopes revealed diverse unicellular life, leading to additional groups. Later systems separated organisms without a membrane-bound nucleus from those with one, and treated fungi as distinct from plants.

System or contributorWhat changedWhy it mattered
Aristotle (ancient system)Grouped animals by habitat and visible features.Simple to observe, but did not reflect cell-level differences or deeper relationships.
Two kingdoms (Linnaeus, 1758)Plantae and Animalia.Microscopic organisms did not fit neatly into these groups.
Three kingdoms (Haeckel, 1866)Added Protista for many unicellular organisms.Recognised microscopic life as distinct.
Four kingdoms (Copeland, 1938)Separated Monera from organisms with a true nucleus.Recognised prokaryotic cell organisation.
Five kingdoms (Whittaker, 1969)Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.Considered cell type, organisation, nutrition and ecological role.

Later molecular evidence added the three-domain system: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. This does not make the five-kingdom model useless; the two systems answer classification questions at different levels and reflect knowledge from different periods.

12.6 Five Kingdom Classification

Whittaker’s five-kingdom system compares organisms using cell type, cell structure, level of organisation and mode of nutrition. These are broad school-level groupings; not every member has every typical feature.

Acellular entities: viruses

Viruses are not made of cells, so they do not fit into any of the five kingdoms. They contain genetic material inside a protein coat and can make more copies only inside a suitable living host cell. This is why the chapter and syllabus discuss them separately as acellular entities.

12.6.1 Monera — unicellular prokaryotes

Prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Monera in the five-kingdom model includes bacteria, Archaea and cyanobacteria. Bacteria occur in many environments; some cause disease, while many are useful in food production, nutrient cycling and other processes.

12.6.2 Protista — mostly unicellular eukaryotes

Protists have eukaryotic cells with a membrane-bound nucleus and are mostly single-celled. Examples include Amoeba, Paramecium, Euglena and Chlamydomonas. Their nutrition and movement vary.

12.6.3 Kingdom Fungi

Fungi are eukaryotes and are mostly multicellular; yeast is a familiar unicellular example. They do not photosynthesise. Many obtain nutrients by absorbing material from dead and decaying matter, so fungi play an important decomposer role. Some fungi are parasites. Their cell walls contain chitin, unlike the cellulose walls of plants.

Do not group fungi with plants

Both fungi and plants commonly have cell walls, but fungi are heterotrophic and absorb nutrients; plants are mostly autotrophic and photosynthesise. Their wall materials also differ.

12.6.4 Kingdom Plantae: Major Plant Groups

Plants are eukaryotic and multicellular, and most prepare food by photosynthesis. The chapter presents five broad school-level plant groups. They show increasing development of transport tissues, seeds and flowers, while each group has adaptations suited to its conditions.

The textbook groups monocot and dicot flowering plants under Angiosperms. Leaf venation, root patterns, seed leaves and flower-part numbers can help compare these groups; individual features should be considered together rather than used as an absolute rule.

12.6.5 Kingdom Animalia: Major Animal Groups

Animals are multicellular eukaryotes that obtain food from other organisms. A major classification clue in the chapter is the notochord, a flexible supporting rod present at least at some stage in chordates. Non-chordates do not have a notochord; chordates do. In vertebrates, the backbone is associated with the axial skeleton; a notochord and a vertebral column are related concepts but are not the same structure.

Classification clue

An earthworm and a beetle are both segmented, but the beetle has jointed legs and a hard exoskeleton, which are characteristic arthropod features. Use a combination of features to identify a group.

12.7 Adaptations and the Hierarchy of Classification

Structural features can help organisms survive in particular environments. Fins and gills suit many fish to aquatic life; feathers and light skeletons help birds fly; a camel’s fat storage and a polar bear’s thick fur are examples of features associated with particular conditions. Adaptations are not plans made by individuals: inherited variations become common in populations over generations when they support survival and reproduction.

12.7.1 Hierarchical classification

Classification proceeds from broad categories to more specific ones. As we move down the hierarchy, organisms in each group share more characteristics. The main levels used in the chapter are Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Plants often use “division” where animals commonly use “phylum.”

12.8 Scientific Naming: The Binomial System

Binomial nomenclature gives each species a two-part scientific name: the genus followed by the specific epithet. For example, the tiger is Panthera tigris and the mango is Mangifera indica. The genus starts with a capital letter; the specific epithet starts with a lower-case letter. Printed names are italicised; when handwritten, underline each word separately.

Writing a scientific name

Correct: Panthera tigris
Incorrect: panthera Tigris; Panthera Tigris; Panthera tigris without italic styling in print.

The genus name can be shared by related species; both words together identify the species.

New molecular evidence can also lead scientists to revise groupings. The three domains—Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya—are an example of classification informed by molecular studies.

12.9 Fossils as Evidence

Fossils are preserved remains, impressions or traces of organisms from the past, found in rocks and other materials. Fossils provide evidence that life has changed over geological time. Comparing fossils from different rock layers can help scientists investigate the history of life and relationships among past and present organisms. Fossil evidence is interpreted alongside other evidence; a single fossil does not tell the whole story.

The chapter highlights Indian palaeobotanist Birbal Sahni, whose work on fossil plants helped researchers understand ancient plants and past environments.

12.10 Biodiversity Under Threat

Species interact through food webs, pollination, seed dispersal and nutrient cycling. Habitat loss, pollution, overuse of natural resources and climate change can reduce populations and biodiversity. If one species declines, other organisms that depend on it may also be affected.

The chapter’s example of the Sangai (brow-antlered deer) and the floating phumdis of Loktak Lake in Manipur shows why protecting a habitat and its ecological relationships matters. Conservation can include protecting and restoring habitats, reducing pollution, using resources sustainably and supporting local communities’ knowledge and participation.

Think in connections

When proposing a conservation action, name the organism, the habitat feature it depends on, the threat, and how the action would reduce that threat.

Worked Examples and Common Mistakes

Example 1: writing a binomial name

For the tiger, genus = Panthera and species epithet = tigris. Write the full name as Panthera tigris: first word capitalised, second lower-case, both italicised in print.

Example 2: compare two kingdoms

A mushroom is eukaryotic, heterotrophic and has a chitin cell wall. These clues place it in Fungi. A green moss is eukaryotic, multicellular, mostly photosynthetic and has a cellulose wall, so it belongs to Plantae.

Example 3: use the hierarchy

In Panthera tigris, Panthera is the genus; tigris is the species epithet. The genus belongs to family Felidae, order Carnivora, class Mammalia, phylum Chordata and kingdom Animalia.

Common mistakes
  • Calling every species that lives in India endemic to India; endemic means naturally restricted to a particular region.
  • Thinking a biodiversity hotspot is simply any place with many species; endemism and habitat loss are central in this chapter’s definition.
  • Grouping fungi with plants because both have cell walls; their nutrition and wall materials differ.
  • Confusing prokaryotes with unicellular eukaryotes; both may be one-celled, but only eukaryotes have a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • Confusing a notochord with a vertebral column.
  • Assuming all plants have flowers or seeds; many groups do not.
  • Capitalising the second word of a scientific name or failing to italicise the printed binomial.
  • Thinking scientific classification never changes; new evidence can lead to revisions.

Original Practice Questions

These original revision questions are written for these notes and are not copied textbook exercise questions.

  1. Define biodiversity and an endemic species.
  2. Give two reasons why classification is useful.
  3. List four criteria used in five-kingdom classification.
  4. State one difference between Monera and Protista.
  5. Why are fungi placed in a kingdom separate from Plantae?
  6. Arrange Thallophyta, Bryophyta, Pteridophyta, Gymnosperms and Angiosperms by the sequence in which vascular tissues, seeds and flowers appear.
  7. What is the role of a notochord in classifying animals?
  8. Write the correct scientific name format for a tiger if its genus is Panthera and its species epithet is tigris.
  9. Give two ways fossils help us study past life.
  10. Name two threats to biodiversity and one conservation action.
Show answers
  1. Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms; an endemic species occurs naturally only in a particular region.
  2. Classification organises the study of organisms and helps compare, identify, communicate about and conserve them.
  3. Cell type, cell structure, level of organisation, and mode of nutrition (ecological role is also considered).
  4. Monera are prokaryotic; Protista are eukaryotic, mostly unicellular organisms.
  5. Fungi absorb nutrients and are heterotrophic, often decomposing dead material; plants are mostly photosynthetic. Fungal walls contain chitin; plant walls mainly cellulose.
  6. Thallophyta → Bryophyta → Pteridophyta (vascular tissues) → Gymnosperms (seeds) → Angiosperms (flowers and fruits).
  7. Chordates have a notochord at some stage; non-chordates lack it.
  8. Panthera tigris.
  9. Fossils preserve remains or traces and provide evidence for changes in life across geological time.
  10. Examples include habitat loss and pollution; actions include habitat protection/restoration and reducing pollution.

Quick Revision

Remember
  • Biodiversity is life’s variety; endemic species are naturally restricted to a region.
  • Classification organises organisms and helps compare features, relationships and ecological roles.
  • Five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
  • Fungi are heterotrophic absorbers with chitin cell walls; plants are mostly photosynthetic with cellulose walls.
  • Plant groups: Thallophyta, Bryophyta, Pteridophyta, Gymnosperms, Angiosperms.
  • Chordates have a notochord at some stage; vertebrates have a vertebral column.
  • Hierarchy: Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
  • Binomial: Genus capitalised + specific epithet lower-case; italicise in print.
  • Fossils are evidence of past life; habitat loss, pollution, overuse and climate change threaten biodiversity.
  • Three domains from molecular classification: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
