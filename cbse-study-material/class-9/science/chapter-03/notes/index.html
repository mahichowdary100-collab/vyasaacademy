Tissues in Action

Study how specialised cells work together in plant and animal tissues, and how tissues support growth, transport, protection and movement.

Learning objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  • explain how tissues help multicellular organisms divide work;
  • compare meristematic and permanent plant tissues;
  • describe the functions of simple and complex plant tissues;
  • identify the four major animal tissue groups and relate structure to function;
  • explain how bones, joints, cartilage, ligaments and muscles support movement;
  • describe habits that support musculoskeletal health.
Core idea

A tissue is a group of similar cells, usually with a common origin, that work together to perform a particular function. Tissues combine to form organs; organs work together in organ systems.

1. Organisation in multicellular organisms

In a multicellular organism, specialised cells share different tasks. This division of labour makes the whole organism more effective than a collection of independent cells.

For example, muscle tissue and connective tissue work together in the heart, an organ. The heart works with blood vessels as part of the circulatory system.

2. Plant growth and meristematic tissue

Plants continue to grow in particular regions because they retain groups of dividing cells called meristems. Meristematic cells are generally small, have thin walls, dense cytoplasm and a prominent nucleus, and have little or no large central vacuole. They divide to produce new cells.

MeristemTypical locationMain contribution
ApicalTips of roots and shootsIncrease in length (primary growth)
IntercalaryNear nodes or at bases of leaves/internodes in some plantsLengthening of these regions; helps regrowth after cutting or grazing
LateralAlong sides of stems and roots, such as cambiumIncrease in girth (secondary growth)
Growth investigation

To compare growth near a shoot tip, mark equal intervals on a young shoot, measure them at the start, then remeasure after a set time. Keep light, water and temperature as similar as possible. Record data before drawing a conclusion.

3. Permanent plant tissues

Cells produced by meristems may mature, become specialised and usually stop dividing. Such cells form permanent tissues. Their shape and wall structure suit their jobs.

Simple permanent tissues

TissueCell featuresFunction and examples
ParenchymaUsually living, thin-walled cells with spaces between themStorage and packing; chlorenchyma contains chloroplasts for photosynthesis; aerenchyma has air spaces that help aquatic plants float and exchange gases
CollenchymaLiving, elongated cells with uneven thickening at the cornersFlexible support in growing stems and leaf stalks
SclerenchymaUsually dead at maturity, with thick, lignified wallsStrength and rigidity, for example in seed coats and fibres

Complex permanent tissues

Complex tissues contain more than one kind of cell working together for transport.

FeatureXylemPhloem
Main material transportedWater and dissolved mineral ionsSugars and other organic food materials
General directionMostly from roots upward through the plantFrom sources (such as leaves) to places where sugars are used or stored; movement can be in different directions in different sieve tubes
Important cellsVessels and tracheids conduct; fibres support; parenchyma storesSieve tubes and companion cells conduct/manage transport; fibres and parenchyma support/store
Common mix-up

Xylem does not carry food made in leaves; phloem transports sugars. Avoid saying all xylem cells or all phloem cells are dead or living—each complex tissue includes different cell types.

4. The four major animal tissue groups

Epithelial tissue

Epithelial cells are closely packed and form coverings and linings. They protect surfaces, line organs and cavities, and may absorb or secrete substances. The shape and number of cell layers suit the site: flat cells allow a thin lining, while taller cells may be suited to absorption or secretion.

Connective tissue

Connective tissues support, bind, protect or transport. Their cells are often spread within an extracellular matrix. Examples include:

  • Areolar tissue: fills spaces and binds tissues;
  • adipose tissue: stores fat, cushions and insulates;
  • tendon: connects muscle to bone and is strong with limited stretch;
  • ligament: connects bone to bone and is comparatively elastic;
  • cartilage: provides flexible support and cushions joint surfaces;
  • bone: rigid support and protection; a living tissue with a mineral-rich matrix;
  • blood: fluid connective tissue that transports gases, nutrients, wastes and other substances.

Muscular tissue

Muscle typeTypical featuresWhere/function
Skeletal (striated)Long fibres, visible stripes; usually under voluntary controlAttached to bones; body movement and posture
Smooth (unstriated)Spindle-shaped cells, no visible stripes; involuntaryWalls of organs such as intestine and blood vessels
CardiacStriated, branched cells joined to work rhythmically; involuntaryHeart wall; repeated pumping

Nervous tissue

Nervous tissue contains neurons that receive and transmit signals, along with supporting cells. A neuron commonly has a cell body, branching dendrites that receive signals and a long axon that carries signals away. Nervous tissue helps coordinate rapid responses and communication in the body.

5. The musculoskeletal system and movement

The skeleton supports the body, protects organs and provides attachment points for muscles. A joint is where bones meet. Skeletal muscles usually work in pairs: when one contracts, its partner relaxes, producing movement in opposite directions. Muscles pull; they do not push bones.

Joint typeMovement/example
HingeMainly back-and-forth movement, as at the elbow
Ball-and-socketMovement in many directions, as at shoulder or hip
PivotRotation around an axis, as between the first two neck vertebrae
GlidingSmall sliding movements, as between some wrist bones

At a movable joint, cartilage covers bone ends and reduces friction; synovial fluid helps lubricate many such joints; ligaments connect bones and stabilise them. Tendons attach muscles to bones.

Example: bending the elbow

The biceps contracts and pulls the forearm upward while the triceps relaxes. To straighten the arm, the triceps contracts while the biceps relaxes. The bones move because muscles pull on them through tendons.

Caring for bones, joints and muscles

  • Use regular age-appropriate physical activity and strength-building movement.
  • Maintain a balanced diet with adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D; follow local health guidance for sunlight and nutrition.
  • Warm up before strenuous activity, use safe technique and suitable protective equipment.
  • Use good posture and take movement breaks during long periods of sitting.
  • Seek help from a responsible adult and qualified health professional for significant or persistent pain or suspected injury.

6. Compare and remember

CompareKey distinction
Meristematic vs permanent plant tissueMeristematic cells divide; permanent cells are differentiated for particular roles and generally do not keep dividing.
Parenchyma vs collenchyma vs sclerenchymaStorage/packing and often living; flexible support and living; rigid support with thick lignified walls and generally dead at maturity.
Xylem vs phloemWater/mineral transport vs food/sugar transport.
Tendon vs ligamentMuscle to bone vs bone to bone.
Cartilage vs boneFlexible cushioning/support vs rigid mineralised support.
Skeletal vs smooth vs cardiac muscleVoluntary movement at bones; involuntary organ movement; involuntary rhythmic heart contraction.
Exam tip

For any tissue, connect its structure to its function. State where it occurs, what its cells or matrix are like, and how those features help it do its job.

7. Original practice question bank

These are original revision questions, not copied NCERT exercise prompts. Answers are included for self-check.

Multiple-choice questions — 10

1. Which plant tissue is chiefly responsible for increase in length at root and shoot tips?
  1. Lateral meristem
  2. Apical meristem
  3. Sclerenchyma
  4. Phloem

Answer: B. Apical meristems at root and shoot tips support lengthwise growth.

2. Which tissue provides flexible support in young plant parts?
  1. Collenchyma
  2. Sclerenchyma
  3. Xylem vessel
  4. Phloem fibre

Answer: A. Unevenly thickened living collenchyma cells provide flexible support.

3. Which tissue mainly transports water and mineral ions from roots?
  1. Phloem
  2. Epidermis
  3. Xylem
  4. Parenchyma only

Answer: C. Xylem conducts water and dissolved minerals.

4. Which plant tissue transports sugars from leaves to areas that use or store them?
  1. Collenchyma
  2. Phloem
  3. Xylem
  4. Sclerenchyma

Answer: B. Phloem transports organic food materials such as sugars.

5. Which animal tissue connects muscle to bone?
  1. Ligament
  2. Tendon
  3. Cartilage
  4. Areolar tissue

Answer: B. Tendons attach muscles to bones.

6. Which muscle tissue forms the wall of the heart?
  1. Smooth
  2. Skeletal
  3. Cardiac
  4. Connective

Answer: C. Cardiac muscle contracts rhythmically in the heart.

7. Which tissue carries signals through the body?
  1. Nervous
  2. Adipose
  3. Epithelial
  4. Cartilage

Answer: A. Neurons in nervous tissue receive and transmit signals.

8. What type of joint allows movement in many directions at the shoulder?
  1. Pivot
  2. Hinge
  3. Ball-and-socket
  4. Fixed

Answer: C. The ball-and-socket joint permits movement in several directions.

9. Which cells are typically living and have uneven corner thickening?
  1. Collenchyma
  2. Sclerenchyma fibres
  3. Xylem vessels
  4. Dead cork cells

Answer: A. This is a characteristic feature of collenchyma.

10. What is the correct order of organisation?
  1. Organ → cell → tissue → organism
  2. Cell → tissue → organ → organ system
  3. Tissue → cell → organ system → organ
  4. Cell → organ → tissue → organ system

Answer: B. Cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs work in systems.

Short answer — 2 marks

  1. Give two features of meristematic cells.

    They actively divide and generally have dense cytoplasm with a prominent nucleus. Their small, thin-walled form supports growth.

  2. Distinguish a tendon from a ligament.

    A tendon joins muscle to bone; a ligament joins bone to bone and helps stabilise a joint.

  3. State one function each of parenchyma and sclerenchyma.

    Parenchyma can store food or water; sclerenchyma provides strength and rigidity.

  4. Why are xylem and phloem called complex tissues?

    Each contains different kinds of cells that work together for transport and related functions.

Answer in 3–4 points

  1. Compare the three simple permanent plant tissues.

    Parenchyma is usually living and supports storage/packing; collenchyma is living with uneven corner thickening for flexible support; sclerenchyma has thick lignified walls and provides rigid strength, usually being dead at maturity.

  2. Explain how an antagonistic muscle pair moves a limb.

    Muscles are attached to bones by tendons. A contracting muscle pulls a bone across a joint while its partner relaxes. To reverse the movement, the partner contracts and the first relaxes.

  3. How do cartilage, synovial fluid and ligaments help a movable joint?

    Cartilage cushions bone ends; synovial fluid lubricates the joint surfaces; ligaments connect bones and help hold the joint stable.

Application questions

  1. A plant shoot is cut above a node and later produces new growth near that node. Which meristem may explain this and why?

    An intercalary meristem may contribute because it occurs near nodes or leaf bases in some plants and can support regrowth after cutting. The exact response depends on the plant species.

  2. A student claims that a plant's food moves upward only. Correct the statement.

    Phloem carries sugars from sources to places that use or store them. The direction can vary in different sieve tubes; it is not accurately described as upward only.

  3. Why can an injury to a joint affect movement even if the bones remain intact?

    Movement depends on coordinated muscles, tendons, ligaments, cartilage and joint surfaces, as well as bones. Damage to any of these can cause pain, instability or restricted motion.

8. Quick revision and concept map

A group of similar cells working together for a function.

At apical meristems near root and shoot tips.

Xylem mainly carries water and minerals; phloem transports sugars and other organic food.

A tendon.

Epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous.

They contract and pull through tendons, usually working in opposing pairs.

Concept map

Cells specialise → tissues divide work → tissues form organs → organ systems coordinate → the organism functions. In plants, meristems form new cells and permanent tissues perform support, storage and transport. In animals, epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous tissues cover, connect, move and coordinate. Bones, joints and muscles together enable movement.
